# Plain English — what we actually know about Philippine schooling, and what we don't

*One page. Every number is checkable in `STYLIZED_FACTS.csv` (fact ids in brackets).*

## What we actually know

**The Philippines finished universalising primary school in 2017, and lower secondary in 2024.** Nine in ten children
finished primary in 2017 for the first time (91.9%), and 98.3% by 2024. Lower secondary crossed 90% in 2024 (92.4%).
[PHL-01, PHL-02]

**Upper secondary never got there.** It peaked at 76.4% in 2017 and was 75.0% in 2024 — never near 90, still 15 points
short. This is the most important thing we know, because the same government, in the same years, moved the two levels
below it. Whatever lifted primary and lower secondary did not lift upper secondary at all. [PHL-03, PHL-06]

**University enrolment went from 16 in 100 to 51 in 100 between 1971 and 2025.** It dipped sharply in 2018 (38% to 28%)
when the school system was reorganised, then recovered. [PHL-04]

**"Universal primary in 2017" is about finishing, not about getting in.** On the "are children enrolled" measure the
Philippines was already above 95% in 1971 — forty-six years earlier — and fell below it only after 2017, when the
reorganisation changed who was counted. [PHL-05]

**It was not just the rich regions.** All 17 regions gained schooling, all 17 saw education inequality fall by at least
0.02, and the worst-off regions gained most. But the advantage for the poorest quarter was mild: about 10% more gain,
not a dramatic catch-up. [REG-01…05]

**Richer countries do educate more, but specifically.** Across 30 countries a 1% income rise goes with 0.2% more
secondary enrolment and 0.57% more tertiary; within a country over time, tertiary responds much more (0.85) and secondary
barely at all (0.09). Higher fertility goes with lower primary completion. [SCA-01…05]

**Four things are genuinely not there.** Remove the common trend and there is no detectable back-and-forth between
schooling and jobs (strongest partial correlation 0.21 against a 0.25 bar); bigger countries do not have more or less
enrolment; two measures of graduate absorption give opposite answers; and countries do not cross the same thresholds at
the same income levels. [NEG-01…05]

## What we thought we knew but don't

**"State capacity doesn't predict when a country crosses."** The quoted correlations (+0.15, +0.10, +0.41, +0.30) are in
no file. The file says **+0.87, +0.90, +0.10, +0.80** — and even that is driven by one country, Japan. Both versions are
unusable at five countries. [CAP-01]

**"Philippine capacity rose 35 points into the primary crossing."** It doesn't. Recomputed, it is +26 if the crossing is
dated 2019 and +14 if dated 2017 — and capacity in 2017 was *below* its 2005 level. What survives is simpler and still
striking: class sizes fell, spending rose from 2.3% to 4.0% of GDP, and the ceiling did not move. [CAP-02, CAP-03]

**"One crossing date per milestone."** Two timing files on disk disagree — 2017 versus 1971 for Philippine primary —
because one measures finishing and the other measures enrolling. Nobody has built the single agreed version. [TIM-04, TIM-05]

**"More education makes countries richer."** The file was labelled backwards. Read correctly, its strongest result is the
opposite: income growth predicts tertiary enrolment (F = 6.5), while education does not predict growth at all. Neither
direction is proven; the data cannot settle it. [PP-01…06]

## What a synthetic world would have to get right

It has to produce **the asymmetry, not three separate numbers**: a floor and a middle that both cross 90 within seven
years of each other, and a ceiling that refuses to move, with about 15 points left explicitly unexplained. It has to keep
"enrolled" and "finished" as different quantities. It has to show income mattering more across countries than within them
for upper secondary, and the reverse for universities. It has to show the visible schooling–jobs co-movement **and** the
absence of a lead-lag once the trend is removed. It has to make capacity a floor condition, never a ceiling driver. And it
must never quote the three numbers above that turned out not to exist.

A fake world that gets all that right would be the first thing in this programme that could have been wrong.
