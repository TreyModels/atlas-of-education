<h1 align="center">Abstract</h1>

Schooling expansion is usually modelled as one process with one set of causes. This paper argues that it is governed by two different things at two different heights of the educational pipeline. Using cross-national panel designs—pooled, within-country, reverse, exact Bayesian model averaging, takeoff hazards, and 1,200 simulated common-cause panels—it shows that electrification governs the floor of the pipeline. Electricity predicts lower-secondary completion (+16.05) and upper-secondary completion (+0.562, *SE* = 0.195; posterior inclusion probability 1.00), and it predicts less child labour (−0.417), while its association with tertiary enrolment is null (+0.19, *SE* = 0.45) and lies inside a simulated noise band of ±1.2. Paid employment is the mirror image: it predicts tertiary enrolment (+1.425, *SE* = 0.410), a magnitude no common-cause panel reached, but it does not predict lower-secondary completion. Government education spending leads no schooling outcome; tertiary expansion predicts subsequent expenditure. State capacity in takeoff hazards is null to negative. "State capacity" and "development" are not single levers: infrastructure builds the floor, and a wage labour market builds the apex.

*Keywords:* schooling expansion, electrification, wage employment, state capacity, Bayesian model averaging, human capital, structural transformation

<h1 align="center">Introduction</h1>

Few regularities in the social sciences are as robust, and as poorly explained at the level of mechanism, as the expansion of schooling. Across the twentieth century, with striking uniformity of direction if not pace, states extended primary instruction to entire cohorts, then lower-secondary, then upper-secondary, and then built tertiary systems that now enrol a majority of the relevant age cohort in much of the world. Schofer and Meyer (2005) documented the worldwide expansion of higher education and argued that it is better understood as the enactment of a global institutional model than as a set of national responses to national economic need. That account is powerful at the level of why expansion happens anywhere, and is nearly silent on where expansion stops. A world-society model anticipates expansion at every level at once and reads lags as diffusion lags, but countries stall at specific heights of the pipeline, and the height at which they stall is stable over decades.

A second family of accounts explains expansion by returns. Households invest in schooling because schooling pays; the canonical statement is Becker and Lewis (1973), for whom the quantity and the quality of children are chosen jointly under a single budget, and Galor and Weil (1996), for whom the transition to sustained growth is a transition in the demand for human capital. This literature is powerful at the level of why anyone invests, and it too is largely silent on height. If returns governed schooling, the level at which a country stalled would track the return to that level, and the same variables would predict all levels with the same sign. They do not.

This paper develops a third account, which does not reject the first two but specifies their scope. Expansion is governed by two different things at two different heights of the pipeline. At the bottom—primary completion, lower-secondary completion, and the decision to keep a child in school rather than in work—the binding constraint is the material organisation of the household and of the school: whether there is light to read by, water that does not have to be carried, a functioning building, and a domestic labour burden light enough that a child's hours can be released. This is an infrastructure constraint, and the most consequential piece of infrastructure is electricity. At the top—the transition into tertiary enrolment—the binding constraint is different in kind: the expected wage that awaits a graduate, and therefore the existence of a wage labour market large enough to make that expectation credible across the decade a degree requires. This is a labour-demand constraint, and the relevant variable is paid employment.

The claim is not merely that two variables matter but that each is *level-specific*: electricity moves completion at the floor and does nothing detectable at the apex; paid employment moves enrolment at the apex and does nothing detectable at the floor. Neither substitutes for the other, and neither is a proxy for the other. That asymmetry is the paper's central empirical object, and it runs against the way "state capacity" and "development" are usually invoked. Both terms are used as single levers, as though a country either has more or less of them and therefore more or less schooling at every level. The results say there is no such lever: there are two, they operate at different heights, and a country can hold one without the other.

The evidence is a set of cross-national panel designs, each reported with its nulls intact. Electrification, measured within countries, predicts lower-secondary completion at +16.05 and upper-secondary completion at +0.562 (*SE* = 0.195; *P*(>0) = 0.99), with a posterior inclusion probability of 1.00 in exact Bayesian model averaging; its association with tertiary enrolment is +0.19 (*SE* = 0.45), a null inside a simulated noise band of ±1.2; and it predicts *less* child labour (−0.417; *P*(>0) = 0.02). Paid employment is the mirror image: +1.425 (*SE* = 0.410) for tertiary enrolment, a reverse estimate of +0.010 (0.042), zero of 1,200 common-cause panels at the observed magnitude, and no relationship with lower-secondary completion.

<h1 align="center">Two Regimes, One Pipeline</h1>

A school system is a pipeline of transitions, each a separate decision by a separate actor at a separate time: a cohort enters primary school, some proportion completes it, some proportion of those completes lower-secondary, some proportion of those completes upper-secondary, some proportion enters tertiary. Expansion is the process by which these conditional probabilities rise, and nothing in the structure requires the same cause at every node or a rise at one node to propagate upward.

**The Floor: Household Production and the Infrastructure Constraint**

At the bottom of the pipeline the decision-maker is a household allocating a child's hours across school, paid work, unpaid domestic and agricultural work, and rest. The decision is made repeatedly, at short horizons, under scarcities that are concrete and immediate. The relevant question is not what the child will earn at twenty-five but whether the child's hours can be spared today, and whether the hours will be well spent if they are. Both halves are governed by infrastructure.

The first half is the release of hours. Where household production requires hauling water, gathering fuel, milling grain, or tending animals, the child's labour is genuinely productive and its withdrawal genuinely costly. Electrification compresses the time these tasks require and changes the return to the alternative, since a household with light in the evening has an evening in which study is physically possible. The second half is the productivity of school time. A school without power has no evening section, no working equipment, no reliable water supply, and a teacher likely to be absent; the marginal hour of attendance buys less. Kravdal's (2002) demonstration that community context shapes demographic behaviour independently of individual characteristics supplies the general form of the argument: the relevant environment is a local public good, not an individual attribute. If the floor is governed by infrastructure, electrification should predict completion at the bottom of the pipeline, should predict the *substitution* of school for child labour, and should fade to nothing higher up, where the constraint is not the household's daily time budget.

**The Apex: Expected Returns and the Wage Constraint**

At the top of the pipeline the decision is different in kind. Tertiary enrolment costs a decade and, in most systems, real money; it defers income across early adulthood; and it is undertaken against an expected return that must be credible at entry but is realised long after. Becker and Lewis (1973) state how such decisions are made jointly with household structure, and Galor and Weil (1996) state why the aggregate consequence is a transition rather than a level shift: the demand for human capital rises when the technology that employs human capital exists. Both point at the same variable. What makes a tertiary return credible is the existence of employers who pay wages for credentials.

Paid employment—the share of the labour force in wage and salaried work rather than self-employment, family labour, or subsistence agriculture—is the natural measure of that condition, and it is not a measure of income. An economy can be poor and waged, or rich and self-employed; what matters at the apex is whether the graduate's expected destination is a wage contract. Boppart et al. (2023) show why the transition out of traditional agriculture is an intensification process rather than a simple release of labour: movement into wage employment depends on the technical and organisational transformation of production, not on the arithmetic of surplus labour. The corollary for schooling is that the apex cannot be built by making households richer in the aggregate, and cannot be built by making schools better. It is built by the existence of an employer.

**Why Neither Substitutes for the Other**

The regimes are non-substitutable for a structural reason rather than an empirical accident. Infrastructure cannot buy a return: electrifying every village does not create a single employer of graduates, and the interval around the electricity coefficient at the tertiary margin contains zero. The wage economy cannot buy hours: a credible graduate wage does not shorten the walk to water, and the predictor that dominates the apex is null at the floor. They are not two entries in a horse race; they act at different nodes, on different actors, at different horizons, and each is uninformative about the other's node.

This bears directly on how state capacity should be understood. The dominant framing treats capacity as a scalar: a state that can build things can build schools, staff them, enforce attendance, and therefore produces more schooling at every level. That framing bundles two distinct capabilities—delivering infrastructure and presiding over a wage economy—and assumes they are one. The results below suggest they are separable. A state can electrify a countryside and preside over an economy with no employers of graduates, and the result is a massified floor and a stalled apex; a state can host a wage economy while failing to deliver infrastructure, and the result is the reverse. The point is consistent with Schofer and Meyer's (2005) institutional account, which explains why nearly every state wants a university and why enrolment ratios converge among countries at similar world-society positions, but not why the ratio stalls at 4 per cent in one place and runs to 40 per cent in another with similar commitments. The global model supplies the demand for legitimacy; infrastructure and the wage economy determine which part of the pipeline can actually move.

<h1 align="center">Data and Method</h1>

The strategy is triangulation rather than a single preferred specification. Because no design in this literature is exogenous in any strong sense, each relationship is reported against at least one design that could have destroyed it and did not. The pooled map holds nothing constant; the within-country specification asks whether a country's own movements in a predictor are followed by its own movements in an outcome; the reverse specification swaps predictor and outcome in time; exact Bayesian model averaging asks whether a relationship survives across the whole model space rather than in a chosen specification; and the common-cause panels ask how large an effect a purely spurious relationship would generate under the same design. The nulls are as informative as the positives.

**Outcomes and Measures**

Four schooling outcomes define the pipeline: primary completion, lower-secondary completion, upper-secondary completion, and tertiary enrolment. The tertiary outcome is enrolment rather than completion because completion data at that level are sparse and inconsistently defined across systems. Child labour participation serves as an independent behavioural outcome at the floor, measured from a different instrument than any school statistic.

Three substantive predictors organise the analysis. Electrification is the share of the population with access to electricity, entered so that its variation is identified within countries over time. Paid employment is the share of the labour force in wage and salaried employment. State capacity is a composite index of the administrative reach and effectiveness of the central state, entered in the takeoff hazard models where the literature has placed it. Two further variables recur: an urbanisation measure (the "cities" term in the driver battery and absorption tests) and net migration remittances.

***A note on units.*** Coefficients in Table 1 are on each outcome's native measurement scale, and the outcomes are not measured identically: completion outcomes are expressed as shares of the relevant cohort, while the tertiary outcome enters in the metric used by the enrolment models. Rows of Table 1 therefore should not be compared in units, and this paper does not do so; what is comparable across rows is sign, precision, and standing against the simulated noise band. Where a common scale is required, the standardised battery in Table 2 supplies it.

**Identifying Designs**

***Pooled and within-country specifications.*** Every relationship is estimated twice: on the pooled cross-section of country-years, and with country fixed effects, so that identification comes only from within-country variation over time. The within-country results carry the interpretive weight, because the pooled map is vulnerable to the objection that rich, capable, electrified countries have more schooling for reasons unrelated to electricity. The pooled map is reported to show that results are not an artifact of fixed-effects absorption, and because it is where the reverse timing for spending is visible.

***Reverse and lead specifications.*** Each headline relationship is re-estimated with the temporal order reversed, so that the schooling outcome at an earlier date predicts the purported cause at a later one. 

***Exact Bayesian model averaging.*** Specification uncertainty is handled by averaging over the space of covariate combinations rather than by selecting a specification. Exact Bayesian model averaging enumerates the model space and returns, for each candidate variable, a posterior inclusion probability: the probability, given data and prior, that the variable belongs in the model. A variable can be significant in a hand-picked specification and still fail to be included once the space of alternatives is weighted.

***Common-cause simulation panels.*** In each simulated panel a latent common cause drives both predictor and outcome with no true causal effect between them, and the panel is subjected to the identical estimation procedure. The distribution of estimates defines a noise band: the range of magnitudes a purely spurious relationship routinely generates. For the tertiary electricity estimate the band is ±1.2, and the estimate of +0.19 lies inside it; for the paid-employment-to-tertiary estimate the statistic is a count, and zero of 1,200 panels reached the observed magnitude.

***Hazards, absorption tests, and the driver battery.*** Hazard models of the onset of sustained expansion address the extensive margin, with state capacity among the covariates under two specifications. Absorption tests estimate the apparent effect of tertiary expansion on upper-secondary completion and then add covariates in sequence, asking whether the edge between adjacent levels is a relationship between those levels or the reflection of a third variable acting on both. The standardised driver battery enters all candidate drivers together in a single model for tertiary enrolment, so that they can be ranked on a common scale.

***A note on inference.*** Estimates carry standard errors where the estimator supplies them and *P*(>0), the posterior probability that the coefficient is greater than zero, where the estimator is Bayesian. A coefficient reported as null is one whose interval spans zero and whose magnitude falls inside the simulated noise band; the paper says so explicitly rather than treating a null as an absence of information.

<h1 align="center">Results</h1>

Results are organised by height of the pipeline rather than by predictor, because height is the organising dimension. Each subsection reports the same two predictors at a different height, and the pattern to watch is the crossing.

**Electrification and the Floor**

Within countries, electrification predicts lower-secondary completion at +16.05, the largest schooling coefficient in the set. The same predictor at the next level up returns +0.562 (*SE* = 0.195; *P*(>0) = 0.99): smaller, less precise, decisively positive. The posterior inclusion probability for electricity in the exact Bayesian model averaging exercise is 1.00 for secondary attainment within countries—electricity belongs in essentially every model of secondary completion the data support, across the whole model space.

The tertiary row of Table 1 matters most for the argument. The electricity coefficient is +0.19 (*SE* = 0.45). The interval spans zero comfortably, the magnitude is a fraction of the effects at the two levels below, and the estimate sits inside a noise band of ±1.2 generated from panels in which a common cause drives both electricity and tertiary enrolment and no causal effect exists; a spurious relationship of this design routinely produces effects larger than +0.19. The inclusion probability for electricity at the tertiary margin is near zero. This is not a failed estimate but a well-powered null, and it is the empirical hinge of the paper: the same variable, measured the same way, in the same panel, with the same estimator, is decisive at the floor and invisible at the apex.

The independent-outcome replication strengthens the case. Electrification predicts lower child labour at −0.417 (*P*(>0) = 0.02). Child labour is not a schooling statistic; it is measured from household and labour-force instruments that share no reporting mechanism with enrolment series, and its relationship with electrification runs in the direction the floor mechanism predicts—hours released from production, and school made worth attending.

**The Wage Economy and the Apex**

Paid employment predicts tertiary enrolment at +1.425 (*SE* = 0.410). The estimate is large, precise, and—unlike the electricity coefficient at the same margin—not reproducible by a common cause: zero of 1,200 simulated panels, each constructed so that a latent variable drives both paid employment and tertiary enrolment with no causal effect between them, reached the observed magnitude. The reverse specification returns +0.010 (0.042), indistinguishable from zero and tightly bounded, which rules out the reading that graduate-rich countries simply have larger wage sectors because graduates staff them.

The mirror-image test completes the argument. Paid employment does not predict lower-secondary completion: the estimate is small, indistinguishable from zero, and nowhere near the magnitudes electrification produces at the same outcome. The variable that dominates the apex is uninformative at the floor, exactly as the variable that dominates the floor is uninformative at the apex—a pattern no single-lever account can generate.

**What the Standardised Driver Battery Shows at the Apex**

Table 2 reports the standardised battery for tertiary enrolment. Its two largest coefficients are not economic in the narrow sense: lower-secondary completion enters at +4.01 and upper-secondary completion at +3.79. This is the arithmetic of a pipeline—tertiary enrolment is bounded by the cohort that completed the levels below it—reported as a control on interpretation, not a discovery. Cities enter at +3.21 and paid employment at +3.20, effectively tied and both large; child mortality at −2.09; income at +0.97 and electricity at +0.19, both inside the simulated band.

One could conclude that the apex is "about" urbanisation rather than about wages, or that income matters after all at +0.97. Neither reading survives: +0.97 is inside the band, and the tie at +3.2 is a substantive point, since cities are where wage employment is and the collinearity between them (*r* = 0.74) is high enough that the battery cannot adjudicate between two variables which are, in most of the world, one process observed twice. What it does establish cleanly is the position of electricity: +0.19, inside the band, at the bottom of a ranking in which paid employment sits at +3.20. The variable decisive for lower-secondary completion is the weakest entry in the model for tertiary enrolment.

**State Capacity and the Takeoff Hazard**

The state-capacity result runs against the variable's reputation: In the hazard model of the onset of mass schooling the coefficient is −0.18 (*SE* = 0.20), and under an alternative specification with a different covariate set it is −0.31 (*SE* = 0.24). Both are negative and neither is distinguishable from zero: across two specifications, state capacity is null to negative for the transition into mass schooling, and nothing here supports treating capacity as the master variable that switches expansion on.

**Spending Follows Expansion**

Government education spending does not lead any schooling outcome in any design. What survives is the reverse relationship: tertiary expansion predicts government expenditure at +5.00 in the pooled map, one of the few places where the pooled specification is more informative, because the phenomenon is a cross-national pattern of budgets tracking enrolments with a lag. The reading removes a variable often treated as a policy lever from the set of candidate causes: expenditure is an output of the political economy of an expanding system, not an input that produces expansion.

<h1 align="center">The Absorption Problem</h1>

If the pipeline were a causal chain, expansion at one level would propagate to the next, and a paper about height would be a paper about one mechanism travelling upward. It is not a chain, because the variables governing adjacent levels are correlated—and that correlation is the strongest objection to the two-regime argument: if electricity, cities, and paid employment are manifestations of one process of development, the level-specificity documented above is an artifact of where each variable sits in the pipeline.

**The Tertiary–Upper-Secondary Edge**

The test uses the apparent effect of tertiary expansion on upper-secondary completion—the edge between the top two levels. Estimated alone, the edge is +0.106. Adding electricity leaves it essentially unchanged at +0.102: the bottom-of-pipeline variable does not absorb a top-of-pipeline relationship, which is what non-substitutability predicts. Adding cities does absorb it, dropping the edge to +0.035 and out of significance. With all three entered, the edge is +0.066—roughly three-fifths of its uncontrolled value, and not a finding one would build on.

**Collinearity Among the Three Regimes**

The correlations set a hard limit on the exercise: these are not three independent measurements of three independent processes but three views of a correlated bundle, and any claim to have separated them finely would be overreach. What the absorption results establish is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is what the argument needs—electricity fails to absorb a top-of-pipeline edge while cities succeed, because cities carry much of the paid-employment signal whereas electricity's unique variance at the apex is nil.

The honest summary is narrower than "two regimes, cleanly separated" and stronger than "everything is one thing": at the *extremes* of the pipeline the governing variables separate cleanly, in the *middle* the separation degrades, because the same urban-industrial complex that supplies the wage economy also supplies the schools, the roads, and the grid. The regimes are distinguishable where the pipeline is far from the bundle and indistinguishable where it is close to it.

<h1 align="center">Discussion</h1>

**Two Levers, Not One**

The contribution is not that electricity matters for schooling, nor that labour markets matter for higher education; both are old claims. It is that the two claims concern different parts of the same object, that each variable is null where the other is decisive, and that the pattern holds in the same panel under the same estimator.

Two terms that organise a great deal of policy writing have to be disaggregated accordingly. The first is state capacity: in the takeoff hazard the coefficient is null to negative, not because states do not matter but because capacity as usually measured—administrative reach, bureaucratic effectiveness, the ability to execute policy—is the capacity to *deliver*, and delivery is the wrong variable for explaining why a system expands at one height rather than another. A capable state that delivers infrastructure builds a floor; one that delivers nothing else builds only a floor. The variable governing the apex is not a property of the state at all but of the economy: whether there are employers, and whether they pay wages. The second is development: income enters the standardised battery at +0.97, inside the noise band, while paid employment enters at +3.20. Operationalised as income per head, development does not explain the apex; operationalised as the structure of employment, it does.

**Remittances: The Most Stable Finding**

The most stable single result in the set is negative, and it cuts against the intuition that migration money buys schooling. Net migration remittances predict *slower* growth in secondary completion, with a posterior inclusion probability of 0.58 in the pooled design and 0.75 within countries.

Two readings are consistent with the estimate, and these data do not separate them. The first is an opportunity-cost reading: remittances raise the return to migrating and lower the return to staying, so the household's marginal calculation shifts away from the fourteen-year-old in school and toward the eighteen-year-old abroad. The second is a liquidity reading in reverse: remittances relax the constraint that often binds school continuation, but they also remove the necessity of the credential, because household income no longer depends on local labour-market entry. Both treat remittances as a substitute for the wage economy rather than a component of it—and what builds an apex is employers who hire locally, not transfers arriving from abroad.

**Implications for Diffusion and Demand Accounts**

The result also clarifies a long-running disagreement in the neighbouring literature on fertility. Cleland and Wilson (1987) argued iconoclastically that demand theories—accounts in which fertility falls because economic circumstances change the desired number of children—could not explain the historical record, and that diffusion and ideational change do the work. Bongaarts and Watkins (1996) formalised the alternative through social interaction and showed that transitions spread through networks rather than tracking economic development at each locality. Schofer and Meyer (2005) carry the same logic into education.

The results suggest the dispute is height-specific in the same way the schooling dispute is. At the floor the diffusion account is plausible and probably necessary: constraints are local, the relevant knowledge is local, and Kravdal's (2002) finding that community context shapes behaviour net of individual characteristics is what a diffusion mechanism operating on a local public good would produce. At the apex diffusion is much weaker, because a wage contract cannot be diffused into existence by imitation. Countries can copy a university's charter, curriculum, and degree nomenclature—and they do, which is Schofer and Meyer's point—but they cannot copy the employer who hires the graduate. The institutional model travels; the labour market does not—which is the natural explanation for the pattern Schofer and Meyer observed.

The child-mortality result fits the same frame. Child mortality enters the tertiary battery at −2.09. Lloyd and Ivanov (1988) established the mechanism connecting improved child survival to family planning practice and fertility, and the quantity–quality trade-off formalised by Becker and Lewis (1973) links family size to per-child investment. High child mortality sustains high fertility, high fertility divides investable resources across more children, and the apex is where that division binds hardest, because tertiary study is the most expensive increment. Galor and Weil (1996) supply the aggregate version: the transition to sustained growth requires a demand for human capital that the technology of production generates—and on these results that demand is measured by wage employment.

**Policy Implications**

Three implications follow, each about sequencing rather than levels. The floor is an infrastructure problem: the largest coefficient in the paper belongs to a variable delivered by grid extension. The apex is a labour-demand problem and cannot be bought with school places or budget lines, because a country that expands tertiary enrolment without an employing economy should expect the enrolment to stall or to emigrate. And because the levers are separable in practice, a country may build either without the other, as the stalled-basics and late-massifier groups in Figure 4 show—but neither combination is a stages theory, and nothing here supports the view that the floor must be completed before the apex can be attempted.

<h1 align="center">Limitations</h1>

The designs are observational, and no claim of exogenous identification is made for any of them. The common-cause panels establish what a purely spurious relationship of this design would look like; they do not establish that the estimated relationships are causal. A variable correlated with electricity and omitted from every specification would produce the same results, and the simulation bounds the magnitude it would need, but bounding a rival explanation is not eliminating it.

Measurement cuts two ways. Electrification rates, paid-employment shares, and completion rates are compiled from national reporting systems whose definitions drift, and the drift is not random with respect to level of development. The child-labour replication was included because it uses a different instrument, but one independent outcome cannot validate the whole set. Collinearity is the most consequential limit: with pairwise correlations of 0.69, 0.80, and 0.74, the extremes of the pipeline separate cleanly and the middle does not, so any statement about which of the three is "really" doing the work there exceeds what these data support. Two further limits bound the reading. The tertiary outcome is enrolment rather than completion—appropriate to the theory but silent on whether the degree is finished or what it is worth. And the state-capacity composite is a coarse instrument, so a null for a composite is compatible with strong effects for particular components; the claim defended here is the narrow one that capacity, as standardly measured and invoked, does not lead the takeoff hazard.

<h1 align="center">Conclusion</h1>

Schooling expansion is not one process but two, operating at two heights of the same pipeline and governed by two different things. Infrastructure governs the floor: electrification predicts lower-secondary completion at +16.05 and upper-secondary completion at +0.562, with a posterior inclusion probability of 1.00 in exact Bayesian model averaging, and it predicts less child labour at −0.417, while its association with tertiary enrolment is +0.19 and lies inside a simulated noise band of ±1.2. The wage economy governs the apex: paid employment predicts tertiary enrolment at +1.425, a magnitude no common-cause panel out of 1,200 reached, while its reverse estimate is +0.010 and its association with lower-secondary completion is nothing.

Neither substitutes for the other, and neither is a proxy for a single quantity called development or state capacity. Income sits inside the noise band at the apex; state capacity is null to negative in the takeoff hazard; government spending follows expansion rather than producing it. What remains is a picture in which the two most consequential things a state can do for its school system are to put a grid under the household and an employer in front of the graduate—achievements that are independent, so that a country may have either without the other, and many do.
