## Abstract

One hundred and eleven countries have fallen below the replacement threshold of 2.1 births per woman and stayed there. This paper treats that group as a club and asks a deliberately modest question: what did its members have in common before they joined? Using a harmonised country-year panel, I compare ten socioeconomic indicators measured 20, 10, and 5 years before each crossing with the same indicators in countries that never crossed. The median crosser was richer, more urban, more electrified, better schooled, and had lower child mortality than the median never-crosser at every lag, and it was moving in each of those directions during the decade before it crossed. Yet no condition was universal. Ten years before crossing, 81% of crossers had child mortality below 50 per 1,000, 72% had income above $5,000, and 72% were more than half urban, while only 66% had lower-secondary completion above 70%, on a denominator of 29 countries. Half crossed below $10,000 per head and eight below $3,000. This is an accounting exercise, not a causal one.

*Keywords:* fertility transition, replacement level, below-replacement fertility, human capital, urbanisation, child mortality, descriptive demography

## Introduction

A total fertility rate of 2.1 births per woman is roughly the level at which a population replaces itself from one generation to the next. For most of the twentieth century it was mainly a line that rich countries had crossed and that others were expected to approach and then rest on. Instead, country after country went through it, kept going, and stayed on the other side. This paper counts those countries, dates their crossings, and asks what they had in common beforehand.

The count is 111: the number of countries that have recorded a total fertility rate below 2.1 and have not returned above it since. It is not a list of rich countries. It contains Norway and it contains Nepal. It contains Japan, which crossed during an economic miracle, and Georgia, which crossed during a collapse. Whatever else the club is, it is not a development league table with a fertility column attached.

The question is narrow, and I want to keep it narrow. It is not *why* fertility falls — that literature is enormous and largely unsettled, and nothing here is designed to settle it. It is: when countries crossed, what did their societies look like just before? Which conditions were near-universal, which were common, and which were merely typical of the richer members? And where the pattern breaks, as it conspicuously does in a handful of very poor countries, what do the exceptions look like?

That framing makes this a descriptive exercise from beginning to end: I report medians, shares, and changes in medians, not regressions with causal pretensions, and I do not claim that schooling or urbanisation produced the crossing. What the tabulation can do is establish the empirical company that crossers kept, and thereby discipline the more ambitious claims routinely made about the transition — including the claim that it is fundamentally an economic story, or a mortality story, or a story about women's education. Each of those claims predicts a different signature in the data. The signature in these pages is plural: many roads, some shared furniture.

## Why Replacement

Replacement is a deceptively simple idea. If a woman must bear, on average, one daughter who survives to reproduce, and if about 105 boys are born for every 100 girls, then a total fertility rate near 2.1 will replace a population in the long run at contemporary mortality levels. The threshold is neither a law of nature nor a constant, which argues for treating 2.1 as a marker rather than a mechanism.

The marker is nonetheless useful, for three reasons. First, it is the line at which the demographic conversation changes register: above it the questions concern growth and absorption, below it they concern ageing, dependency, and cohort arithmetic. Second, it is the terminus the transition literature itself has used — the classic accounts describe movement from high to low mortality and then from high to low fertility, with "low" in practice meaning at or below replacement (Cleland & Wilson, 1987; Reher, 2004). Third, a crossing date is a discrete, datable event, which makes the club a tractable object of description in a way that "low fertility" as a continuous condition is not.

It is worth saying what the club is not. It is not a set of countries that have completed a transition in any teleological sense. Several members have fertility well below one birth per woman; several have rebounded modestly; several will shrink substantially this century on current rates. Nor is it homogeneous in the texture of the fall. The literature on lowest-low fertility in Southern and Eastern Europe describes a rapid, politically inflected collapse in the 1990s that looks nothing like the slower secular decline recorded in Western Europe a generation earlier (Kohler et al., 2002). East Asian cases, where marriage and childbearing are tightly bundled and low fertility has persisted despite prosperity, look different again (Frejka et al., 2010; Raymo et al., 2015). And sub-Saharan cases, where the transition has been slow or stalled, are the standing counter-example to any claim that development reliably delivers a crossing (Bongaarts, 2008; Bongaarts & Casterline, 2013).

Two older strands frame how I read the results: the demand-and-diffusion debate over whether fertility falls because households want fewer children or because ideas and contraceptive information spread through social networks (Cleland & Wilson, 1987; Bongaarts & Watkins, 1996), and the human-capital literature that embeds the transition in a broader transformation of economy, family, and educational profile (Lutz et al., 2014). The indicators below are drawn from both — not to adjudicate between them but to see which of their implications describe the company that crossers kept.

## Data and Method

### The Panel

The analysis rests on a harmonised country-year panel of national populations from the mid-twentieth century to the most recent year available. Fertility is measured as the total fertility rate. Socioeconomic indicators are drawn from national statistical reporting, census tabulations, and household survey programmes, harmonised to common definitions so that an indicator means approximately the same thing across periods and regions. Definitions, source provenance, and harmonisation rules are documented in the accompanying codebook.

Ten indicators are used, each with a plausible link to the demand for children in the standard literature and each available for enough country-years to be tabulated without wholesale imputation: lower-secondary completion in the adult population; secondary completion; tertiary enrolment; the ratio of women to men in tertiary education; deaths before age five per 1,000 live births; income per head; the urban share of population; the share of households with electricity; the share of employment that is paid employment; and the female labour force participation rate.

Three require comment. The attainment measures are stock measures: they describe the schooling of the adults alive in a given year, not the enrolment of children that year. That makes them slow-moving and causally ambiguous, since the adults of 1990 were schooled in 1960, but it also makes them an honest description of the human capital actually present in a society when it crossed. Tertiary enrolment, a flow measure, behaves differently, and the gap between the two is informative. The women-to-men ratio is included because a raw enrolment rate cannot distinguish a society that educates its sons from one that educates its daughters. The tenth indicator is included because its misbehaviour is instructive.

### Defining the Crossing

A country joins the club in the first year in which its total fertility rate falls below 2.1 and does not subsequently return to or above 2.1 for the remainder of its series. The second condition matters: a single year below the threshold is often noise, whether from economic shock, postponed births, or reporting artefact, so a country that fell to 2.0 in 1998 and rose to 2.2 in 2001 is excluded. The rule is conservative in one direction and liberal in another — conservative because a genuine early member observed through a noisy series may be omitted, liberal because the confirmation window is shorter for later crossers, who need only a few subsequent years at the low level. Crossings are then assigned to a decade. Countries that crossed before 1980 are counted as members but not assigned to one of the five tabulated decades, because their antecedents lie in the 1960s and 1970s, where coverage thins; the five decades account for 78 of the 111 members.

### Lag Structure and Comparison Group

For each crossing, indicator values are extracted at 20, 10, and 5 years before the crossing year. These are conventions rather than design features. Twenty years is roughly one generation; ten years is the horizon over which most observable change occurs; five years is close enough to describe the society at entry, at the cost of some overlap between measurement window and outcome. The comparison group is the set of countries that never crossed — those whose series remained at or above 2.1 through the most recent year. It is heterogeneous and it is not a control group: it contains countries plausibly on the way to a crossing, countries that have stalled, and countries whose fertility has risen. I report its medians as a reference point and describe differences in medians as differences, not effects. Because the never-crossers are observed across a long window while crossers are observed at fixed lags, the contrast confounds group differences with period differences, which is the most important caveat on every number that follows.

### Composition, Denominators, and Missing Data

Countries are unweighted: India counts once, and so does Luxembourg. That suits a question about what countries did, but it means these tabulations say nothing about how many people experienced each configuration. Country sets also differ by indicator and lag because coverage is uneven; attainment series are missing for many small states and for several countries in the earliest decades. Rather than impute, I report denominators alongside shares. No value is modelled, interpolated, or back-cast, which produces the apparently odd feature, discussed below, that a median can fall and then rise across the three lags.

### What the Numbers Can and Cannot Show

Before any results appear: all of this is descriptive, there is no identification strategy here and none is implied, and the lags are not instruments. A table like this one will be read by many as a story about development causing fertility decline. It is not that story. It is a story about what development and fertility decline look like when they occur together — which they frequently do, and that co-occurrence is itself the finding. The distinction between co-occurrence and causation is the difference between an accounting exercise and a research design, and this paper is the former.

## The Club

One hundred and eleven countries have fallen below 2.1 births per woman and stayed there. The group spans every inhabited continent and every institutional tradition: the European Union in full, most of East Asia, the Anglophone settler economies, the Caribbean, the Southern Cone, the Maghreb, much of Southeast Asia and, as of the last few years, South Asia's largest country. The range of experience inside the club is wider than the label suggests, from countries that fell below replacement in the 1970s and have settled at levels implying orderly shrinkage, to countries whose rates are far below one birth per woman and whose arithmetic is not orderly at all.

### Arrival by Decade

Seventeen countries crossed in the 1980s, twenty-one in the 1990s, eighteen in the 2000s, nineteen in the 2010s, and three in the 2020s: Belize, India, and the Philippines (Figure 2).

The counts do not trend. This is the paper's first substantive point and it is easy to miss, because the eye wants a diffusion curve — a slow start, an acceleration, a long tail. What the counts show is a flat flow of roughly eighteen to twenty-one new members per decade for four consecutive decades, followed by an abrupt drop in the 2020s that is an artefact of the window rather than a change in the world. Three crossings in the first years of a decade is not evidence of exhaustion; it is evidence that the decade has not finished.

The steadiness survives the business cycle: five new members every two and a half years, through the debt crisis of the 1980s, the post-socialist collapse, the Asian financial crisis, and the global financial crisis. Underneath it, the composition shifted.

The 1980s cohort is heavily European and East Asian, disproportionately already industrialised. The 1990s cohort is dominated by the post-socialist world, the largest single addition in any decade, arriving in a compressed burst after 1989. The 2000s and 2010s cohorts are more mixed, drawing in Latin American, North African, Southeast Asian, and Middle Eastern members. The 2020s cohort, so far, is South Asian and Central American. The flow of members was steady; the flow of regions was not.

## What They Shared

Table 1 presents the central tabulation: the median value of each indicator among crossers at 20, 10, and 5 years before crossing, and among countries that never crossed (see also Figure 1).

The first and most robust observation is directional. On nine of the ten indicators the median crosser sits on the more developed side of the median never-crosser at every lag, and the gaps are not marginal. At twenty years before crossing the median crosser's income was $5,125 against $1,329, a ratio of roughly four to one; its child mortality 45 per 1,000 against 67; its urban share 52% against 39%; its electrification 95% against 44%; its paid employment share 59% against 31%.

Schooling shows the same direction with a distinctive shape. At twenty years before crossing the median crosser had 55% lower-secondary and 65% secondary completion against 44% and 49% for never-crossers; by five years before crossing those medians had reached 85% and 82%. A society in which 85% of adults have completed lower secondary is one in which nearly every woman of childbearing age has spent her adolescence in an institution organised by age and calendar rather than by household need. That is a different social world from the one summarised by 44%.

### The Trajectory

Table 2 reports the same information as change: median movement over the ten years preceding the crossing.

In that decade the median crosser added 13.7 percentage points to lower-secondary completion, 10.3 to secondary completion, and 8.4 to tertiary enrolment; reduced child mortality by 7.1 per 1,000; added $1,825 to income per head; and added 3.6 percentage points to the urban share. Every movement runs in the direction the cross-sectional comparison predicts. The implication a cross-section alone would miss is that crossers were not merely *at* a higher level of human capital and a lower level of mortality; they were *moving* on both, and moving quickly. A country arriving at 85% lower-secondary completion from 55% in twenty years has run an educational expansion at a pace few rich countries ever managed.

### Conditions at Ten Years

Medians describe the average member; they say nothing about how many members met any particular threshold. Table 3 and Figure 4 turn to that question.

Child mortality below 50 per 1,000 was the most widely shared condition, met by 81% of crossers. Income above $5,000 per head was met by 72%, and an urban share above 50% by 72%. Electricity coverage above 90% was met by 67%, lower-secondary completion above 70% by 66% on a denominator of 29 countries with usable data at that lag, and a female tertiary advantage — more women than men in tertiary education — by 53%. The strictest condition, income above $10,000, was met by 51%.

Two things follow. The first is that the ordering of conditions by universality is not the ordering the cross-sectional gaps would predict: child mortality ranks first, income second and, at the $10,000 threshold, last. Gaps measure how far apart the two groups were; shares measure how high an absolute threshold sits relative to the whole distribution. The second is the paper's headline, and it is negative: no condition is universal. The best-performing condition fails for nearly one crosser in five; income above $10,000 fails for nearly half. Had the exercise been designed to find the necessary condition for crossing, it would have failed. Nor does lowering the thresholds help, since a condition loose enough to be universal is one the never-crossers meet too. What the table shows is a set of conditions individually common, jointly near-universal in combination, and never individually complete. That is what a multi-causal process looks like when it is described rather than modelled.

### The Inversion

The tenth indicator breaks the pattern instructively. Female labour force participation among crossers was 42% at twenty years before crossing, 45% at ten, and 48% at five; among never-crossers it was 56%. A reader expecting the standard narrative would predict that crossers, being richer and better schooled, had *more* women in the labour force, not fewer.

The inversion is a measurement artefact. The participation rate counts women as employed if they report work for pay or profit, but the standard definition also counts unpaid work in a family enterprise — which, in an agricultural household, means subsistence farm work. In the poorest countries, where the never-crossers are concentrated, women's days are spent in field labour that the instrument duly records as participation. The rate for a subsistence economy can be very high and say very little about women's access to independent income or to a wage labour market. The paid employment share, which counts only work for pay or profit, runs the other way: 59%, 67%, and 68% for crossers against 31% for never-crossers. Crossers therefore had a smaller share of women in recorded economic activity but a far larger share of the economy organised as paid work. I report the inversion rather than suppressing it because it illustrates the hazard of this kind of tabulation: an indicator that appears to measure women's economic position is partly measuring the structure of agricultural production.

## The Exceptions

A profile built from medians describes a typical member; the interesting information is often in the members who do not fit. Figure 3 plots income per head against schooling at the moment of crossing.

### The Poor Crossers

Half of the club crossed below $10,000 per head — roughly the income of a middle-income country, not a rich one. That alone unsettles any account that treats a crossing as the culmination of development. The distribution has a more striking tail still. Table 4 lists the eight members that crossed below $3,000.

Armenia crossed in 1994 at $857 per head; China in 1991 at $990; Nepal in 2019 at $1,077; Vietnam in 1998 at $1,110; Georgia in 1992 at $1,514; India in 2020 at $1,807; Albania in 2002 at $2,297; Bhutan in 2012 at $2,655. These eight are not a random sample of poor countries. They fall into two recognisable families.

#### ***Post-Soviet Collapse***

Armenia, Georgia, and Albania crossed within a few years of 1990, in economies that had lost a large share of measured output, in states that had lost their institutional scaffolding, and in populations that had lost the certainties — housing allocations, guaranteed employment, subsidised childcare, a plannable future — on which family formation rested. The Armenian crossing is dated 1994, three years after independence, in an energy crisis and a war; the Georgian crossing is dated 1992. In all three, income per head at crossing was lower in real terms than a decade earlier. Whatever produced these crossings, it was not rising income. The post-socialist fertility collapse has been documented extensively (Kohler et al., 2002); these three are its poorest instances.

#### ***Early-Crossing Asia***

China, Vietnam, Nepal, India, and Bhutan crossed early with respect to development, arriving below replacement at income levels the European members had passed decades before their own crossings. The label needs a caveat, because they did not all cross early in calendar time — Nepal and India crossed in the last decade and Bhutan in the 2010s. China at $990 in 1991 and Vietnam at $1,110 in 1998 are the extreme cases: both had built unusually strong human capital for their income level, with high literacy, near-universal primary enrolment, and mortality profiles well ahead of what their income would predict, and both ran fertility programmes of unusual intensity. Nepal's crossing at $1,077 is the most surprising of the set, from a country with a large subsistence agricultural sector and a fertility rate above five as recently as the 1980s.

What unites the Asian family is not income but the configuration of the other indicators: child mortality far below what their income would predict, schooling far above it, and, in China and Vietnam, a labour force already organised around paid work. They crossed poor, but not undeveloped on every dimension at once — which is precisely the configuration a median-based profile conceals.

### A Third Kind of Exception

The Philippines is a different case again: a late crosser with a long history of high unmet need for contraception and institutional resistance to family planning, which crossed just below the threshold at about 1.2 women per man in tertiary education, with high secondary completion and income well below the levels at which most of the 1990s cohort crossed. It is awkward: if female education were the trigger, the Philippines should have crossed earlier; if female educational advantage were a consequence of low fertility, it should not have had that advantage beforehand. The defensible reading is the modest one: female educational advantage is neither a sufficient trigger nor a necessary consequence, and its presence a decade or two before a crossing is compatible with a long delay in the crossing itself.

### What the Exceptions Establish

Taken together the exceptions establish three things. First, the conditions in Table 3 are neither necessary nor sufficient, individually or jointly: Armenia met few and crossed, and several never-crossers meet more and have not. Second, the combination matters in a way individual shares cannot capture — the Asian poor crossers were poor on income but far ahead on mortality and schooling, while the post-Soviet crossers were poor on income and not ahead on anything, having crossed during an institutional rupture in which the usual relationship between development and fertility was temporarily inverted. Third, no single-mechanism story accommodates the club: income-led accounts cannot explain Armenia, schooling-led accounts cannot explain the timing of the Philippines, and mortality-led accounts cannot explain why so many countries with child mortality well below 50 have not crossed. None of this refutes the standard accounts, each developed for particular transitions in particular places; it is a reminder that any summary of the club, including this one, summarises a heterogeneous set.

## Discussion

The results are easy to summarise and hard to interpret: a clear direction of travel, no universal condition, and a set of exceptions that resist any single-mechanism story. Four points follow.

### The Company, Not the Cause

Nothing here identifies a cause. The tabulation describes the company that crossers kept on the way through the threshold, and company is not causation. Whether education produced the decline, the decline permitted the education, or a third process drove both is a question this design cannot answer, and the demand-versus-diffusion debate remains unresolved (Cleland & Wilson, 1987; Bongaarts & Watkins, 1996). What the tabulation can do is constrain the claims. An account that says the transition is fundamentally about income must explain a median income of $5,125 per head twenty years before crossing and eight members below $3,000. An account centred on child mortality must explain why 19% of crossers were still above 50 deaths per 1,000 a decade before crossing, and why so many countries below that level have not crossed. Each standard account survives these constraints, but each survives by being amended.

### The Convergence and the Flow

Two features sit awkwardly together. On every indicator, crossers were closer to the rich-country frontier than never-crossers, and closer at five years before crossing than at twenty: convergence. Yet the number of new members per decade barely moved for four decades — seventeen, twenty-one, eighteen, nineteen: constancy. If crossing were a threshold phenomenon driven by a single condition on which the world was converging, the flow should have accelerated and then decelerated. It did neither. That is consistent with partly substitutable conditions, some routes opening as others close, and equally consistent with the timing of crossing being set by something these ten indicators do not capture. The data cannot distinguish these readings, but they rule out the simplest threshold story, in which one variable gates entry and everything else is commentary.

### Human Capital and Its Discontents

The schooling indicators produced both the largest cross-sectional gaps and the weakest condition shares: the median crosser had 85% lower-secondary completion five years before crossing against 44% for never-crossers, a forty-one point gap, the largest in Table 1, yet only 66% cleared the 70% threshold at the ten-year mark. The reconciliation is that the schooling distribution among crossers is wide and its floor is low: the club includes countries whose adult populations were less than half lower-secondary complete. What distinguishes crossers is not that every crosser had a schooled population but that its population was schooled to a degree unusual for its income, and rising fast. That is consistent with the human-capital literature's emphasis on the joint evolution of education, mortality, and the economy rather than on any one of them as a trigger (Lutz et al., 2014).

### What the Never-Crossers Tell Us

The comparison group is not merely statistical. The never-crossers are mainly the countries of sub-Saharan Africa plus a scattering of fragile and stalled states: median income $1,329, child mortality 67, electrification 44%, paid employment 31%. The literature on stalled African transitions argues that the region's decline has been slower and more interrupted than the historical record elsewhere would predict (Bongaarts, 2008; Bongaarts & Casterline, 2013). The tabulation supports the descriptive half of that claim without endorsing the explanatory half. The gaps are widest on the variables describing the physical and occupational structure of the household — electricity, paid employment — rather than on individual attributes such as schooling or mortality. That describes where the frontier currently sits. It does not predict that any country now among the never-crossers will cross on reaching a particular value on any of these scales, and the exceptions above are why such a prediction would be unwise.

## Limitations

The limitations fall into five groups. The first is measurement. Total fertility rates are period measures sensitive to timing as well as number, so several crossings may reflect tempo effects as much as quantum. Adult attainment lags schooling by decades; tertiary enrolment is a flow that depends on cohort size; the urban share depends on administrative definitions that differ by a factor of two across countries; electrification may mean connection or reliable supply; and female labour force participation conflates subsistence with market work.

The second is the dating rule. Requiring that the rate stay below 2.1 for the remainder of the series means the confirmation window is short for late crossers: the 2020s cohort is confirmed on very few observations and any of the three could return above the threshold and be reclassified. The rule also excludes countries that dipped and recovered, which may include genuine early members.

The third is the comparison. The never-crossers are observed across a long window while crossers are observed at fixed lags, so the contrast confounds group differences with period differences, and the never-crossers include countries on the verge of crossing as well as countries with rising fertility.

The fourth is aggregation. Every indicator is national, and several members — India above all — contain subnational populations that differ from one another by more than crossers differ from never-crossers. Countries are also unweighted, so the tabulations describe countries, not people.

The fifth is inference, and it is why the paper refuses causal language: there is no design here, the lags are descriptive conventions, and the indicators are mutually determined. A final limitation is framing: the indicators are the ones the literature names, and a different set — contraceptive prevalence, marriage timing, housing costs, childcare prices — would produce a different profile. Their absence reflects coverage, not a judgment about importance.

## Conclusion

One hundred and eleven countries have fallen below 2.1 births per woman and stayed there, arriving at roughly eighteen to twenty per decade for four decades: seventeen in the 1980s, twenty-one in the 1990s, eighteen in the 2000s, nineteen in the 2010s, and three so far in the 2020s — Belize, India, and the Philippines.

What they shared, before crossing, was a direction of travel more than a level. At twenty, ten, and five years before crossing the median member was richer, more urban, more electrified, better schooled, and less likely to lose a child than the median country that never crossed, and it was moving on all of those fronts. But the shares are more chastened than the medians: 81% had child mortality below 50 ten years before crossing, 72% had income above $5,000, 72% were more than half urban, 67% were more than 90% electrified, 66% had lower-secondary completion above 70% on a denominator of 29, 53% had more women than men in tertiary education, and 51% had income above $10,000. No condition was universal. Half crossed below $10,000 per head and eight below $3,000, from two very different families — post-Soviet collapse and early-crossing Asia.

This is an accounting exercise. It says what the club looked like on the way in and nothing about what pushed anyone through the door. The distinction is not modesty for its own sake: a field that knows the shape of the transition but not its mechanism will keep producing advice that is confidently wrong about the countries that do not fit the median.
