## Abstract

A widely held account of the fertility transition holds that the collapse of smallholder agriculture displaced families into wage employment, and that this displacement—by raising the cost of children and dissolving the family labour economy—drove the secular decline in births. This paper decomposes that account into four links and tests each on a country-decade panel covering 185 countries and up to 4,335 country-decades. Three links are confirmed. A new shift-share test prices what farms *buy* rather than what they *sell*: world fertiliser prices relative to GDP per head, interacted with baseline farm dependence, show that falling input prices accelerate farm exit (+0.445 on the farm employment share, *SE* = 0.082) and accelerate the growth of wage employment (−0.243, 0.096). The mechanisation shock is real and it moves labour. The fourth link is not confirmed, and the chain breaks there: the same interaction has no effect on births per woman (−0.006, 0.010), a well-powered null on 8,419 country-years. The older finding also stands: births predict the subsequent farm employment share (+1.536, 0.406) while the farm share does not predict births (+0.0011, 0.0041). Farm exit therefore has two drivers, input prices and falling fertility, and fertility responds to neither. An earlier test of output prices—the FAO Food Price Index, available only from 1990—points the other way, and measures a different price: farm revenue under crisis, not the cost of mechanisation relative to labour.

*Keywords:* fertility transition, agricultural employment, structural change, input prices, mechanisation, food prices

## Introduction

Few propositions in the social sciences are as widely repeated, and as rarely put at risk, as the claim that the fertility transition was carried into the world by the decline of peasant agriculture. The story is not foolish: in country after country the share of the workforce in farming fell and the number of children born to each woman fell within the same generation. The mechanism is intuitive: when a household farms, children are productive assets whose labour the household head can direct and whose cost is largely the food they eat; when the household becomes a wage-earning urban household, children become expensive and their contribution to the household economy disappears.

The most fully specified version of the argument is Boppart, Kiernan, Krusell, and Malmberg (2023), who model an economy in which a very large share of the workforce works in agriculture at very low labour productivity, and in which the choice of technique—how much capital and purchased input to use per worker—jointly determines labour demand and the return to children. Their mechanism implies a specific price to test: not the price of what farms sell, but the price of what farms buy, relative to what labour costs. Mechanisation is the substitution of purchased inputs for workers, and its incentive is the price of a unit of modern input relative to a unit of labour.

This paper tests the chain in both places, and the results divide cleanly. Three of its four links hold. Input prices, measured as world fertiliser prices relative to domestic income, move farm employment and wage employment in the direction the mechanism predicts, on a sample that begins in 1960 rather than 1990. Farm exit feeds wage employment. Prior fertility predicts subsequent farm exit. The link that fails is the one that carries the argument into demography: the same input-price shock has no effect on births. The mechanisation story is therefore supported for labour and unsupported for fertility—not rejected, as an earlier draft of this paper concluded, but narrowed to the half of the economy in which it operates.

That correction matters, because the earlier conclusion was too strong in both directions. It dismissed a mechanism that turns out to work, and it accepted a framing—farming follows fertility—that is accurate as far as it goes but names only one of the two things pushing labour off the land. Farming does follow fertility: the reverse regression is large and precise (+1.536, *SE* = 0.406) while the forward regression is empty (+0.0011, 0.0041). But farming also follows the price of fertiliser, in the direction Boppart's model requires.

## The Mechanisation Story

### The Wage-Labour Variant

The first variant is a theory of the price of time. In a peasant household production and reproduction share one budget and one set of adults; the marginal cost of a child is largely the food the child eats, and the marginal product of a child's labour accrues to the household that raises it. When agricultural work gives way to wage employment, work is separated from the residence, the return to women's time rises outside the home, and the child's future earnings no longer accrue to the household that bore the cost of raising it. This is the Becker–Lewis quantity–quality trade-off transposed to the economy (Becker & Lewis, 1973), formalised by Galor and Weil (1996) and extended by Doepke (2005) to mortality decline. The proximate variables are wages, schooling, and the opportunity cost of women's time.

Boppart et al. (2023) supply the missing production side. In their account agriculture is not a passive reservoir but a technique choice: farmers may farm intensively, with large doses of capital and purchased input per worker and small labour requirements per hectare, or extensively, with many hands and little capital. Which technique is chosen depends on the price of the modern input relative to the wage, and the technique determines how much labour agriculture releases and at what productivity. That is what makes the price of fertiliser, machinery, and capital—rather than the price of grain—the relevant shock.

### The Dispossession Variant

The second variant is a theory of land. The mechanism is not the wage but the loss of the household's productive base: enclosure, consolidation, and the reorganisation of tenancy push families off land they had worked, and landlessness—not employment status—changes the reproductive calculus. A household without secure land cannot settle a son, cannot divide a holding among heirs, and cannot marry early for want of a livelihood; fertility falls. This is the variant most people have in mind when they say that agricultural decline drove the fertility transition, because it names a villain.

The variants are not interchangeable: in the dispossession variant a country can lose its smallholders while farm employment rises, since the dispossessed become labourers on large estates. The cross-national data measure employment, not tenure, which governs everything that follows.

## Data and Method

### Unit of Analysis and Sample

The unit of analysis is the country-decade. For each country, observations are built at ten-year intervals, and each pairs the level of a predictor at the start of a decade with the change in the outcome over the following decade. The design places the hypothesised cause before the effect in calendar time.

The widest specification covers 4,335 country-decades and 185 countries; the narrowest covers 1,899 country-decades and 162 countries. The shrinkage is driven by the covariates with the thinnest coverage—schooling, child survival, and the paid-employment share—and removes disproportionately the poorest countries and earliest decades, precisely those in which the mechanism should be most visible. A null on the narrow sample is the weaker evidence.

### Measures

**Outcome.** The dependent variable is the ten-year change in the crude birth rate, in births per 1,000 population. The crude rate is used rather than the total fertility rate because it is available annually for essentially every country in the panel and is not distorted by the models that produce period fertility measures where registration is incomplete. It moves with age structure and migration, a liability I return to below; the results rest on changes rather than levels. The input-price test uses births per woman, available for a denser set of country-years, which allows the fertility null to be estimated with more power.

**Predictors.** The farm employment share is employment in agriculture as a percentage of total employment, from the modelled International Labour Organization series distributed through the World Bank's World Development Indicators. It is a quantity measure—the proportion of the workforce actually in farming—and the measure most directly implied by the wage-labour variant. The farm output share is agriculture, forestry, and fishing value added as a percentage of GDP: a hybrid of quantity and price, since both numerator and denominator move with world prices, and the results will show it to be the less trustworthy of the two.

**Prices.** The *output price* is the FAO Food Price Index, a trade-weighted index of world prices for the food commodities farms sell. The *input price* is a fertiliser index built from the World Bank's Pink Sheet real commodity price series (World Bank, 2024): phosphate rock, diammonium phosphate, and potassium chloride, averaged in equal weights and divided by GDP per head. The division matters. A deflated fertiliser price says what a tonne of nutrient costs in dollars; divided by income per head it says what a tonne costs in units of domestic output, the quantity that governs whether a farmer substitutes fertiliser for a worker. That series runs from 1960 to 2024, thirty years earlier than the food price index.

**Covariates.** Five covariates enter as baseline (start-of-decade) levels in the sequence the variants imply. *Income* is the log of GDP per capita. *Cities* is the urban share of the population. *Schooling* is mean years of schooling of the adult population. *Child survival* is the under-five survival rate in percent. *Paid employment* is the wage and salaried share of total employment, the closest cross-national analogue to the "wage labour" of the first variant.

### Estimation

All specifications are estimated by ordinary least squares on decade differences, with decade fixed effects and standard errors clustered by country; country fixed effects are absorbed by the differencing. The coefficient of interest is always on a baseline level of an agricultural measure: how much does a higher initial agricultural share shift the subsequent ten-year change in births? A negative coefficient supports the mechanisation story. Specifications are built as a sequence and reported in full, because a result resting on one specification invites the objection that the author chose the wrong controls.

The price tests use the same estimator with one change: the price series is global and identical for every country in a given year, so its main effect is absorbed by decade fixed effects. What is estimated is its interaction with *baseline* farm dependence—the farm employment share in the first decade a country is observed—fixed before the price movements studied. Countries more agricultural at baseline are more exposed to shocks to the cost of farming. The identifying assumption is that baseline farm dependence is not correlated with other country-specific trends in the outcome.

### What Would Have Counted as Support

Because much of this paper reports a failure, it owes the reader a statement of what success would have looked like, fixed in advance. Four conditions were set: the coefficient on baseline farm employment should be negative and at least twice its standard error (on the order of −0.25); its sign should not change across covariate sets; the farm share should predict subsequent births better than births predict the subsequent farm share; and the coefficient should shrink, not grow, when the mediators the mechanism names are added. None of the four is met.

The input-price test is a separate exercise with its own criterion, stated in advance: if the mechanism is real, the price of an input relative to labour should move farm employment and wage employment in opposite directions, with the sign such that cheap inputs accelerate exit, and it should move births in the same direction as the labour variables if the demographic link belongs to the same mechanism.

## Results

### Farm Employment

Table 1 reports the coefficient on the baseline farm employment share in five specifications, each adding one covariate. The coefficient is never negative in the way the hypothesis requires and never statistically distinguishable from zero. With income controlled it is +0.0013 (*SE* = 0.0041); with cities, −0.0007 (0.0038); with schooling, +0.0051 (0.0054); with child survival, −0.0026 (0.0048); with paid employment, −0.0035 (0.0055). Across all specifications estimated, it ranges from +0.0013 to −0.0059. It has no location: it changes sign three times while the standard error stays in a narrow band.

The mediators compete with the relationship rather than carrying it, which is what one expects when the agricultural and non-agricultural variables are all proxies for the same underlying quantity.

### Farm Output

The farm output share tells a superficially more encouraging story, and its collapse is correspondingly more instructive (Table 2). Estimated alone, agriculture's share of value added carries a coefficient of +0.0128 (*SE* = 0.0030)—large, precise, and pointing the wrong way for the hypothesis: countries with a larger agricultural share of output saw births fall *less* over the following decade. Adding income and cities reduces it to +0.0081 (0.0028), still precise and still positive. Adding schooling and child survival turns it to −0.0050 (0.0041), the sign the hypothesis wants and not significant.

## The Direction Problem

Everything so far tests the hypothesis in one direction. A coefficient hovering around zero is compatible with two quite different situations—no relationship at all, or a relationship that runs the other way, so that the forward regression is contaminated by reverse causation and the estimates are pulled toward zero and beyond.

The panel allows a direct comparison. If the mechanisation story is right, the farm share at the start of a decade should carry more information about the subsequent change in births than the birth rate at the start of a decade carries about the subsequent change in the farm share. Estimating both directions gives the opposite answer. The farm share predicting the ten-year change in births yields +0.0011 (*SE* = 0.0041): nothing. Births predicting the ten-year change in the farm employment share yields +1.536 (0.406): nearly four times its standard error, and in the direction that says a country with a birth rate one point higher at the start of a decade had a farm employment share roughly 1.5 points higher ten years later.

Right panel: coefficient on the baseline birth rate predicting the ten-year change in the farm employment share (*β* = +1.536, *SE* = 0.406, significant). Same estimator and fixed effects throughout. This is not an identification strategy: neither regression isolates exogenous variation, and a country with strong kinship pressure toward large families may also have institutions that keep labour on the land, in which case both coefficients reflect the same unobserved variable. What the asymmetry establishes is weaker and still useful—the data do not permit the claim that farming leads. Nor is +1.536 a structural multiplier; its role here is diagnostic.

## Prices

One test comes closest to an exogenous shock. If a rise in agricultural prices should retain labour in agriculture and slow the fertility decline in farm-dependent countries, then the FAO Food Price Index interacted with baseline farm dependence tests the mechanism directly, on samples of 3,232 to 3,407 country-decades and 183 countries, since the paid-employment series is shorter than the farm-employment series. The results (Table 4, Figure 4) are large, precise, and uniform: farm employment share −0.668 (*SE* = 0.072), paid employment −0.712 (0.110), births −0.733 (0.045).

The signs are unambiguous and they run the wrong way for the hypothesis. A rise in world food prices makes farm-dependent countries shed farm employment *faster*, pushes them into paid employment *faster*, and lowers their birth rate *faster*. On the mechanisation story, higher prices should have protected the farm sector. What the data show is a distress response: when food prices spike, the farm-dependent economy empties out, households scramble for cash work, and fertility falls—a story about crisis, in which exit and fertility decline are joint consequences of a shock.

By then the farm employment transition had largely finished across Latin America, East Asia, Southern Europe, and the high-income world, so the countries where the farm share was still large enough for an interaction to mean anything were, by construction, the laggards.

## The Input-Price Test

The FAO test prices what farms sell; the mechanism in Boppart et al. (2023) is about what farms buy. A farmer deciding whether to keep a son on the land, hire a labourer, or buy a machine faces the price of non-labour input relative to the wage; a farmer deciding whether farming is worth doing at all faces the price of output relative to everything he must pay for. The two prices can move apart, and when they do the theory speaks to only one of them.

### Construction

The input price is built from the World Bank's real commodity price series, the Pink Sheet, which runs from 1960 to 2024 (World Bank, 2024). Three fertiliser series are averaged in equal weights—phosphate rock, diammonium phosphate, and potassium chloride—to form a real fertiliser price index, and that index is divided by GDP per head. The division makes it a mechanism variable rather than a commodity price: a tonne of nutrient is converted into the quantity of domestic output required to buy it. If a tonne of fertiliser cost 30 tonnes of grain in 1960 and 10 tonnes in 2000, mechanisation has become cheaper in exactly the sense the model means.

Because the series is global, identification comes from interaction with baseline farm dependence: countries heavily agricultural at baseline are the ones whose producers buy fertiliser, so a shift in its relative price should be visible in their labour allocation and invisible in countries already urban. The main effect of the global price is absorbed by decade fixed effects; what is estimated is the differential response of exposed countries, with country and decade fixed effects, the outcome's own starting level controlled, and standard errors clustered by country. Employment outcomes use 4,297 country-decades across 184 countries; fertility uses 8,419 country-years, since births per woman is available annually and the extra observations buy precision on the null.

### Reading the Signs

Take the farm employment coefficient, +0.445. A rise in fertiliser prices relative to income is associated with a farm employment share that falls *more slowly*: a country exposed to a price increase retains labour on the land. Read the other way, which is what matters for the historical record: the long secular fall in fertiliser prices relative to income—one of the great unnoticed price declines of the twentieth century—is associated with farm employment falling *faster*. Cheap input accelerates exit, because a unit of modern input that costs less relative to a worker induces substitution of input for labour.

The fertility coefficient, −0.006 with a standard error of 0.010, is nothing, and its sign is not even the one the mechanism would want. Nor is this an underpowered test: the estimate rules out at conventional confidence any effect larger than about ±0.02 births per woman per unit of the interaction—far too small to carry a fertility transition, on 8,419 observations. This is a clean, well-powered zero on the outcome the paper cares most about, from a shock that demonstrably moves the labour variables in the same specification family.

### The Chain, Link by Link

The four links can now be read off. The first, input prices to farm exit, is confirmed: +0.445 (0.082). The second, farm exit to wage employment, is confirmed: −0.243 (0.096). The mechanisation shock is real and it moves labour as the model says it should. The third, input prices to fertility, is not confirmed: −0.006 (0.010). The fourth, from fertility to farm exit, is confirmed and large: +1.536 (0.406). The chain holds from the first link to the second, breaks between the second and the third, and is joined from the other end by the fourth. Agricultural decline has two drivers—the falling price of modern inputs and the falling number of births—and fertility responds to neither.

### Why Output Prices and Input Prices Disagree

The first is which price is priced, and the second which margin it moves. Output price is revenue per unit sold and acts on the *extensive* margin—how much land is farmed, whether farming is worth doing at all. Input price is cost per unit of technique and acts on the *intensive* margin—how much capital and purchased input per hectare, and therefore how many workers per hectare. A rise in the food price also raises the value of land, and rents capitalise most of the gain: the owner captures it, the tenant does not, and the labourer who might have stayed captures none of it. A rise in the fertiliser price changes the cost of the technique directly, and its effect on labour demand does not depend on who owns the land. Mechanisation is an intensive-margin phenomenon, so a test built on output prices tests whether farming expands or contracts, not whether it becomes more or less labour-intensive—and the two can run in opposite directions, as they do here.

The third is what a food price spike actually is. The FAO index is dominated by the 2008 and 2011 episodes—global food crises accompanied by export bans and collapsing real incomes in import-dependent countries. An interaction estimated on those years picks up distress, and all three FAO coefficients being negative and similar in size is the signature of a common shock rather than of a mechanism with a labour-market channel and a separate demographic one.

None of this rescues the FAO result or overturns it; the output-price test is correctly estimated and answers a different question. The lesson is that "prices" is not one variable: a literature that tests the mechanisation story with the price of grain will keep finding distress, and keep concluding that the mechanism is absent, when what it has found is that the mechanism is not about grain.

## Discussion

The results support a narrower claim than either a confirmation or a refutation: the shock is real, it moves labour, and it stops at the labour market. The first reading of the remaining failure is that the demographic link is genuinely absent—that fertility is governed by something other than the cost of a child's time and food. The strongest support is not the null on the farm share but the coefficient on prior fertility (−0.644) and the persistence of child survival (+0.0067): the variable that survives measures how many children are dying, not what people do for a living. Nor is the input-price null a null of ignorance. If the fertility transition were the demographic shadow of agricultural mechanisation, a shock that visibly reallocated labour out of farming should have left some trace in births. It left none, which is what the demand-sceptical literature would expect—Cleland and Wilson (1987) on diffusion, Bongaarts (2008) on stalled transitions, Bongaarts and Casterline (2013) on sub-Saharan Africa's slower and differently shaped decline.

The child-survival coefficient needs a note. With prior fertility held constant, higher survival predicts a *smaller* decline in births—not because survival raises fertility, but because of a lag: survival improvements precede the recognition that fewer births are needed, the interval Lloyd and Ivanov (1988) documented and Doepke (2005) formalised.

The second reading is that the measure is wrong, though the new result weakens it. Farm *employment* is not farm *dispossession*: a country can lose its smallholders without any fall in agricultural employment, since the consolidated estate hires the former smallholder as a labourer. But the employment measure has now been shown to detect the technique shock where the technique shock operates—it responds strongly to input prices, in the right direction. A measure sensitive enough to register the mechanisation of farming, and registering nothing when the same shock is turned on births, is more plausibly registering the half of the process it can see than failing outright.

What the paper no longer says is that the mechanisation story is unsupported. It says the story is supported for the movement of labour and unsupported for the movement of births, and that the interesting question is why a shock which plainly reorganises production should leave reproduction untouched. Two answers are worth stating: that the demand for children is anchored in institutions—kinship, religion, the sexual division of labour, the availability of birth control—that respond to production with a lag measured in generations; and that the fertility transition is a diffusion process, driven by the spread of birth control and the example of neighbours rather than by the wage.

## Limitations

The measure of agricultural decline is an employment share built from modelled estimates rather than census microdata. It counts as agricultural employment the own-account and unpaid family labour that agricultural censuses record inconsistently and that is disproportionately female. Women's farm work is the most undercounted labour in the world's statistics, and it is the labour most directly implicated in the mechanism: the child-rearing cost that rises when women leave the farm is precisely the cost these data fail to see.

The input-price test has three limitations of its own, and they bound what can be claimed for it. First, fertiliser is one input. Boppart et al. (2023) also cover machinery and capital, and the relative price of a tractor is a different series with a different history; the fertiliser index stands in for the whole class of labour-substituting inputs and may move imperfectly with the others. Second, GDP per head is a crude proxy for an agricultural wage: dividing a world price by economy-wide income per person is not dividing it by the wage a farm worker earns or by the shadow price of a family member's time on a smallholding. An agricultural value-added per worker series would be the better denominator, and its absence means the coefficient should be read as an elasticity with respect to *affordability*, not the input–labour price ratio proper. Third, the design is a single specification family. The fertility results were run through a full sequence of covariate sets and reported in full; the input-price test was not, and has not been run through a specification curve. The employment coefficients are large enough that a curve is unlikely to overturn them, but the fertility null deserves the same treatment the paper gives its other nulls.

The outcome in the main tests is the crude birth rate, which moves with age structure and migration as well as fertility. The direction test is especially exposed, since emigration from agrarian economies could generate a spurious association between high births and a persistently high farm share. The input-price test uses births per woman and is less exposed, which is part of why it carries the paper's conclusion.

The covariate sequence is not a causal ordering, and the estimates are associations in differences. No instrument is used and no natural experiment exploited. The price tests are shift-shares, and a shift-share coefficient is a weighted average of heterogeneous responses whose weights are baseline farm dependence; it is not a structural elasticity of substitution, and it cannot say whether the countries driving the result are the ones the theory would nominate. The food-price exercise is confined to the post-1990 period. The criteria for support were written down before the results were assembled but not preregistered; the full set of specifications is reported so the suspicion that they were fitted to the failure can be checked.

## Conclusion

The hypothesis was reasonable, it was tested in two places, and it survived in one of them.

Across 4,335 country-decades and 185 countries, the farm employment share does not predict the subsequent change in births; its coefficient moves across zero, changes sign, and never attains precision. The farm output share produces a significant coefficient in sparse specifications and loses it once schooling and child survival enter. In the seven-variable horse race the only precise predictors are prior fertility and child survival. And the reverse test shows the farm share following fertility at +1.536 (0.406) while fertility does not follow the farm share at all (+0.0011, 0.0041).

But the price of what farms buy tells a different story from the price of what farms sell. On fertiliser prices relative to income from 1960 to 2024, a fall in the relative price of modern input is associated with farm employment falling faster (+0.445, 0.082) and wage employment growing faster (−0.243, 0.096), across 4,297 country-decades and 184 countries. The mechanisation shock is real: it moves labour in the direction Boppart et al. (2023) predict, on a sample spanning the period in which the transition happened, with the price variable the theory names.

What the shock does not do is move births. On 8,419 country-years, with a standard error of 0.010, the interaction has no effect on births per woman. The chain has four links—input prices to farm exit, farm exit to wage employment, input prices to fertility, and fertility to farm exit—and it breaks between the fourth and the third, not between the first and the second. Farming matters for the labour market; the fertility transition runs on a clock of its own.

The dispossession variant may still be true everywhere, and it has not been tested here, because the data that would test it—the distribution of land and tenure rather than the count of farm jobs—do not exist as a comparable cross-national series. Building that series is the next step, and it is now more sharply posed: not whether agricultural change moves people, but whether landlessness moves births in a way that employment status does not.
