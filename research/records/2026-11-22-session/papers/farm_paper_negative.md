## Abstract

A widely held account of the fertility transition holds that the collapse of smallholder agriculture displaced families into cities and wage employment, and that this displacement—by raising the cost of children, separating work from the household, and dissolving the family labour economy—drove the secular decline in births. This paper tests that account on a country-decade panel covering 185 countries and up to 4,335 country-decades. The farm employment share is a poor predictor of the subsequent ten-year change in births: its coefficient wanders across zero across five covariate sets (+0.0013 to −0.0059), is never statistically distinguishable from zero, and changes sign. The farm output share performs no better once schooling and child survival enter, at which point its coefficient collapses from +0.0128 (0.0030) to −0.0050 (0.0041). A direction test is decisive: the farm share does not predict births (+0.0011, 0.0041), but births predict the farm share (+1.536, 0.406). Farming follows fertility. Food-price interactions confirm the co-movement while reversing its interpretation: rising prices accelerate farm exit *and* fertility decline, a crisis response rather than a profitability mechanism—and the price series begins in 1990, after most of the world's agricultural transition had ended. The paper distinguishes farm *employment*, which was measured, from smallholder *dispossession*, which was not, and specifies the land-distribution data required to test the latter.

*Keywords:* fertility transition, agricultural employment, structural change, negative result, food prices, child survival, land inequality

## Introduction

Few propositions in the social sciences are as widely repeated, and as rarely put at risk, as the claim that the fertility transition was carried into the world by the decline of peasant agriculture. The story is not foolish. In country after country the share of the workforce in farming fell and the number of children born to each woman fell, usually within the same generation. The correlation is strong, the timing suggestive, and the mechanism intuitive: when a household farms, children are productive assets whose labour the household head can direct and whose cost is largely the food they eat; when the household becomes a wage-earning urban household, children become expensive and their contribution to the household economy disappears. On this reading, agricultural decline is not merely a correlate of the demographic transition but one of its motors.

This paper takes that claim seriously and tests it, and reports that it does not survive. Across a country-decade panel of unusual coverage, the share of the labour force in agriculture does not predict the subsequent ten-year change in births—not on its own, not with income, urbanisation, schooling, child survival, or paid employment controlled. Worse for the hypothesis, the predictive information runs the other way: a country's fertility predicts its subsequent agricultural employment share with a coefficient nearly four times its standard error, while the reverse prediction is indistinguishable from noise. Farming follows fertility. It does not lead it.

The next section reconstructs the mechanisation story in its two variants, because a hypothesis must be stated at its strongest before it is rejected. I then describe the data and specify in advance what would have counted as support: a negative coefficient of stable sign and meaningful size, robust to the standard battery of covariates, that survives the reverse-direction test and is attenuated by the mediators the mechanism names. None of these four conditions is met. The farm employment coefficient moves from +0.0013 to −0.0059 across specifications, with standard errors of four to six thousandths. The farm output share, which produces a large and precise positive relationship in sparse specifications, reverses sign when schooling and child survival are added. A seven-variable horse race leaves prior fertility and child survival as the only precise predictors.

Two further sections deepen the negative result rather than softening it. The Direction Problem reports the reverse regression that settles the temporal question empirically even if it cannot settle it causally: fertility leads, farming follows. Prices uses the FAO Food Price Index interacted with baseline farm dependence and finds large, consistently signed effects on farm exit, paid-employment growth, and births alike—the sign pattern of distress, not of mechanisation, and estimated almost entirely after 1990, by which time most of the world's agricultural transition was complete.

The contribution is deliberately negative. Negative results are rarely published in this literature, and the effect is visible: the record contains the studies that found something and not the studies that did not, so the field's impression of how well agricultural decline predicts fertility decline is formed from a censored sample. I do not claim to have disproved a mechanism. I claim that the cross-national record refuses the hypothesis—and that the measures that exist are the wrong ones for the hypothesis as originally stated, because they record farm *employment* while the story is about smallholder *dispossession*.

## The Mechanisation Story

The story comes in two variants that are often run together and should be kept apart, because they make different predictions.

### The Wage-Labour Variant

The first variant is a theory of the price of time. In a peasant household, production and reproduction share one budget and one set of adults; the marginal cost of a child is largely the food the child eats, and the marginal product of a child's labour accrues directly to the household that raises the child. When agricultural work gives way to wage employment, three things change at once. Work is separated from the residence, so child care must be purchased or undertaken at the cost of foregone wages. The return to women's time rises outside the home, raising the opportunity cost of intensive child-rearing. And the child's future earnings no longer accrue to the household that bore the cost of raising the child, weakening the old-age security motive.

This is the Becker–Lewis quantity–quality trade-off transposed from the household to the economy (Becker & Lewis, 1973). It does not require agriculture as such; it requires that the return to child quality rise relative to the return to child quantity, which is what happens when wages available to educated workers rise relative to those available to illiterate farm labour. Galor and Weil (1996) formalised the transition from a Malthusian regime to modern growth in which the rising return to human capital induces parents to substitute quality for quantity, and Doepke (2005) showed that mortality decline interacts with this substitution in ways the simplest Barro–Becker framework does not capture. In this variant, agricultural decline matters because it is the occasion for the shift to wage labour, not because land tenure as such matters. The proximate variables are wages, schooling, and the opportunity cost of women's time.

### The Dispossession Variant

The second variant is a theory of land. Here the mechanism is not the wage but the loss of the household's productive base: enclosure, consolidation, mechanisation, and the reorganisation of tenancy push families off land they had worked, and landlessness—not employment status—changes the reproductive calculus. A household without secure land cannot settle a son, cannot divide a holding among heirs, and cannot feed a large family from its own plot; marriage is delayed for want of a livelihood, and fertility falls. This is the variant most people have in mind when they say that agricultural decline drove the fertility transition, because it names a villain.

The variants are not interchangeable. In the wage-labour variant a country can industrialise and its fertility fall while its farm employment share stays high, provided wages rise and schooling expands. In the dispossession variant a country can lose its smallholders and its fertility can fall while farm employment rises, because the dispossessed become labourers on large estates—still employed in agriculture, no longer smallholders. This distinction governs everything that follows: the cross-national data measure employment, not tenure, and therefore speak to the first variant while leaving the second largely untested.

### Why the Story Was Reasonable

Three considerations made the story attractive enough to be treated as background fact. First, timing: in most of Latin America, East Asia, and Southern Europe the farm employment share and the birth rate fell together across the second half of the twentieth century, and the cross-sectional correlation between them is strong. Second, the mechanism is legible at household level, and survey and ethnographic evidence from many settings is consistent with it. Third, there is a coherent aggregate account of why the two should be linked: Boppart, Kiernan, Krusell, and Malmberg (2023) argue that developing economies employ a very large share of their workforce in agriculture at very low labour productivity, and that the interaction of this labour-intensive structure with demographic behaviour is central to development. If agriculture absorbs labour at low productivity and agricultural households want large families, then reallocation out of agriculture is not a side-effect of the fertility transition but part of its machinery.

Against this, the critical literature has long warned that demand-side accounts of fertility decline, of which the mechanisation story is one, over-predict and under-explain. Cleland and Wilson (1987) mounted the classic case that demand theories cannot account for the timing and diffusion of fertility decline, and that the spread of birth control does explanatory work that changes in the structure of production cannot do. Bongaarts (2008) documented stalled transitions in countries whose development should, on demand accounts, have produced continued decline. Bongaarts and Casterline (2013) concluded that sub-Saharan Africa's transition is slower and differently shaped, with demand for large families and limited uptake of birth control doing more work than economic structure. None of these authors refutes the mechanisation story directly, but all make room for the possibility that the correlation between agricultural decline and fertility decline is a correlation between two consequences of something else.

## Data and Method

### Unit of Analysis and Sample

The unit of analysis is the country-decade. For each country, observations are built at ten-year intervals, and each observation pairs the level of a predictor at the start of a decade with the change in the outcome over the following decade. This design is coarse; it is also the coarsest design that can carry a claim about a transition, and finer intervals buy precision at the cost of the coverage that makes a cross-national claim possible. It has one virtue that matters here: it places the hypothesised cause before the hypothesised effect in calendar time, so the test is at least capable of detecting a lead.

The widest specification covers 4,335 country-decades and 185 countries; the narrowest covers 1,899 country-decades and 162 countries. The shrinkage is not random. It is driven by the covariates with the thinnest historical coverage—schooling, child survival, and the paid-employment share—and it removes disproportionately the poorest countries and earliest decades, which are precisely those in which a mechanism running through agricultural labour markets should be most visible. A null on the narrow sample is therefore weaker evidence than a null on the wide one, and I lean on the wide sample wherever the comparison is available.

### Measures

**Outcome.** The dependent variable is the ten-year change in the crude birth rate, in births per 1,000 population. The crude rate is used rather than the total fertility rate because it is available annually for essentially every country in the panel, is not distorted by the models that produce period fertility measures where registration is incomplete, and is the measure the descriptive literature most often reports. It moves with age structure and migration, a liability I return to below; the results rest on changes rather than levels, and the changes are large enough that level measurement noise is unlikely to account for the patterns reported.

**Predictors of interest.** Two measures of agricultural decline are used. The farm employment share is employment in agriculture as a percentage of total employment, from the modelled International Labour Organization series distributed through the World Bank's World Development Indicators. It is a quantity measure—the proportion of the workforce actually in farming—and it is the measure most directly implied by the wage-labour variant. The farm output share is agriculture, forestry, and fishing value added as a percentage of GDP. It is a hybrid of quantity and price, since both numerator and denominator move with world prices and the exchange rate, and the results will show it to be the less trustworthy of the two.

**Covariates.** Five covariates enter as baseline (start-of-decade) levels in the sequence the two variants imply. *Income* is the log of GDP per capita. *Cities* is the urban share of the population. *Schooling* is mean years of schooling of the adult population. *Child survival* is the under-five survival rate in percent, so its coefficient reads as the effect of a one-percentage-point improvement in the probability of reaching age five. *Paid employment* is the wage and salaried share of total employment, the closest cross-national analogue to the "wage labour" of the first variant.

### Estimation

All specifications are estimated by ordinary least squares on decade differences, with decade fixed effects and standard errors clustered at the country level; country fixed effects are absorbed by the differencing. The coefficient of interest is always on a baseline level of one of the two agricultural measures, and its interpretation is always the same: how much does a higher initial agricultural share shift the subsequent ten-year change in births? A negative coefficient supports the mechanisation story, since it means that countries starting a decade with more agriculture saw births fall further over the following ten years.

Specifications are built as a sequence rather than a single preferred model, and the sequence is reported in full. A negative result resting on one specification invites the objection that the author chose the wrong controls; a negative result that holds across the whole plausible sequence, including specifications that raise and reduce precision, is harder to dismiss. I therefore report the coefficient on the agricultural measure under each covariate set and report the full range of estimates rather than a selected one.

### What Would Have Counted as Support

Because this paper reports a failure, it owes the reader a statement of what success would have looked like, fixed before the results are reported. Four conditions were set.

First, *sign and precision*: the coefficient on baseline farm employment share should be negative and at least twice its standard error. If farm employment falling by 20 points were to account for a fall of five births per thousand, one would expect something on the order of −0.25—comfortably detectable.

Second, *stability*: the sign should not change across covariate sets, and the magnitude should not vary by more than a factor of two. A quantity whose coefficient changes sign when a control is added is not measuring a stable structural relationship.

Third, *direction*: the farm share should predict subsequent births better than births predict the subsequent farm share. This is the minimum requirement for a causal story running from agriculture to fertility.

Fourth, *mediation*: if the mechanism operates through urbanisation, schooling, or wage employment, the farm-share coefficient should shrink when those variables are added, and shrink most when the variable closest to the mechanism enters. A coefficient that grows when mediators enter is telling us the mediators were suppressing a relationship rather than carrying it.

None of the four conditions is met. I report them in that order.

## Results

### Farm Employment

Table 1 reports the coefficient on the baseline farm employment share in five specifications, each adding one covariate. The coefficient is never negative in the way the hypothesis requires and never statistically distinguishable from zero. With income controlled it is +0.0013 (SE = 0.0041); with cities, −0.0007 (0.0038); with schooling, +0.0051 (0.0054); with child survival, −0.0026 (0.0048); with paid employment, −0.0035 (0.0055). Across all specifications estimated, including those not tabulated, the coefficient ranges from +0.0013 to −0.0059.

The pattern is not that the coefficient is small. It is that the coefficient has no location. It changes sign three times across five specifications differing only in which control is included, and the sign changes are not accompanied by changes in precision: the standard error stays in a narrow band (0.0038 to 0.0055) while the point estimate moves across a band roughly three times as wide. If the farm employment share measured a stable structural relationship, adding a control correlated with development should move the estimate smoothly toward zero, or leave it alone. It should not drive it from positive to negative and back.

Condition 4 therefore fails cleanly. The mechanism claims that agricultural decline works *through* urbanisation, schooling, and wage employment; if so, controlling for those variables should attenuate the farm-share coefficient, since part of its association with fertility would be carried by the mediators. Instead the coefficient is largest and most positive with schooling held constant (+0.0051) and turns negative with paid employment held constant (−0.0035). The mediators are not carrying the relationship; they are competing with it, which is what one expects when the agricultural and non-agricultural variables are all proxies for the same underlying quantity—the level of development—rather than links in a chain.

### Farm Output

The farm output share tells a superficially more encouraging story, and its collapse is correspondingly more instructive (Table 2). Estimated alone, agriculture's share of value added carries a coefficient of +0.0128 (SE = 0.0030)—large, precise, and pointing the wrong way for the hypothesis: countries with a larger agricultural share of output saw births fall *less* over the following decade. Adding income and cities reduces it to +0.0081 (0.0028), still precise and still positive. Adding schooling and child survival turns it to −0.0050 (0.0041), which is the sign the hypothesis wants and which is not significant.

A coefficient that is significant, correctly signed, and stable would be evidence. A coefficient that travels from +0.0128 to −0.0050 as covariates accumulate is evidence only that the measure is not identified separately from the level of development. The reason is not mysterious: value added as a share of GDP is a ratio whose denominator moves with the price of everything else the country sells, so in a country with a large agricultural share a fall in the world price of its principal export raises the measured agricultural share without a single worker leaving the fields. The employment share is at least a count of people. The output share is a count of people divided by a price index, and the fact that it yields a bigger and prettier coefficient is a warning rather than a finding.

### The Horse Race

The last descriptive step puts all seven predictors on the table at once, on the sample where all seven are available: 1,899 country-decades covering 162 countries (Table 3, Figure 1). Two variables carry information. Child survival enters at +0.0067 (0.0018), small per percentage point but precisely estimated.

The substantive reading is that once a country's own prior fertility is in the model, little is left for structural variables to explain. Prior fertility is not a "cause" in any deep sense—it is the same variable measured ten years earlier, carrying everything unobserved that makes a country's fertility what it is: kinship systems, religion, the sexual division of labour, the history of birth-control diffusion. What Table 3 shows is that the structural variables add little to that summary. The fertility transition, in these data, looks less like a response to changes in the organisation of production and more like a process with strong country-specific momentum—roughly what Cleland and Wilson (1987) argued, and roughly what the diffusion literature has said for four decades.

## The Direction Problem

Everything reported so far tests the hypothesis in one direction: does a high farm share in a decade predict a larger fall in births over the following decade? A coefficient hovering around zero is compatible with two quite different situations—no relationship at all, or a relationship that runs the other way, so that the forward regression is contaminated by reverse causation and the estimates are pulled toward zero and beyond.

The panel allows a direct comparison. If the mechanisation story is right, the farm share at the start of a decade should carry more information about the subsequent change in births than the birth rate at the start of a decade carries about the subsequent change in the farm share. Estimating both directions on the same panel gives the opposite answer. The farm share predicting the ten-year change in births yields +0.0011 (SE = 0.0041): nothing. Births predicting the ten-year change in the farm employment share yields +1.536 (0.406): a coefficient nearly four times its standard error, and in the direction that says a country with a birth rate one point higher at the start of a decade had a farm employment share roughly 1.5 points higher ten years later.

**Figure 2**

*The Two Directions of the Farm–Fertility Relationship*

![Figure 2](figures/fig13_farm_direction.png)

*Note.* Left panel: coefficient on the baseline farm employment share predicting the ten-year change in births (*β* = +0.0011, *SE* = 0.0041, not significant). Right panel: coefficient on the baseline birth rate predicting the ten-year change in the farm employment share (*β* = +1.536, *SE* = 0.406, significant). Same estimator and fixed effects throughout. The asymmetry is the substantive finding: predictive content runs from fertility to farming, not from farming to fertility.

Two cautions belong before the interpretation rather than after it. First, this is not an identification strategy. Neither regression isolates an exogenous source of variation, and the reverse coefficient is as vulnerable to omitted variables as the forward one; a country with strong kinship pressure toward large families may also have institutions that keep labour on the land, in which case both coefficients reflect the same unobserved variable. What the asymmetry establishes is weaker and still useful: the data do not permit the claim that farming leads, because the lead the hypothesis requires is not in the record. Second, +1.536 is a level-on-level relationship in differences and should not be read as a structural multiplier. Its role here is diagnostic.

With those cautions in place, the substantive interpretation is the one the demography of agrarian economies suggests. High fertility keeps the supply of labour abundant and cheap, which keeps labour-intensive smallholding viable longer than it would otherwise be; large cohorts need land, and land is subdivided; the farm sector stays large because the people are there. When fertility falls, the labour surplus that everyone attributes to agricultural "push" is partly created: fewer young people are available to work the land, households reallocate daughters' time from farm and family labour to schooling and wage work, and the farm share falls from the supply side. Boppart et al. (2023) supply the theoretical complement, in which agricultural labour intensity and low productivity jointly shape the demand for children. The direction of causation in their model is not a one-way street from agriculture to fertility, and these results suggest the reverse lane carries most of the traffic.

## Prices

One test remains, and it comes closest to an exogenous shock. If agricultural decline pushes labour out of farming and that push lowers fertility, then a shock to the profitability of farming should move both: a rise in agricultural prices should retain labour in agriculture and, on the mechanisation logic, slow the fertility decline in farm-dependent countries. The FAO Food Price Index provides such a shock, and interacting it with a country's baseline farm dependence asks whether farm-dependent countries respond differently when world food prices move.

The samples cover 3,232 to 3,407 country-decades and 183 countries, depending on the outcome, since the paid-employment series is shorter than the farm-employment series. The results (Table 4, Figure 4) are large, precise, and uniform: farm employment share −0.668 (SE = 0.072), paid employment −0.712 (0.110), births −0.733 (0.045).

The signs are unambiguous and they run the wrong way for the hypothesis. A rise in world food prices makes farm-dependent countries shed farm employment *faster*, not slower; pushes them into paid employment *faster*; and lowers their birth rate *faster*. On the mechanisation story, higher agricultural prices should have protected the farm sector and slowed the transition. What the data show is a distress response: when food prices spike, the farm-dependent economy empties out, households scramble for cash work, and fertility falls. That is a story about crisis, in which agricultural exit and fertility decline are joint consequences of a shock rather than one causing the other.

One limitation no amount of precision can repair: the FAO index begins in 1990. By then the farm employment transition had largely finished across Latin America, East Asia, Southern Europe, and the high-income world, so the countries where the farm share was still large enough for an interaction to mean anything were, by construction, the laggards. The test is estimated on the tail of the process it is meant to explain. It describes how the last agrarian economies respond to price shocks, not how the first industrialising ones did. The results are consistent with the null and inconsistent with the mechanism, and that is all that can honestly be claimed.

## Discussion

Three readings of the null are available, and the data do not choose among them.

The first is that the null is true: agricultural decline is not a driver, and the observed correlation is produced by a common cause—development broadly, and the mortality decline that accompanies it. The strongest support for this reading is not the null on the farm share but the size of the coefficient on prior fertility (−0.644) and the persistence of child survival (+0.0067) after everything else is added. The variable that survives measures how many children are dying, not what people do for a living.

The child-survival coefficient needs a note, since its sign will puzzle readers who expect that saving children lowers fertility. With prior fertility held constant, higher survival predicts a *smaller* decline in births. That is not evidence that survival raises fertility; it is evidence of a lag. Survival improvements precede the recognition that fewer births are needed to reach a desired family size, and in a horse race that already controls for how high fertility started, the survival variable picks up the interval in which mortality has fallen and fertility has not yet responded—the lag Lloyd and Ivanov (1988) documented and Doepke (2005) formalised.

The second reading is that the measure is wrong. Farm *employment* is not farm *dispossession*. A country can lose its smallholders without any fall in agricultural employment: the consolidated estate hires the former smallholder as a labourer, and the employment share barely moves while the tenure distribution is transformed. It can also lose agricultural employment without dispossession, as rural households move into rural non-farm work while keeping their land. The two processes have opposite implications for the household economy of child-rearing—the first destroys the family enterprise, the second diversifies it—and the employment share averages them together. If the dispossession variant is the true mechanism, the test reported here is a test of something else.

The third reading is that the design is underpowered in the way that matters. Ten-year differences on national aggregates discard exactly the within-country variation in which the mechanism would operate: which households lost land, when, and what happened to their births. National averages may cancel a strong effect among the dispossessed against a weak or opposite effect among those who stayed.

What the paper does contribute is a demonstration that the easy version of the hypothesis—the version asserted in textbooks and introductions and treated as established—is not supported by the cross-national record. That version claims the farm share is a leading indicator of fertility. It is not. It is contemporaneous at best, and lagging at worst.

## Limitations

The measure of agricultural decline is an employment share built from modelled estimates rather than census microdata. It counts as agricultural employment the own-account and unpaid family labour that agricultural censuses record inconsistently and that, in most of the world, is disproportionately female. Women's farm work is the most undercounted labour in the world's statistics, and it is also the labour most directly implicated in the mechanism: the child-rearing cost that rises when women leave the farm is precisely the cost these data fail to see.

The outcome is the crude birth rate, which moves with age structure and migration as well as fertility. A country whose young adults emigrate records a falling birth rate with no change in children per woman. The direction test is especially exposed here, since emigration from agrarian economies is exactly the process that would generate a spurious association between high births and a persistently high farm share.

The covariate sequence is not a causal ordering, and the estimates are associations in differences. No instrument is used and no natural experiment exploited; the reverse-direction test compares predictive content rather than identifying anything. Coverage thins precisely where the mechanism should be strongest. The food-price exercise is confined to the post-1990 period, after the transition it is meant to illuminate had largely run its course. And the criteria for support were written down before the results were assembled but not preregistered; a reader who suspects they were fitted to the failure is entitled to that suspicion, and the full set of specifications is reported so the suspicion can be checked.

## Conclusion

The hypothesis was reasonable, it was tested, and the data refused it. Across 4,335 country-decades and 185 countries, the farm employment share does not predict the subsequent change in births; its coefficient moves across zero, changes sign, and never attains precision. The farm output share produces a significant coefficient in sparse specifications and loses it once schooling and child survival enter. In the seven-variable horse race the only precise predictors are a country's own prior fertility and child survival. And when the test is reversed, the farm share follows fertility with a coefficient of +1.536 (0.406) while fertility does not follow the farm share at all.

The correct summary is that farming follows. Agricultural decline is what happens to a society after its fertility has begun to fall, not what causes it to fall: fewer births mean fewer hands, and fewer hands mean a smaller farm sector. The mechanisation story may still be true of particular places at particular times, and the dispossession variant may still be true everywhere—but it has not been tested here, because the data that would test it, the distribution of land and tenure rather than the count of farm jobs, do not exist as a comparable cross-national series. Building that series is the next step. Until it is built, the confident version of the story should be told less confidently.
