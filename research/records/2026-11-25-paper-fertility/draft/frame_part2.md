### 8.2 What the gradient is a gradient of

The strongest temptation in this literature is to treat the between-country gradient as evidence that schooling
causes fertility to fall, and the strongest version of that claim is Caldwell's (1980): mass education is a
determinant of the timing of the fertility decline. The synthesis's verdict is that the gradient is real, global,
and steepest at the massification margin — and that it is a **development gradient**, not an identified schooling
effect. The placebo result is the reason: future schooling, which cannot cause current fertility, predicts it
about 90 per cent as well as current schooling does. That is what a shared development process looks like in a
cross-section of countries. The same process raises schooling and lowers fertility, and the level correlation is
the shadow it casts on two of its indicators. This reading is not a denial of the quantity–quality mechanism
(Becker & Lewis, 1973; Hanushek, 1992; Black, Devereux, & Salvanes, 2005); it is a statement about what the
between-country design can identify. The mechanism may well operate within families and within countries —
Section 4's survey-level gradient is consistent with it — but the cross-country level correlation cannot be
used as its evidence.

The synthesis also does not find support for the mortality-led insurance account as the *leading* driver of the
historical collapse: freezing the mortality decline removes only 3.2 per cent of the simulated fertility fall,
and the engine's development-only rival accounts for the rest at least as well out of sample. That does not
mean mortality is irrelevant to fertility; it means the historical collapse is schooling-led in the magnitudes
this project can compute, and the paper keeps the record's separate ordering result (mortality decline leads
fertility decline by about twelve years in the historical panel) as a description of timing, not as a
demonstration of the driver. The two statements coexist in the paper because they are about different things:
ordering is about when, the counterfactual is about how much.

### 8.3 Why the within-country null is the right answer to the wrong question

The change design is not a defective version of the levels design; it is a different design. By absorbing
country fixed effects and country trends it removes the very variation — the between-country development
differences — in which the gradient lives, and it asks whether a decade's extra schooling within a country is
followed by a faster fall. The honest answer from the project is that it is not, and in several measures the
opposite. That answer is compatible with a world in which the development gradient is entirely real: if
schooling and fertility are both moving parts of a slow development process, the short-run within-country
association between their increments can be weak, positive, or noisy, because the increments are driven by
different parts of the process (expansion of enrolment, marriage timing, economic cycles) and because a decade
is a short window for a quantity–quality mechanism to operate. The literature's mistake is not choosing one
design; it is interpreting the two designs' coefficients as the same parameter. Robinson's (1950) warning and
the panel-data literature's within-versus-between distinction (Nickell, 1981; Hausman & Taylor, 1981; Bell &
Jones, 2015) say the same thing in different vocabularies. What the project adds is the demonstration for this
relation, on one panel, with every specification reported.

### 8.4 Massification: what the threshold shape means

The threshold analysis is the synthesis's bridge between the cross-section and the transition. The gradient is
steepest in the first six years of schooling — the minimal-to-lower-secondary margin that the massification of
schooling traversed in the twentieth century — flattens between six and nine years, and reverses above nine.
That shape is what both the development-gradient reading and a quantity-quality reading predict; the first six
years of schooling are where the indicators of development diverge most, and the reversal at the top is the
well-known high-development J-shape, so the shape alone is not evidence for either story. It also means that "more schooling" has no constant meaning: a two-year increase at the bottom of the
distribution and a two-year increase at the top sit on different parts of the gradient. The massification of
schooling and the fertility transition overlapped at the bottom of the distribution, which is where the
association is largest; the twenty-first-century fertility transition is happening among highly schooled
populations, which is where the association is weakest and may reverse. Policy that reads "the cross-country
gradient is steep at six years" as "six years of schooling will lower fertility" is making the causal leap the
placebo forbids; policy that reads it as "the places where massification is happening are the places where the
transition is happening" is reading the evidence as it stands.

The survey-level results sharpen this. The DHS gradient is large and negative in every pipeline tercile, but it
is *shallower* where schooling is more massified (from −2.10 in the low-pipeline tercile to −1.60 in the high),
and it is *less* steep in later cohorts. Both patterns are consistent with composition — the label "secondary
education" selects differently as it spreads — but they are also a warning against reading the gradient as a
stable structural parameter. The massification margin is where the action is; it is also where the measurement
of "education" changes meaning fastest.

### 8.5 Where the paper stands against the literature

The closest claim-level competitor is **Caldwell (1980)**, who argued that mass education is a determinant of
the timing of the fertility decline. This paper agrees with Caldwell that the association is large and that
massification is central; it disagrees with the causal reading of the cross-country gradient, and it supplies
the placebo that makes the disagreement measurable. The closest empirical decomposition competitor is
**Kravdal (2002)**, who separated individual and community education effects in sub-Saharan Africa; our
transportability experiment is the country-level counterpart of his community-effects design, and our
contribution is to show that the between-country relation reproduces itself in arbitrary country groupings
while the within-country change relation does not. The closest methodological competitors are the
cross-country growth critiques of **Brock and Durlauf (2001)**, **Durlauf, Johnson, and Temple (2005)**, and
**Eberhardt and Teal (2011)**, who show that cross-country level and within-country change regressions can
disagree substantially; this paper applies their warning to a relation where the disagreement had been read as
a substantive puzzle rather than as a design artefact. On the survey side, **Cleland and Rodriguez (1988)**,
**Kravdal (2012)**, and the DHS-based literature establish the individual gradient that our survey-level
analysis corroborates and cannot replace. On the transition side, **Notestein (1945)**, **Davis (1945, 1963)**,
and **Lee (2003)** frame the demographic transition; **Galor and Weil (2000)** and **Doepke (2005)** provide the
mortality–education–fertility mechanisms the counterfactual tests. What is new is not any of these elements; it
is the coordinated application — the specification curve, the re-partition test, the quantified placebo, and the
synthesis of nulls — recorded so that each number can be checked.

### 8.6 Policy implications, stated descriptively

Three implications follow, and they are descriptive rather than prescriptive. First, **do not use the
cross-country gradient as an impact estimate**. The gradient is where countries sit, not what a schooling
programme does; the placebo result is the formal version of that caution. Second, **expect the association to
be strongest at the massification margin**. If a policy question is about the first six years of schooling, the
gradient says the cross-section association is largest there; it says nothing about the programme's causal
effect, which requires a design the cross-section cannot supply. Third, **the project's nulls matter for
policy too**: the care-work loop is absorbed by income and urbanisation, a strong no-off-switch is falsified
(only 34 of 396 country-episodes rebound, and the event study is far from significance), and the transition
model's individuation channels do not beat a development-only rival out of sample. Policies premised on a
self-reinforcing low-fertility trap or on care-work reform as the lever should be argued on other evidence; the
cross-country record assembled here does not support those premises.

## 9. Limitations

The synthesis's limits are as important as its findings, and they are stated here in the order in which a
referee would raise them.

**Observational designs throughout.** No workstream identifies a causal effect of schooling on fertility. The
levels workstream is a between-country description; the changes workstream is a within-country association; the
survey workstream is ecological; the transition workstream is a calibrated model, not an experiment. The
placebo result is the paper's own statement of the limit, not an appendix to it.

**Measurement.** "Years of schooling" and "secondary education" mean different things across countries and
cohorts, and the DHS cohorts show exactly the composition problem that this creates. The cross-country series
are harmonised reconstructions with documented assumptions; the enrolment and completion measures are not
identical objects; the gender-parity indices aggregate in ways that can hide distributional change. The
robustness of the levels result to all eight measures is reassuring for the sign, not for the magnitude.

**Multiple comparisons.** The paper reports large specification grids, and it does not apply a formal
multiple-testing correction to the counts of significant cells. The defence is procedural rather than
statistical: the grids were pre-registered, every cell is reported, and the headline claims are about
sign consistency across the grid rather than about any single cell's p-value. A reader who wants a family-wise
error rate for a cell-level claim will not find one here, and the paper does not pretend otherwise.

**Data gaps.** DHS individual microdata were not obtainable, so the individual-level test, the wealth
horse-race, the community placebo, and the quantum/tempo decomposition remain undone. The literature review's
search is documented but is not a PRISMA-style systematic review. The project's simulated transition engine is
calibrated, and its wide parameter intervals are an identification limit (Section 7), not a sample-size problem.
One ad-hoc levels-multiverse run that the project produced during the synthesis could not be re-verified because
the compute node went offline; it is not used for any number in this paper, and the published battery covers
the same ground.

**Generalisability.** The panel is 196 countries over 1960-2024, and the paper's claims are about that panel.
The transportability experiment says the level relation reproduces in arbitrary *subsets* of those countries;
it does not say it would reproduce in a different era or in a different measurement regime. The threshold
shape's above-nine-years reversal is estimated on the richest and most schooled countries and should not be
read as a general statement about tertiary expansion.

**Authorship of evidence.** This is a synthesis of work by several hands; each section carries its own record
and provenance, and the paper's voice is unified but the responsibility for a number is the record's. The
claim-to-source map in the record is the audit trail.

## 10. Conclusion

The level of analysis decides the answer. Estimated between countries, the schooling–fertility relation is one
of the most robust regularities in the social sciences: negative for all eight schooling measures, stable across
a 336-specification curve, and reproduced in 71–99 per cent of 13,800 arbitrary country groupings. Estimated
within countries on decade changes, the relation weakens, and for several measures it reverses: the decades in
which schooling expanded fastest are not the decades in which fertility fell fastest. Both estimates are
correct answers to different questions, and the literature's habit of reporting one as if it answered the
other's question is what produced the apparent contradiction that motivated this programme.

The paper's second conclusion is less comfortable for the winning side of that contrast. The between-country
gradient is not an identified schooling effect. A future-schooling placebo is about 90 per cent as strong as the
correct-time relation, which is what a development gradient looks like: schooling and fertility are both
consequences of a country's position in the development process, and the level correlation is the shadow that
process casts on two of its indicators. The gradient is therefore descriptive — real, global, and policy-relevant
as a description of where countries sit — and the paper says so in its abstract, its introduction, and here,
because the alternative is to let a descriptive regularity be cited as a causal lever it is not.

The synthesis's third conclusion is about method. Pre-registered specification curves, re-partition
transportability tests, placebos, and the discipline of reporting nulls are not exotic additions to
demographic practice; they are what is needed to make a large observational literature self-correcting. The
project's nulls — the care-work loop absorbed by income, the falsified strong no-off-switch, the absent tipping
point, the mortality counterfactual that removes 3.2 per cent, the tolerance ladder that is an identification
limit — are part of the paper's contribution, not a failure to find something to say. What survives is a
development gradient at the massification margin; what fails is the causal reading of it; what cannot be tested
with these data is the individual-level mechanism, and the paper names the data that would test it. That is a
smaller set of claims than the literature usually offers, and it is the set the evidence supports.

**Survives.** A large, global, between-country schooling–fertility gradient (all eight measures; 71–99% of

(−0.326 [−0.405, −0.248]) and reversing above nine (+0.112 [+0.057, +0.171]); a survey-level DHS gradient

community placebo; a causal effect of schooling on fertility without an exogenous schooling shift.
