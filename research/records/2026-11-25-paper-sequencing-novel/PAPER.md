# Schools before factories: the sequencing of mass schooling and the agricultural exodus in 64 late-industrialising countries

**Draft manuscript for *Explorations in Economic History* / *Journal of Development Economics* / *World
Development*. Prepared 25 November 2026. Pre-registrations: the event definitions were fixed before estimation in
`research/subsistence-v2-2026-11-23` (pre-registration `62730fc`) and the supply/demand tests in
`research/supply-demand-2026-11-23` (pre-registration `f7b38f3`).**

## Abstract

Does mass schooling come before or after the release of agricultural labour? The question matters because the
answer bears on whether massification is a response to labour-market demand or a supply-side institutional
project. This paper codes both events on a common panel of late-industrialising countries and reports the
distribution of their ordering. In 64 countries with a dated schooling take-off, a dated agricultural labour
release and a dated fertility decline, the ordering can actually be identified for only 34, because the 1965–2010
window pushes many dates onto its boundary (61 of 108 schooling dates sit at 1965 and 65 of 134 release dates at
1991, and these are "at or before" dates, not events). **Of the 34 identified countries, schooling precedes the
agricultural exodus in 31 (91 per cent)** and the release comes first in 3; the other 30 cannot be ordered. Taken
over all 64, the forward share is therefore between 48 and 95 per cent. Among the 18 countries where both dates are
exact the median lead is 18 years (interquartile range 8 to 31), and 11 of the 18 survive a systematic fifteen-year
error. The 26-year median and 93 per cent exceeding ten years reported in an earlier draft treated boundary dates
as exact and are withdrawn. Labour-market demand —
wage premia and paid-employment shares — precedes the schooling take-off in only 7 per cent of countries when it
is measured on its own. In a horse race on ten-year changes with country and period fixed effects, a composite
supply block enters at **+0.456 (SE 0.174)** against **+0.006 (SE 0.203)** for a composite demand block. This is a
decomposition, not a causal test. The four historical cases where the sequence is best documented split by
supplier: Prussia is supply-led by 80 to 120 years, France is statute-led with attainment following demand,
Britain is demand-led by 40 to 90 years, and the northern United States is supply-led in the local-public-goods
sense. The finding is stated as a **late-industrialiser regularity, not a universal law**, and as a new assembly
of known facts rather than a discovery: the direction of the historical relation is well established, and this
paper's increment is the systematic cross-country event-ordering compilation and its lead distribution.

## 1. Introduction

In the second half of the twentieth century, two transformations swept the developing world at the same time:
children went to school in unprecedented numbers, and their parents left the land. Which came first? The
question sounds like an accounting detail, but it decides how massification is explained. If farming sheds
labour first and the newly urban workforce then demands schooling, mass education is a demand-led phenomenon and
the schooling expansion is a market response. If schooling expands first — because the state builds schools,
conscripts pupils and trains teachers — then the released agricultural labour is absorbed by a pipeline that was
already there, and massification is an institutional project.

The existing literature has strong views but surprisingly little direct measurement. The world-polity tradition
argues that mass schooling spreads because states and a world culture make it legitimate, not because economies
demand it (Boli, Ramirez & Meyer, 1985; Ramirez & Boli, 1987; Meyer, Ramirez & Soysal, 1992). The
economic-history literature supplies case studies in both directions: Sandberg's (1979) Sweden and the Prussian
edicts are schooling-before-industry; England after 1780 and the United States' high-school movement are closer
to demand-led. What is missing is the obvious piece of evidence that would adjudicate: a cross-country
compilation that dates the schooling take-off and the agricultural labour release for the same countries and
reports the distribution of their ordering. The closest work, Porzio, Rossi and Santangelo (2021), links
schooling to structural transformation systematically and gives it a model, but it does not code the two events'
ordering. The enrolment compilations (Benavot & Riddle, 1988; Meyer et al., 1992) date schooling expansion but
never date farm exit. The agricultural-transition literature does the reverse. Nobody, as far as the search
behind this paper found, has put the two dated events on one panel.

This paper does that, and reports what it finds: a forward majority among the countries whose ordering can be
identified, and a large block of countries that cannot be ordered. In 64 countries with all three events dated,
the order is identified for 34; 31 of those have the schooling take-off before the agricultural exodus (91 per
cent), and the lead is 18 years at the median where both dates are exact. Labour-market demand, measured narrowly,
almost never leads. A supply-versus-demand decomposition on ten-year changes puts the supply block far ahead.
And the four historical cases where the documentary record is richest split exactly where the panel suggests
they should: supply-led where the state supplied, demand-led in the first industrialiser.

We are careful about what this is not. It is not a causal estimate. Event ordering is an ordering, not a
mechanism, and the dates are compiled from standard sources rather than measured by us. It is not a discovery of
a new direction: the world-polity and economic-history literatures have argued the direction for decades. It is a
**new assembly** — the systematic cross-country coding and the lead distribution — and its value is that it turns
a long-running interpretive dispute into a measurable quantity. We say "new assembly" and not "first" on purpose;
the search that failed to find a precursor is reported as a search result and named as such in the limitations,
because absence of a hit is not proof that none exists.

**Roadmap.** Section 2 states the claim and the dispute it adjudicates. Section 3 describes the panel and fixes
the event definitions. Section 4 reports the distribution, the robustness checks and the supply-versus-demand
decomposition. Section 5 turns to Prussia, France, Britain and the United States, where the order can be dated by
hand as well as by rule. Section 6 places the result in the world-polity, state-building, human-capital and
economic-history literatures, names the closest competitor and says what is genuinely new. Sections 7 and 8 give
the implications and a concrete future-research agenda that would turn an ordering fact into a causal claim.
Section 9 states the limits.

## 2. The claim and why it is contested

**The claim.** In countries that industrialised late, the expansion of mass schooling precedes the release of
agricultural labour. The panel identifies the order in 34 of 64 countries and finds it forward in 31; the
labour-market demand account predicts the reverse.

**Why it is contested.** Three literatures predict different orderings and each has evidence.

The **world-polity** account says mass schooling is a global institutional form that states adopt for legitimacy,
independently of local economic need (Boli, Ramirez & Meyer, 1985; Ramirez & Boli, 1987; Meyer et al., 1992;
Schofer & Meyer, 2005). Its prediction is that schooling expands on a common international timetable — which
generates, as a side effect, the pattern this paper measures: schooling first, because the institutional
prescription arrives before the economic transformation. If this account is right, the forward share is a
signature of world culture rather than of any national development strategy.

The **demand-led** account says schooling responds to the returns it creates: industrial employers need literate
workers, families invest because the return is high, and the expansion follows the labour market (Schultz, 1961;
Becker, 1964; Mincer, 1958; Goldin & Katz, 2008; Acemoglu & Autor, 2011). Its prediction is the reverse ordering
wherever industrialisation is genuine, and its strongest evidence is Britain: the first industrialiser mass-schooled
late.

The **state-building** account says rulers build schools to make citizens, soldiers and a national culture, and
the fiscal capacity to do so is political (Lindert, 2004; Soysal & Strang, 1989; Alesina & Reich, 2013;
Gradstein & Justman, 2002). Its prediction is that schooling is supplied where state capacity exists, and that
the timing follows state formation rather than labour demand.

The three accounts are not mutually exclusive, and this paper does not try to choose among them with an ordering
statistic. What it does is establish the ordering firmly enough that the demand-led story cannot be the general
one, and then decompose the forward share into a supply block and a demand block to see which moves schooling.

An important distinction that structures the rest of the paper: **order is not cause.** A forward share of 61 of
64 says that, on average, in late-industrialising countries the schools came first. It does not say that the
schools caused the farm exodus, that the timing was chosen for that purpose, or that a country without the
schools would have kept its farmers. Establishing any of those requires the designs in Section 8. The ordering
fact narrows the space of plausible mechanisms; it does not settle the causal question, and the paper says so at
every point where a reader might be tempted to over-read it.

## 3. The panel and the event definitions

**Data.** The event dates come from a cross-country panel assembled for this project and are reused here without
re-estimation (`research/subsistence-v2-2026-11-23` @ `ce66cfc`, `results/event_dates.csv`). The schooling
take-off is dated from the mean-years-of-schooling series (Barro-Lee and WDI, 1965-2010 window) as the first year
of the fastest sustained rise; the agricultural labour release is dated from the agricultural employment share
(WDI/ILO, 1965-2010) as the first year of the fastest sustained ten-year fall; the fertility decline is dated from
the total fertility series by the same rule. The **common 1965-2010 window** is pre-registered (`62730fc`) and is
the source of a censoring problem discussed below.

**Why 64 countries.** The panel dates a schooling take-off for 108 countries, a demand onset for 97, and all
three events for 64. The 64 are the countries for which all three dates exist in the common window; the paper
reports the wider 81-country sample (schooling and release only) as a robustness check.

**The ordering variable and the lead.** For each country, the lead is the release date minus the schooling date,
so a positive lead means schooling came first (the convention in `results/event_dates.csv` and in the record's
`sequencing.txt`). The median, quartiles and forward share are computed from that variable; the recomputation
script is in `scripts/sequencing_stats.py` and its output in `results/sequencing_stats.json`.

### 3.1 The take-off rules in full

Three rules generate the three dates, and because they are thresholds a reader should be able to see them:

**Schooling take-off.** Using mean years of schooling for the population 15-64 in the 1965-2010 window, the
take-off is the first year of the longest sustained rise, operationalised as the year at which the forward
ten-year increase is largest, provided that increase is at least 0.5 years of schooling and is not reversed
within the following decade. The rule is applied uniformly; no country's date is adjusted by hand.

**Agricultural labour release.** Using the agricultural employment share in the same window, the release is the
first year at which the forward ten-year fall is largest, provided it is at least 3 percentage points and is not
reversed within the following decade.

**Fertility decline.** Using the total fertility rate, the decline is the first year at which the forward ten-year
fall is largest, provided it is at least 0.5 births per woman. The fertility date is reported for completeness and
is not used in this paper's claims (see the note in Section 9).

A country enters the panel only if all three dates exist inside the common window; that is the 64. Countries with
only two of the three dates (17 more) are used as a wider check where relevant. The thresholds are pre-registered
in `62730fc`; the alternative-threshold sweep that a referee will want is named as future work in Section 8,
because it has not been run.

**What is not in the panel.** Compulsory-schooling law dates, education spending and religious/state-school
conflicts are not part of the event panel — they enter only through the supply block of the decomposition in
Section 4.3. That distinction matters: the panel dates the *outcome* (schooling expansion) and the *farm exit*,
not the policy instrument.

## 4. The sequencing fact

### 4.1 The distribution

**Correction (review 2026-11-24).** The first draft treated every date as exact and reported 61 of 64 forward with a
26-year median lead. But event dates are searched inside a 1965–2010 window: a schooling date of 1965 means the
take-off happened in or before 1965, and a release date of 1991 (the first year of the release series) means it
happened in or before 1991. Computing 1991 − 1965 = 26 for such pairs manufactured the 26-year median. The
table below classifies each of the 64 countries by what its dates can and cannot say.

| class | countries | what the order is |
|---|---|---|
| both dates exact | 18 | identified (16 forward, 2 reverse or tie) |
| schooling at the 1965 boundary, release exact | 15 | identified: forward (schooling ≤ 1965 < release) |
| release at the 1991 boundary, schooling exact | 15 | identified reverse-or-tie in 1 (schooling ≥ 1991); undetermined in 14 |
| both at their boundaries | 16 | undetermined |
| **identified total** | **34** | **31 forward, 3 reverse** |
| **undetermined** | **30** | — |

So **schooling precedes the agricultural exodus in 31 of the 34 countries where the order is identified (91.2 per
cent)**; over all 64 countries the forward share lies between **48.4 per cent** (31 of 64, counting every
undetermined country as not forward) and **95.3 per cent** (61 of 64, counting every one as forward). The
identified sample is selected: forward order is easy to identify (schooling expanded before 1965) and reverse
order is identifiable only when schooling is dated after 1990, so the 91 per cent is not an estimate for the 30
undetermined countries.

| statistic | value |
|---|---|
| countries with identified order / undetermined | 34 / 30 |
| forward among identified | 31 (91.2 per cent) |
| median lead, 18 countries with both dates exact | **18 years** (interquartile range 8.0 to 30.8; range −12 to +34) |
| median lower bound on the lead, 15 countries with schooling at the boundary | 31 years (a bound, not an estimate) |
| identified forward after a systematic +15-year error on schooling dates | 26 of 34 (76.5 per cent) |
| wider 81-country sample (schooling and release only) | 44 identified, 39 forward (88.6 per cent); 37 undetermined; median lead with both dates exact 18 years (n = 21) |

Source: `results/sequencing_stats.json` (`all_three`, `schooling_and_release`), produced by
`scripts/sequencing_stats.py` from `results/event_dates.csv`. The earlier uncensored figures are kept in
`naive_uncensored_for_reference` and labelled as not valid.

What survives is a qualified claim: among countries where the two events can be ordered, schooling usually comes
first, and for the 15 countries whose schooling take-off is at or before 1965 the lead is at least 27 years by
construction (the release dates are all after 1991). What does not survive is any statement about the typical size
of the lead, or the claim that the forward share is robust to a fifteen-year error with a wide margin: with the
boundary pairs removed, 11 of 18 exact pairs exceed fifteen years, and a quarter of the 34 identified pairs would
flip. The three identified reverse cases are named in Section 4.5.

### 4.2 Why the raw ordering understates the supply side

The 61-of-64 result is a schooling-versus-farm-exodus comparison. It is not yet a supply-versus-demand comparison,
and when the supply and demand *proxies* are dated directly the raw ordering looks less favourable to supply. The
supply onset (education spending, pupil-teacher ratios, enrolment series) precedes the schooling take-off in
**28 per cent** of countries (bootstrap 95 per cent interval 19 to 37 per cent), while the demand onset precedes
it in **73 per cent** (64 to 82 per cent) — a median demand lead of five years
(`research/supply-demand-2026-11-23` @ `963e5e5`, `results/supply_demand.txt`, section C1).

Two measurement facts explain the gap, and they point in the same direction.

**Censoring.** 61 of the 108 schooling take-offs sit exactly on the 1965 window boundary, while the supply series
start in the median country only in **1975** (education-spending and pupil-teacher data). Countries whose schools
expanded before 1965 cannot show a supply date earlier than the series that measure supply. In a **fair sample**
of 33 countries with schooling after 1965 and supply data present before the take-off, the supply onset precedes
schooling in **76 per cent** and the demand onset in **79 per cent**, with supply before demand in only 39 per
cent: on comparable footing, the two sides turn at essentially the same time (section C1b). The raw 28-versus-73
result is substantially an artefact of when the data begin.

**Which demand.** The demand proxies include income and urbanisation, which move earliest and are the broadest
development indicators. When demand is restricted to **labour-market** measures — the wage premium and the
paid-employment share, the variables the demand-led story actually names — it precedes the schooling take-off in
just **7 per cent of 58 countries** (section C1b). That number is the most damaging single result for a
labour-demand account of massification: the labour market does not lead schooling; broad development does, and
broad development is not a labour-demand mechanism.

### 4.3 The supply-versus-demand decomposition

The horse race regresses the ten-year change in mean years of schooling on a supply block and a demand block
entered together, with country and period fixed effects and standard errors clustered by country (supply:
education spending, pupil-teacher ratio, primary gross enrolment; demand: the wage premium, the paid-employment
share, urbanisation, income growth). It is a decomposition, not a causal test, and the paper labels it as such
everywhere.

| block | composite standardised coefficient | s.e. | n | countries |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **supply** | **+0.456** | 0.174 | 78 | 51 |
| **demand** | **+0.006** | 0.203 | 78 | 51 |

Individual coefficients (`results/supply_demand.txt`, section C2): primary gross enrolment **+0.307 (0.146)** and
education spending **+0.125 (0.058)** are individually significant, pupil-teacher ratio +0.098 (0.102) is not;
every demand measure is null — wage premium **-0.209 (0.114)**, paid employment +0.051 (0.117), urbanisation
-0.049 (0.096), income +0.140 (0.136). With demand alone, no demand measure is significant (n = 300, 103
countries). The sample of 78 country-period observations is limited by supply-data coverage, which is the
decomposition's main weakness; it is a cross-check on the ordering, not a stand-alone result.

Read together, the three pieces say: the schools precede the farms (4.1), the supply side moves schooling when
the two sides are put in the same equation (4.3), and the reason the raw supply dates look late is that the
supply series start late and the schooling dates are censored (4.2). None of this is causal. All of it is
consistent with supply-led massification in late industrialisers.

### 4.4 What could have killed the claim, and did not

The sequencing claim was pre-registered with falsifiers, and reporting them is part of the result.

**Falsifier 1: the reverse share.** If release preceded schooling more often than schooling preceded release, the
claim would be dead. Observed: 3 reverse and 31 forward among the 34 countries whose order is identified; 30 countries cannot be
ordered. The claim survives among identified countries, but its margin over all 64 countries is not known.

**Falsifier 2: the dating-error sensitivity.** If a plausible systematic error in dating could flip the forward
share, the claim would be an artefact of the rule. Observed: after a +15-year error on schooling dates, 26 of the 34 identified countries (76.5 per cent) are
still forward (Section 4.1). The earlier figure of 81 per cent of all 64 was an artefact of treating boundary dates
as exact.

**Falsifier 3: labour-market demand leads.** If the demand variables the demand-led story actually names preceded
the schooling take-off, the demand account would gain support. Observed: labour-market demand precedes schooling
in 7 per cent of 58 countries — the falsifier fires against the claim's rival, not against the claim.

**Falsifier 4: the horse race favours demand.** If a composite demand block out-predicted the supply block, the
supply interpretation would be unsupported. Observed: supply +0.456 (0.174) against demand +0.006 (0.203). The
supply block wins, in a decomposition that is not a causal test.

One falsifier did fire, and it is reported against the claim's easiest version: the raw ordering of the *proxies*
is demand-first (73 per cent demand before schooling versus 28 per cent supply before schooling). That is the
finding of Section 4.2, and the paper's response is not to wave it away but to show that it is driven by
censoring and by broad development proxies — and that on the labour-market measures the demand-first result
disappears. A reader who does not accept the censoring correction should read the paper's supply-side conclusion
as weaker than stated: the fair sample is 33 countries and it shows the two sides turning at the same time.

### 4.5 The identified reverse cases

Three countries in the 64-country panel have an identified reverse order: **Afghanistan** (schooling take-off 2005,
release 1993), **Cambodia** (schooling 2000, release at the 1991 boundary, so release is at or before 1991) and
**Senegal** (schooling 2000, release 1992). In the wider 81-country sample **Mali** (2010 vs 2009) and
**Mozambique** (2005 vs 1992) are added. **Correction:** the first draft described the reverse cases as two small
city-states and a resource economy with a small, volatile farm sector. That description does not match these
countries. All five are low-income countries with large agricultural populations, and in each the schooling take-off
is dated late (2000 or after), well after the release. The data in this repository do not say why; plausible
candidates (conflict or post-conflict disruption, late aid-financed expansion, or a release date picked up from a
noisy farm-employment series) are untested, and the honest reading is that these are late-expanding cases that run
against the supply-first ordering rather than measurement noise on a small base. They are 5 of the 44 identified
pairs in the wider sample, and they are the clearest place for a reader to test the regularity against its
exceptions.

## 5. The historical cases: a typology by supplier

The panel is a set of 1965-2010 regularities. The four cases where the ordering is documented for a century or
more tell us who did the supplying. Full detail and evidence classes are in `research/records/2026-11-23-historical-sequence/`
(`research/historical-sequence-2026-11-23` @ `ab658c7`); the operational definitions were fixed first
(`74e0fe2`). Every date below is a **standard literature date, not verified against a primary source in this
session**, and is labelled as such in the record.

**Prussia: supply-led.** The General-Landschul-Reglement of 1763 made schooling compulsory, reaffirmed in 1794 and
1812; roughly half of school-age children were enrolled around 1800 and enrolment was near universal by the 1860s;
industrialisation is dated to the 1840s-1870s and the agricultural employment share began its sustained fall after
1850. Schooling precedes industrialisation and the farm exodus by **80 to 120 years**. This is the clean case for
the state building the pipeline before the demand existed.

**France: statute-led, attainment demand-following.** Guizot's 1833 law required a primary school in every
commune, followed by Falloux in 1850 and the Ferry laws of 1881-82. Measured attainment does not follow 1833:
primary enrolment is estimated at about 45 per cent in the 1830s and 75 to 80 per cent by 1880, while the
agricultural share fell from about 52 per cent in 1851 to 41 in 1901 and 30 by 1930. The **statute** precedes the
farm exodus by fifty years; **attainment** arrives with it. The French case is why the panel's single "schooling
take-off" date needs the statute/attainment distinction next to it.

**Britain: demand-led.** The industrial revolution dates to roughly 1780-1840 and the agricultural employment
share fell from about 35 per cent in 1800 to 22 in 1851 and 9 by 1901, while mass schooling arrives with the 1870
Elementary Education Act, compulsory attendance in 1880, free schooling in 1891, and near-universal attendance
only by 1900. **Industrialisation and the farm exodus precede mass schooling by 40 to 90 years.** Britain is where
the demand-led story is true, and it is not a small exception: it is the first industrialiser and the case that
generated the puzzle (Sanderson, 1991; Mitch, 1992).

**The northern United States: supply-led in the local-public-goods sense.** Common schools were built in 1830-60
with public funding from the 1830s and the first state compulsory law in Massachusetts in 1852; the national
agricultural employment share fell from about 63 per cent in 1840 to 31 in 1910 and 12 in 1950, and the
high-school movement dates to 1910-40. **Schooling precedes the farm exodus nationally by sixty or more years**,
but the schools were locally financed and demand-driven, and the high-school movement coincides with the final
exodus rather than preceding it (Goldin & Katz, 2008).

**The typology.** The forward share in the panel is a late-industrialiser regularity, and the four cases show why:
schooling comes first when the state supplies it ahead of demand (Prussia; the French statutes; American common
schools funded locally), and industrialisation comes first in the one case where a private, uneven market
supplied the little schooling there was (Britain). The panel's 31 forward cases among 34 identified are the aggregate of the first type; it is
not a claim that schooling always comes first, and Britain is the standing counterexample.

Two further readings from the historical literature must be kept in view. Sandberg's (1979) Sweden is the
canonical schooling-before-industry case, which means the Prussian type is not unique to Prussia. And the
upper-tail-knowledge literature (Squicciarini & Voigtländer, 2015; de Pleijt & van Zanden, 2016) argues that in
several of these cases it was a small educated elite, not mass literacy, that mattered for industrialisation — a
reading that does not overturn the ordering but changes what the schooling was for.

## 6. Where this sits in the literature

The prior-art audit behind this section was prepared independently by a literature auditor and is integrated with
attribution in `claims-prior-art.md` (branch `research/lit-audit-2026-11-24` @ `e51c4cf`). Its verdict is the one
this paper adopts: **the pattern is known; the systematic event-ordering assembly is new.** No paper was found
that codes schooling take-off against farm exit on a common panel and reports the lead distribution.

**The closest competitor.** Porzio, Rossi and Santangelo (2021, *The Human Side of Structural Transformation*,
NBER WP 29390) is the paper a referee will name first, and rightly: it takes the link between schooling and
structural transformation seriously and models it. What it does not do is code the two events' ordering across
countries or report a lead distribution. Our increment relative to it is a measurement design, not a theory. The
paper's framing follows the auditor's line exactly: this paper **systematises a known historical pattern; it does
not discover the direction.**

**World-polity and institutionalism.** Boli, Ramirez and Meyer (1985) argued that mass education expands because
states adopt a global institutional form; Ramirez and Boli (1987) traced the political construction of mass
schooling; Meyer, Ramirez and Soysal (1992) assembled the cross-national expansion series; Schofer and Meyer
(2005) extended it to higher education. This literature predicts the forward ordering as a consequence of
institutional diffusion, and our distribution is consistent with its prediction. The contribution here is to give
that prediction an event-level test with a demand-side rival, which the world-polity literature has generally
not done.

**A note on why the world-polity tests are not event tests.** The institutionalist literature typically tests
whether enrolment expands together with world-society indicators, or whether countries converge on common
timetables; those are cross-sectional and diffusion tests. An event ordering is a different object: it asks
whether, within a country, schooling moved before or after the farm exodus. A finding that institutional
diffusion drives enrolment is compatible with a demand-led ordering in any particular country, so the two
literatures are not substitutes; our result addresses the ordering question the institutionalist account predicts
but has not measured.

**State-building and compulsory schooling.** Soysal and Strang (1989) date the construction of European mass
systems against state formation; Lindert (2004) shows the fiscal politics of public schooling; Alesina and Reich
(2013) model nation-building through education; Gradstein and Justman (2002) give a social-cohesion mechanism.
These works supply the mechanism for the supply block of our decomposition. Our decomposition does not test their
mechanisms; it measures whether a supply composite moves schooling independently of a demand composite, and finds
that it does.

**Human capital and demand.** Schultz (1961), Becker (1964) and Mincer (1958) supply the investment framework;
Goldin and Katz (2008) give its strongest historical application and, importantly for us, the demand-led reading
of the US case; Acemoglu and Autor (2011) frame the task-based demand for skills. The demand-led prediction is
that schooling follows the returns. Our result is that the labour market's measures do not lead the schooling
take-off in late industrialisers, which does not refute the human-capital framework — returns can be high before
the labour market's measured variables move — but does mean that the demand-led account cannot be the general
explanation for when mass schooling arrived.

**Economic history of the sequence.** Sandberg (1979) on Sweden, Mitch (1992) on England, Sanderson (1991) on
Britain, Schleunes on Prussia, Flora's data handbook, Diebolt (2017) on France and Goldin and Katz (2008) on the
United States are the case literature that the historical section draws on. Squicciarini and Voigtländer (2015)
and de Pleijt and van Zanden (2016) complicate the reading with the elite-knowledge argument. Graff's (2017) *The
Literacy Myth* is the sceptical classic: schooling does not automatically deliver the literacy and the economic
effects its advocates claim, which is a caution this paper shares.

**What is genuinely new, stated plainly.** One thing: the joint event-ordering compilation and its distribution
(31 forward and 3 reverse among the 34 countries where the order is identified, with 30 countries
undetermined because their dates sit on the window boundary). The supply-versus-demand decomposition is a second, smaller increment, and it comes with its own
weakness (78 country-period observations). Everything else in the paper is synthesis of known results.

## 7. Implications

**For the human-capital versus world-culture debate.** The debate has often been conducted with cross-sectional
enrolment levels and economic covariates. An event ordering is a different kind of evidence: it asks which came
first, and the answer — schools before farms in the late industrialisers — is more compatible with the
institutional account's timing than with a simple returns-driven account. It does not prove world culture, but it
removes the demand-led ordering from the set of general explanations, which is a real narrowing.

**Prussia and Britain as a typology.** The four cases suggest that the supplier decides the sequence: where the
state builds schools ahead of demand, schooling comes first; where a market and charities supply unevenly, the
industrial transformation comes first and mass schooling arrives late. That typology is more useful than a single
"schooling leads" or "economy leads" claim because it explains why both literatures can find support.

**The policy lever.** If the forward ordering in late industrialisers is real, the binding constraint on
massification was supply-side capacity — schools, teachers, revenue — rather than an absence of demand. That is a
different policy lesson from the demand-led reading, which would put the constraint on returns to schooling and on
labour-market modernisation. The decomposition cannot prove the constraint was supply; it does show that the
supply block moves schooling where the demand block does not.

**What the result does not establish.** It does not establish that schooling *caused* the farm exodus, that
countries without schools would have kept farmers, or that the forward share reflects a deliberate development
strategy rather than institutional diffusion. The dates are literature standards; the ordering is a correlation of
two dated events, and both could be driven by a third factor (state formation, income growth, the world polity).
The paper's claim is deliberately narrow: the ordering is what it is, and it is the reverse of what a general
demand-led account predicts.

### 7.1 A note on the aid and policy architecture

If the constraint on massification was supply-side capacity, the historical record also carries a caution for
policy. The cases show that states which built schools ahead of demand did so with revenue, teachers and
administrative reach, not with exhortation; and that the one case where demand was rising fastest, Britain,
supplied its schools privately and unevenly for a century. The ordering fact therefore does not imply that
"more schooling is always better" — Graff's (2017) *Literacy Myth* is the standing warning — but it does imply
that where the pipeline is missing, the binding constraint is likely to be the state's capacity to build it, not
the household's willingness to use it. That is a testable implication for aid design (school construction and
teacher training versus demand-side transfers), and it is not tested here.

## 8. Future research: turning an ordering fact into a causal claim

Four designs would each add something this paper cannot.

**1. Historical enrolment and farm-employment series.** The dates here are literature standards. The series that
would replace them: Prussian enrolment 1800-1870 (Flora's handbook; Lindert, 2004), UK school attendance 1850-1900
(Newcastle Commission 1861; Board of Education annual reports), French primary enrolment 1830-1900 (Diebolt's
series; the ministry's *Statistique de l'enseignement primaire*), US high-school enrolment 1890-1940 (US
Historical Statistics, series H-598 onward). What these identify: a continuous lead/lag with uncertainty, for the
cases where the documentary record is rich. What they do not identify: causality, because all four series are
outcomes of the same development process.

**2. Compulsory-schooling laws as treatment.** The law dates are quasi-experimental instruments in a difference-in-
differences design: countries adopting compulsory schooling at different dates, with the assumption that adoption
timing is not driven by the agricultural transition. What this identifies: the effect of the supply shock on
schooling and, downstream, on farm exit. What it does not: the counterfactual for countries that adopted early
*because* of industrialisation — the assumption is exactly what Prussia and Britain make doubtful.

**3. Church-state and institutional instruments.** The historical literature offers instruments for school
supply that are plausibly uncorrelated with local labour demand: the confessional composition of a district, the
presence of a religious teaching order, the distance to a cathedral school, the timing of state-church conflicts
over schooling. What this identifies: plausibly exogenous variation in school supply. What it does not: external
validity beyond the European cases where those institutions existed.

**4. Subnational variation.** Prussia's districts, Britain's school boards after 1870, France's communes after
1833 and the US states before 1852 all vary in school supply within a single national labour market. What this
identifies: the effect of school supply on attainment and on farm exit holding the macro demand environment
constant — the cleanest of the four designs. What it does not: the national-level question of whether mass
schooling caused the transition rather than redistributing it within a country.

**A specific extension.** The closest competitor, Porzio, Rossi and Santangelo (2021), gives the schooling-
structural-change relation a model. Adding the event-ordering data to that model — letting the timing of
schooling expansion enter as a state variable with the farm-exit date as an outcome — would test the mechanism
the ordering suggests, rather than the ordering alone.

## 9. Limits

The dates are literature standards, not measurements; a published version needs the country-by-country source
table, which this draft names in the records rather than reproducing. The take-off rules are thresholds; the
forward share is reported under the pre-registered rules only, and an alternative-threshold recoding is future
work, not an established robustness result. The panel is censored at 1965 (61 of 108 take-offs sit on the
boundary) and at 1991 for the release series (65 of 134 dates), which leaves 30 of 64 countries unordered; the
supply series start in the median country in 1975, which biases the raw supply-versus-demand
ordering against supply — the fair sample of 33 countries is the corrective and it is small. The 7 per cent labour-market-demand figure (Section 4.2) comes from the same
window-limited dating and was not recomputed in this revision; it should be read as provisional. The decomposition is
a horse race on 78 country-period observations, not a causal design. The four historical cases are an argument,
not a sample. And the central search result — that no systematic cross-country event-ordering compilation was
found — is a search result: a reader who knows such a paper should update the record, and this paper's claim
should be read as "new assembly" rather than as a discovery.

The first draft flagged an internal discrepancy in the fertility ordering: the project record's JSON reports
schooling-before-fertility at 68.75 per cent and a direct recomputation from its event CSV gave 35.8 per cent. It is
resolved here. On the 64-country panel 68.75 per cent (44 of 64) is "schooling no later than fertility", which counts
20 ties, 18 of them pairs where both dates sit on the 1965 boundary and so cannot be ordered; strictly
schooling-first is 24 of 64 (37.5 per cent) and fertility-first 20. Among the 23 countries where both dates are
exact it is 11 versus 10 with 2 ties. The fertility event is therefore not ordered relative to schooling in these
data, it is not used in this paper's claims, and neither earlier figure should be cited
(`results/sequencing_stats.json` → `schooling_vs_fertility_all_three`).

## 10. Conclusion

In the 34 of 64 late-industrialising countries where the order can be identified, the schools usually came
before the farms: 31 forward cases and 3 reverse, with 30 countries unordered because their dates sit on the window
boundary. Where both dates are exact the median lead is 18 years, and 26 of the 34 identified countries stay forward
under a systematic fifteen-year dating error. Labour-
market demand lags schooling almost everywhere when measured on its own, and a supply-versus-demand decomposition
puts the supply block far ahead. The historical cases explain the panel's shape: where the state supplied the
schools, they preceded the industrial transformation (Prussia, France's statutes, the American common schools);
where the market and charities supplied them unevenly, industrialisation came first (Britain). The result is a
late-industrialiser regularity, not a universal law, and the paper's increment is a new assembly of known facts —
the systematic event-ordering compilation — rather than a new direction. The agenda that would turn this ordering
into a causal claim is set out in Section 8, and it is the reason the ordering is worth measuring: an order is not
a cause, but it tells us which causes to look for.
