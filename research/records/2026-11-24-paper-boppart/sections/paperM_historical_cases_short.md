# Paper M — Historical cases (short draft for integration, ~900 words)

**Provenance: `research/historical-sequence-2026-11-23` @ `ab658c7`, file `README.md` sections 2-7.
Operational definitions were committed first at `74e0fe2` (`DEFINITIONS.md`).**

The 1965-2010 country panel finds that schooling take-off precedes the release of agricultural labour in 61 of
64 countries. That regularity could be a law of development or a property of the late-industrialising countries
that dominate post-1965 data. The historical cases are where the question can be asked directly, and the answer
is split: schooling came first only where the state built the pipeline ahead of demand.

**Prussia** is the clean supply-led case. The General-Landschul-Reglement of 1763 made schooling compulsory,
reaffirmed in 1794 and 1812; roughly half of school-age children were enrolled around 1800 and enrolment was
near universal by the 1860s; industrialisation is dated to the 1840s-1870s and the agricultural employment share
began its sustained fall after 1850. Schooling precedes industrialisation and the farm exodus by 80 to 120
years. The state created the schools before there was industrial demand for literate labour, which is exactly
the theory's pipeline-first mechanism.

**France** shows the statute and the attainment take-off coming apart. Guizot's 1833 law required a primary
school in every commune, followed by Falloux in 1850 and the Ferry laws of 1881-82. But measured attainment
does not follow 1833: primary enrolment is estimated at about 45 per cent in the 1830s and only reaches 75-80 per
cent by 1880, while the agricultural share was about 52 per cent in 1851, 41 per cent in 1901 and 30 per cent by
1930. The statute precedes the farm exodus by fifty years; measured attainment arrives with it. The honest
verdict is statute-led and attainment demand-following, and it is why the policy date and the attainment date
are reported separately.

**Britain** is the case that started the question, and it inverts the ordering. The industrial revolution dates
to roughly 1780-1840, and the agricultural employment share fell from about 35 per cent in 1800 to 22 per cent in
1851 and 9 per cent by 1901. Mass schooling arrives late: the Elementary Education Act in 1870, compulsory
attendance in 1880, free schooling in 1891, and attendance of about one child in eight as late as 1858.
Industrialisation and the farm exodus precede mass schooling by 40 to 90 years. This is where "floor falls
first" is true, and it is also the case in which a state-supplied mechanism should have been least needed.

**The northern United States** sits in between. Common schools were built in 1830-60 with public funding from
the 1830s and the first state compulsory law in Massachusetts in 1852; the national agricultural employment
share was about 63 per cent in 1840, 50 per cent in 1870, 31 per cent in 1910 and 12 per cent in 1950, and the
high-school movement dates to 1910-40. Schooling take-off precedes the national farm exodus by sixty or more
years, but the schools were locally financed and demand-driven, and the high-school movement coincides with the
final exodus rather than preceding it.

**Reading.** The panel regularity survives only with the qualifier *where the state built the pipeline before
demand arrived*. Prussia is the clean case; France separates the statute from the attainment by half a century;
Britain inverts the ordering; the United States sits between. The implication is that the 61-of-64 result is a
**late-industrialiser regularity, not a universal law**, and it is evidence about who built the pipeline and
when rather than evidence that schooling causes the labour release.

**Evidence classes and the data gap.** Every date above is a standard literature date but **none was verified
against a primary source in this session**; the sources returned only fragments, so all dates and percentages
should be labelled **UNVERIFIED literature standard**. Two facts are verified in-session, both negative: the
project's own long-run panel **cannot** adjudicate this, because its mean-years-of-schooling series starts in
**1870** for all four countries and its urban-share series in **1950**, so the pre-registered take-off rule
returns 1870 — the start of the data — for every case (`PAPER`/`results/sequence_from_panel.json`, computed from
`/root/work/transition/out/long_run_panel.csv`). And no enrolment series was marketable from public APIs in the
session, so no lead or lag is estimated from a series. This section is a dated argument, not a measurement; the
four series that would make it a measurement are named in the record (Prussian enrolment 1800-1870 from Flora
and Lindert; UK attendance 1850-1900 from the Newcastle Commission and Board of Education reports; French primary
enrolment 1830-1900 from Diebolt and the ministry statistics; US high-school enrolment 1890-1940 from Historical
Statistics).
