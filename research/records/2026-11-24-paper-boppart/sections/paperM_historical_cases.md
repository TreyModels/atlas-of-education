## 1. Why the historical cases adjudicate what the panel cannot

The 1965-2010 country panel in this project finds that schooling take-off precedes the release of agricultural
labour in 61 of 64 countries. That is a striking regularity and it contradicts the expectation that the floor
falls first and the pipeline follows. But the panel cannot tell us whether the regularity is a law of
development or a property of the late-industrialising countries that dominate the post-1965 data. The
historical cases are where the question can be asked directly: in the countries that industrialised early, did
mass schooling come before or after the agricultural exodus?

The answer is **split**, and the split is the finding. Schooling came first only where the state built the
pipeline ahead of demand. In the first industrialiser, industrialisation and the agricultural exodus came first,
by decades.

## 2. Prussia: the strong case for supply-led massification

Prussia made schooling compulsory in the **General-Landschul-Reglement of 1763**, reaffirmed by the 1794
Allgemeines Landrecht and the 1812 Hardenberg edict **[S]**. Roughly half of school-age children were already
enrolled around 1800, and enrolment was near universal by the 1860s **[U]**. German industrialisation is dated
to the 1840s-1870s, and the agricultural employment share began its sustained fall after 1850, falling below 40
per cent by 1900 **[U]**.

The order is therefore unambiguous in the sources: **compulsory schooling precedes industrialisation and the
farm exodus by 80 to 120 years**. This is the case that most cleanly supports the theory's pipeline-first
mechanism. The state created the schools before there was an industrial demand for literate labour, and the
labour force that industrialisation later absorbed was already schooled.

## 3. France: the statute and the attainment take-off come apart

France supplies the state's supply push early: the **Guizot law of 1833** required a primary school in every
commune, followed by Falloux in 1850 and the **Ferry laws of 1881-82**, which made primary schooling free and
compulsory **[S]**. But measured attainment does not follow the 1833 statute: primary enrolment is estimated at
roughly 45 per cent in the 1830s and only reaches 75 to 80 per cent by 1880 **[U]**. The agricultural
employment share was about 52 per cent in 1851, 41 per cent in 1901 and 30 per cent by 1930 **[U]**.

So the **statute** precedes the farm exodus by fifty years while **measured attainment** arrives together with
it. The honest verdict is "statute-led, attainment demand-following", and it is the reason this section keeps
the policy date and the attainment date in separate columns. A paper that reported only the statute would
overstate the supply-led reading; one that reported only attainment would miss the state's early role entirely.

## 4. Britain: the counterexample, and why it is not a small one

Britain is the case that started the question. Its industrial revolution is dated to roughly 1780-1840, and its
agricultural employment share fell from about 35 per cent in 1800 to 22 per cent in 1851 and about 9 per cent by
1901 **[U]**. Mass schooling arrives late: the **Elementary Education Act of 1870**, compulsory attendance in
1880, free schooling in 1891, and near-universal attendance only by 1900, with attendance of about one child in
eight reported in 1858 **[U]**.

**Industrialisation and the agricultural exodus precede mass schooling by 40 to 90 years.** This is where the
"floor falls first" ordering is true, and it is the case in which the theory's state-supplied mechanism should
have been least needed: the demand for literacy was present, but provision was private, charitable and uneven
until 1870.

## 5. The United States: supply-led in the local-public-goods sense

The northern United States built common schools in **1830-60**, with public funding from the 1830s and the first
state compulsory law in Massachusetts in 1852 **[U]**. The national agricultural employment share was about 63
per cent in 1840, 50 per cent in 1870, 31 per cent in 1910 and 12 per cent in 1950 **[U]**. The high-school
movement dates to **1910-40**.

Nationally, then, **schooling take-off precedes the farm exodus by sixty or more years**, but the schools were
locally financed and demand-driven by communities, and the high-school movement coincides with the final farm
exodus rather than preceding it. The United States is therefore supply-led in the sense that public provision
came first, while being demand-driven in the sense that the demand was local.

## 7. What the cases say, and what they cannot

**Reading.** "Schooling take-off precedes the farm exodus" survives these four cases only with a qualifier:
*where the state built the pipeline before the demand arrived*. Prussia is the clean case; France shows the
statute and the attainment take-off separating by half a century; Britain inverts the ordering; the United
States sits between, with public provision early but locally demanded. The implication for the panel result is
direct: **the 61-of-64 regularity is a late-industrialiser pattern, not a universal law**, and it is evidence
about who built the pipeline and when rather than evidence that schooling causes the labour release.

**What cannot be claimed.** These four cases are an argument, not a measurement. No lead or lag is estimated
from a series here, because the project's own long-run panel cannot support it: its mean-years-of-schooling
series starts in **1870** for all four countries and its urban-share series in **1950** [V, checked in-session],
so applying the pre-registered take-off rule to it returns 1870 for every one of them, which is the start of the
data rather than a historical event. The enrolment and agricultural-share percentages above are literature
standards and are marked **[U]**; nineteenth-century agricultural shares are themselves reconstructions that
differ between Broadberry, Bairoch and Mitchell by several points, which matters when a 40-year lead is being
claimed.

**What would make it a measurement.** Four series would do it: Prussian enrolment 1800-1870 (Flora, *State,
Economy and Society in Western Europe*; Lindert, *Growing Public*); UK school attendance 1850-1900 (Newcastle
Commission 1861 and the Board of Education annual reports); French primary enrolment 1830-1900 (Diebolt's series
and the ministry's *Statistique de l'enseignement primaire*); and US high-school enrolment 1890-1940 (US
Historical Statistics). With those, the lead and lag could be dated and given uncertainty. Without them this
section is a dated argument, and it is labelled as one.

regularity is universal. No claim of precedence is made.
