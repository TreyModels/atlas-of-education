# Five usable facts from the UN marriage data (plain words, one page)

Companion to `README.md`. For a non-technical reader; numbers are from the UN World Marriage Data
2019 workbook and the UN living-arrangements database, all survey/census observations kept at their real
years with no interpolation. Country medians are unweighted across countries, so they describe the
typical country with data, not the average person in the world.

**1. The data are good enough to describe 210 countries over sixty years.** Women's mean age at marriage
is observed in 210 countries and about 2,070 country-years between 1960 and 2019; 207 of those countries
have at least two observations, so change can be seen directly rather than modelled. Men's marriage age
covers 209 countries, and the share of women still never married at 45-49 covers 211. A caution: about one
third of the marriage observations are the UN's own model-based estimates rather than censuses or
surveys; removing them is one of the tests in the main report.

**2. Women now marry late almost everywhere, and the change was large.** The earliest surveys (1960s,
only 14 countries) put the typical country's women at about 19 years at marriage; by the 1970s it was
about 22 and by the 2010s about 26. The latest observation per country has a median of 24.4, with nearly
half of countries at 25 or above. The rise shows up in rich and poor countries alike and has not reversed.

**3. Later marriage and lower fertility go together across countries — but not decade to decade.** Across
countries the association is strong (roughly −0.65): the 1960s surveys show a marriage age near 19 with
about 6.5 births per woman, the 2010s a marriage age near 26 with about 2.6. Within the same country,
however, the rise in marriage age and the fall in births over the same ten-year window are essentially
uncorrelated (+0.007 over 1,339 country-windows). Read plainly: marriage age marks where a country is in
its transition; it is not a short-run lever.

**4. Never marrying is a minority outcome by age 45-49 in most countries.** The median country has 7.2%
of women never married at 45-49. It is above 10% in about four countries in ten and above 20% in about
two in ten, with a maximum of 46%. So non-marriage explains a great deal of the low fertility in a few
places (notably East Asia and parts of Europe) but little of it in most countries. Whether changes in this
share predict later fertility change is **undetermined** in this data: the level association is not
significant, and its sign is not stable across specifications.

**5. The age gap at marriage is universal and it is narrowing.** In every country in the latest data,
women are the younger partner at marriage; the typical gap is 3.3 years (men older). When that gap narrows
faster, fertility falls faster over the same window (about 0.10 fewer births per woman over the window for
each year the gap narrows, 190 countries), but this result is provisional: it changes sign under one defensible
specification change. Treat it as a lead, not a finding.

**For context, if needed: most older people live with their adult children.** In the UN living-arrangements
data the median country has about half of people aged 60 and over living with children aged 20 or over,
and about 57% in a multi-generation household (categories overlap), with about 13% living alone. The
coverage is thin — 143 countries, mostly 1990-2020, from household survey rosters — so these are
descriptive numbers only; they add nothing measurable to fertility in the tests.

**What this data cannot say.** It cannot say that later marriage causes lower fertility, or the reverse.
The two move together across countries and over long periods, but not window by window, and marriage age
sits on the causal path between schooling and fertility, so it must never be used as a control in that
analysis. The honest summary is: useful for description and for markers of where a country sits, not for
a causal claim.
