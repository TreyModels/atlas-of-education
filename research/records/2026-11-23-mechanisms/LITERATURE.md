# Part 1 — Literature map: why might fertility track child survival and life expectancy?

Every mechanism below predicts the same two country-level facts we found: higher child mortality → higher fertility;
longer life expectancy → lower fertility. That is the observational-equivalence problem this whole record is about.
Citations are marked **[verified]** where the title/record and a URL were returned by a web search in this session,
and **[unverified details]** where the standard bibliographic details (volume/pages/authors) were not captured in the
retrieved record. Nothing here is invented; where I could not verify a source it is flagged rather than asserted.

## 1. Child survival, replacement, and hoarding/insurance

**Replacement.** Parents who lose a child have more births to reach a desired number of surviving children. The
demand for children is a demand for *survivors*, so a fall in mortality lowers the number of births needed.

- **Preston (ed.), The Effects of Infant and Child Mortality on Fertility (Academic Press, 1978) [verified]** —
  the standard comparative collection on replacement, with the size of the replacement effect varying by setting.
- **The child survival hypothesis, Population Studies 30(2):263-278 (1976), doi:10.1080/00324728.1976.10412734
  [verified record; author not captured in the retrieved snippet]** — the hypothesis stated for the demographic
  transition: mortality decline as a precondition for sustained fertility decline.
- **Ben-Porath, "Fertility response to child mortality: micro data from Israel", Journal of Political Economy
  84(4) Part 2, doi:10.1086/260537 (1976) [verified]** — an early micro replacement estimate.
- **Ebanks, "Infant and child mortality and fertility — Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana and Jamaica", World Fertility
  Survey Scientific Reports (1985) [verified record]** — WFS-era micro evidence on replacement.
- **"Fertility response to infant and child mortality in Africa, with special reference to Cameroon", National
  Academies chapter (recid 5842, chap. 254-315) [verified record]** — regional micro evidence.

**Hoarding / insurance.** If child survival is uncertain, parents may have *extra* births in anticipation of losses —
the opposite temporal signature to replacement: more births *before* any death, and a weaker or absent response to an
actual death. **Pörtner, "Children as insurance", Journal of Population Economics, doi:10.1007/s001480050162 (2001)
[verified]** formalises children as a hedge against old-age and income risk.

- **Signature prediction:** replacement = a *positive* response of the next birth hazard to an actual child death;
  hoarding/insurance = *higher fertility where risk is higher*, with little or no response to realised deaths.
- **Separating data:** parity-specific birth histories (DHS/WFS), which distinguish "extra births before a death"
  from "a birth after a death"; and level-versus-volatility contrasts (insurance responds to risk, replacement to
  realised losses).
- **Contested:** the literature disagrees on the size of replacement in contemporary settings and on whether it is
  strong enough to matter at aggregate scale. The old comparative claims are large; modern micro estimates are
  usually modest. **[Contested]**

## 2. Uncertainty, volatility, and precautionary demand

Distinct from the level of mortality: the *unpredictability* of survival. Insurance/hoarding is the fertility-side
prediction; precautionary saving and human-capital investment are the other side. If fertility responds to volatility
rather than to the level, then a smooth mortality decline (the historical experience) should have moved fertility less
than the same decline delivered through repeated shocks.

- **Signature prediction:** fertility responds to the *dispersion* of mortality (or to shocks) conditional on the
  level; places with the same average child mortality but different volatility have different fertility.
- **Evidence:** mostly theoretical and micro; the aggregate-level test is rarely run cleanly because volatility and
  level are correlated. Weather-shock work (**"Adjusting fertility to weather shocks. What implications for food
  security?" — IHSN catalogue record [verified record]**) is one route.
- **Separating data:** a within-country panel that decomposes child mortality into a slow trend and transitory
  deviations (this is the one mechanism we can test *at country level* with data already on the server).
- **Contested:** whether "uncertainty" is best measured as volatility, shock frequency, or subjective risk; and whether
  it is separable from the level in observational data. **[Contested]**

## 3. Quantity-quality and human capital

Becker-Lewis, Becker-Barro and the unified-growth literature model fertility as a quantity decision interacting with
child quality. A mortality decline raises the return to investing in each survivor (fewer births, more schooling per
child); longer life expectancy extends the horizon over which returns accrue, raising the return to education and
lowering fertility.

- **Becker & Lewis, "On the interaction between the quantity and quality of children", Journal of Political Economy
  81(2) (1973) [verified title/venue; details from a citation record]**.
- **Becker & Barro, "A reformulation of the economic theory of fertility" (1988), NBER Working Paper 1793
  [verified]**.
- **Galor & Weil, "Population, technology, and growth: from the Malthusian regime to the demographic transition and
  beyond", NBER Working Paper 6811 (2000) [verified]** — unified growth with human capital as the transition engine.
- **Doepke, "Child mortality and fertility decline: does the Barro-Becker model fit the facts?", Journal of Population
  Economics, doi:10.1007/s00148-004-0208-z (2005) [verified]** — the key test: the bare Barro-Becker model predicts
  a *weak* fertility response to child mortality, and Doepke argues the fit to the facts is poor unless other
  channels (e.g., uncertainty) are added. This is direct support for taking the uncertainty channel seriously.
- **Soares, "Mortality reductions, educational attainment, and fertility choice", American Economic Review (2005)
  [verified title/venue]** — lower mortality raises the return to education and lowers fertility.
- **Kalemli-Ozcan, "A stochastic model of mortality, fertility, and human capital investment" [verified title;
  journal/volume details unverified in the retrieved record]**; **Kalemli-Ozcan, Ryder & Weil, "Mortality decline,
  human capital investment, and economic growth" [verified title record; journal/volume unverified]** — the
  mortality-fertility link works through human capital and growth.
- **Empirical quantity-quality:** Black, Devereux & Salvanes, "The more the merrier?" (NBER Working Paper 10720)
  **[verified record]**; Angrist, Lavy & Schlosser, "New evidence on the causal link between the quantity and quality
  of children" **[verified record]**. Both find the trade-off is real but context-dependent.
- **Signature prediction:** the mortality-fertility link should be *weaker where returns to schooling are higher*
  (parents substitute quality for quantity), and longer life expectancy should raise education before fertility falls.
- **Separating data:** country- or region-level returns to education interacted with the mortality-fertility link;
  micro data on schooling and sibship size.
- **Contested:** whether quantity-quality is a first-order driver of the transition or a second-order mechanism;
  the empirical record on the trade-off is mixed. **[Contested]**

## 4. Life-history and evolutionary theory

Life-history theory predicts that when extrinsic (unavoidable) mortality is high, the optimal strategy is to start
reproducing earlier and faster ("fast" strategy); when extrinsic mortality falls, slower schedules with more
investment per child pay off. This predicts a *timing* effect (earlier first birth, shorter spacing, lower parental
investment under high mortality) that standard economics does not necessarily produce.

- **Promislow & Harvey, "Living fast and dying young: a comparative analysis of life-history variation among
  mammals", Journal of Zoology, doi:10.1111/j.1469-7998.1990.tb04316.x (1990) [verified]** — the classic comparative
  statement.
- **"Two tiers, not one: different sources of extrinsic mortality have opposing effects on life history traits",
  Behavioral and Brain Sciences (Cambridge, forthcoming/2026 record) [verified record; authors not captured]** — recent
  work arguing that the *source* of extrinsic mortality (predation vs disease) changes the predicted response, which
  matters for interpreting human mortality decline.
- **Signature prediction:** high extrinsic mortality → earlier first birth and shorter spacing, independent of the
  number of surviving children chosen; a life-history effect should show up in *age at first birth* even with the
  same completed family size.
- **Separating data:** individual birth histories with ages; life expectancy at birth by cohort.
- **Contested:** how to separate "extrinsic" from "intrinsic" mortality in human data; evolutionary predictions and
  economic predictions overlap heavily at aggregate level. **[Contested]**

## 5. Tempo versus quantum

A longer horizon may change *when* births happen (tempo) rather than *how many* (quantum). Bongaarts-Feeney showed
that period fertility can fall purely because births are postponed, and that tempo-adjusted measures differ
substantially from the raw ones.

- **Bongaarts & Feeney, "On the quantum and tempo of fertility", Population and Development Review 24(2) (1998)
  [verified title; volume/pages standard, not captured in the retrieved snippet]**.
- **Davis, "The theory of change and response in modern demographic history", Population Index 29(4):345-366 (1963)
  [verified, PubMed 12335951]** — the "multiphasic response": families adjust different behaviours (marriage, spacing,
  migration) to changing survival, not just the number of births.
- **Notestein, "Population: the long view", in T.W. Schultz (ed.), Food for the World (1945) [verified title record;
  publisher/year standard, not captured]** — the classic transition narrative in which mortality decline precedes
  fertility decline.
- **Signature prediction:** if the effect is tempo, completed family size is unchanged and only ages shift; if quantum,
  completed family size falls.
- **Separating data:** age-specific and parity-specific fertility; cohort completed fertility; tempo-adjusted TFR.
- **Contested:** the size and direction of tempo distortions; whether mortality decline operates mainly through tempo
  at all. **[Contested]**

## 6. The common-cause rival (development, public health, state capacity)

Both mortality and fertility may be driven by a third factor: development, public-health infrastructure, female
education, or state capacity. A correlation between them is then not causal in either direction.

- **Cleland, "The effects of improved survival on fertility: a reassessment", in Bulatao & Casterline (eds), Global
  Fertility Transition, Population and Development Review Supplement 27 (2001) [verified title; collection verified]**
  — the most direct statement of the reassessment: improved survival explains less of the fertility transition than
  the classic account claims.
- **"Health infrastructure, demographic transition and growth", Economics of Transition (Wiley),
  doi:10.1111/j.1467-9361.2005.00293.x [verified record; authors not captured]** — public-health infrastructure as a
  common driver.
- **"Demographic, epidemiological, and health transitions: are they relevant to population health patterns in
  Africa?", Global Health Action (2014), doi:10.3402/gha.v7.22443 [verified record]** — the co-movement of the
  transitions as a systems phenomenon.
- **Signature prediction:** the mortality-fertility association disappears once state capacity / public health /
  development is properly controlled or instrumented; and both series respond to the same interventions.
- **Separating data:** exogenous mortality shocks (campaigns, vaccine introductions) plus measures of state capacity;
  or the timing of health-technology diffusion.
- **Contested:** the only rival that, if true, invalidates the whole mechanism family as a causal explanation.
  Distinguishing it from the others is the hard part, and the reason Part 3 is built around exogenous shocks. **[Contested]**

## 7. Evidence from natural experiments and shocks (the bridge to Part 3)

- **"Childhood immunization, mortality and human capital accumulation: micro-evidence from India" (MPRA 27127;
  EconPapers record) [verified record]** — vaccination and its human-capital consequences.
- **Wilde & Apouey (?), "The effect of antimalarial campaigns on child mortality and fertility in sub-Saharan Africa"
  [verified title record; author list from the search index, not the paper itself]** — a direct campaign → mortality →
  fertility design.
- **"How conflict shapes the impact of infant and child mortality on fertility in the Democratic Republic of the
  Congo", (2025) [verified record]** — conflict as a mortality shock and its interaction with fertility.
- These are the designs that can identify a *causal* mortality effect, because campaign rollout and shocks are not
  chosen by the same families whose fertility we observe. Their weakness is that campaign rollout correlates with
  state capacity — the common-cause rival — which is why every design in Part 3 carries explicit identifying
  assumptions.

## Where the literature is genuinely contested

1. **Size of replacement:** classic comparative work says large; modern micro estimates are modest and heterogeneous.
2. **Level versus volatility:** theory says risk matters; clean aggregate tests are rare because level and volatility
   are correlated.
3. **Quantity-quality:** real but fragile; the causal estimates are mixed and sensitive to instrument choice.
4. **Tempo versus quantum:** tempo effects are real but contested in size, and the mortality → tempo link is untested.
5. **Common cause:** the most damaging rival and the hardest to rule out; the natural-experiment literature is still
   thin and often underpowered.
6. **Reverse causation:** higher fertility could raise child mortality by diluting resources — our matrix finds the
   reverse-direction links null (see README), which weakens but does not eliminate this channel.
