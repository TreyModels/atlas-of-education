# Novelty assessment: what the paper can and cannot claim

14 November 2026. Basis: the v1 outline (`docs/engine/methods_paper_outline.md`), the repo files named in `OUTLINE_v2.md`, and the sources checked in `REFERENCES_VERIFIED.md`. Limits of this assessment: I read abstracts, tables of contents and catalogue records, not the full texts of the analytical-sociology works, and I did not run a systematic search of the comparative-education journals for simulation work. Both should be done before submission (see the last section).

## 1. What is already done

**The argument.** "Explain a social pattern by specifying the generating mechanism, and use a simulation to show the mechanism generates it" is the programme of analytical sociology. Hedström (2005) states it as a book-length programme: a phenomenon is explained by identifying the mechanism that regularly produces it, and the table of contents includes a chapter on agent-based simulation. Hedström and Swedberg (1998) is the edited statement of the mechanism idea; Hedström and Ylikoski (2010) is the review, and its abstract names simulations of interacting agents as a way to build mechanism-based explanations; Hedström and Bearman (2009) is the handbook. Hedström and Manzo (2015) introduce a *Sociological Methods & Research* special issue on agent-based research in the same terms. Boudon himself used the language of "generating models" (cited via Hedström and Manzo 2015; the original is unchecked), so the generative idea in educational sociology predates Epstein (1999) and is not an import from complexity science.

**Executable Boudon-type and educational-choice models.** Manzo has built them. Manzo (2013) is a formal model plus agent-based simulation of educational choice, built on Breen and Goldthorpe (1997), with a network term that formalises a friend-dependence argument attributed to Boudon, tested against French survey data; its finding is that individual calculation alone cannot reproduce the observed stratification of choices and network influence is needed. Manzo (2011) is an agent-based generalisation of Boudon's relative-deprivation model. Manzo's 2009 book (*La spirale des inégalités*) does a multi-agent simulation of school choice in France and Italy. Manzo (2022) is a book on what agent-based models can and cannot establish about causal inference. Breen and Goldthorpe (1997) supply the formal rational-action model that most of this work extends; their model is analytical rather than simulated.

**Education-policy ABMs outside sociology.** Maroulis and colleagues have been running agent-based models of school choice since at least 2014 (Maroulis et al. 2014, 2016) and argued for a complex-systems view of education policy research in *Science* in 2010. Salgado, Marchione and Gilbert (2014) set an ABM of school effectiveness against a multilevel model on the same data. Reardon et al. (2016) model college sorting. Leoni (2021) models tertiary choice in Italy and builds on Manzo. These are small in number, but they exist and they cover school choice and educational inequality.

**Validation methodology.** The "cocoon" components are standard elsewhere. Docking: Axtell et al. (1996). Pattern-oriented modelling: Grimm et al. (2005). History matching: Craig et al. (2001) and the HIV-in-Uganda tutorial (Andrianakis et al. 2015). ABC calibration: Beaumont et al. (2002), with an ABM cookbook by Thiele et al. (2014). Empirical validation of ABMs as a topic: Fagiolo, Moneta and Windrum (2007) and Fagiolo et al. (2019) in economics, Bruch and Atwell (2015) in sociology. Stock-flow-consistent accounting: Godley and Lavoie (2007), in macroeconomics. The idea of checking for leakage from the test period and comparing against a naive benchmark is standard forecasting practice, not specific to any field.

**The field's own prior step.** The author's 2012 conference paper applied a complex-adaptive-systems analysis to comparative education. Whatever its reach, a reviewer will treat it as prior art by the same author, so the paper should cite it and say what is new relative to it.

## 2. What is not done, as far as I could find

I found no source that does any of the following. Absence in my search is weak evidence, and the claim should be phrased as "we are not aware of", after a proper search.

1. **Bringing this approach into comparative and international education as a field.** Everything above is in sociology, computational social science, economics, ecology or US school-choice research. The comparative-education journals' use of simulation I did not survey. If it is as thin as I expect, "introducing generative modelling to comparative education, with attention to cross-national testing" is a credible disciplinary claim; "inventing generative explanation of education" is not.
2. **A written register that separates calibration numbers from test numbers, with dated moves between them.** I found nothing equivalent in the ABM-validation sources I checked, though careful modellers do this informally and I did not read the full texts. This is a documentation practice, so claim it as a practice and show it working, including the cases where it caught errors.
3. **Docking and exact accounting as routine, checked-in tests across a family of models.** The ideas are old; making them routine, automated and public across a collection of education models is a practice claim, and defensible as such.
4. **Publishing scored negative results from one's own models.** The repo contains plenty: a 1.09-versus-0.89 loss, withdrawn passes, and a review that downgraded most models to "illustrative". I did not find ABM-of-education papers that report their own forecast losses against a naive rule. That is a norm-setting claim, and only credible if the paper's own evidence is clean (see section 4).
5. **Cross-national comparison as the testing ground.** Manzo's work compares France and Italy; the repo's cases (China, Cambodia, Philippines, Vietnam, India) are further apart institutionally. Using the same generative structure across countries with different data availability, and reporting which country-pattern each structure fails, is a plausible contribution, but it has not been done yet in this repo at a level that supports a strong claim: the cross-country results are mostly "the data do not discriminate" or single-country failures.
6. **A recovery test for explanations.** Whether rival mechanisms can be told apart by the data available is, I believe, under-reported in education modelling. The new work in `research/shadow-education/` and `research/credential-floor/` is aimed at this. Results are not in yet and nothing should be claimed before they are.

## 4. The claim the paper can defend

The most defensible contribution is a practice paper, not a theory paper:

1. A discipline-specific translation. Analytical sociology and computational social science have the generative argument and the tools. Comparative and international education, where the same rival-explanation problem recurs (credential expansion, shadow education, school-to-work), has not, as far as I can tell, adopted them. The paper shows what adoption looks like on the field's own cases.
2. A set of auditable practices: a facts register, docking tests, a frozen forecast ledger, scored negative results, and a recovery test before a model is used to adjudicate rival explanations.
3. A worked record of what the practices caught: the hard liquidity threshold that produced the "relational floor rejected" verdict and vanished under a graded constraint; the unreproducible figure ("+22.8 pp no waiting", now +12.2); a withdrawn Kyrgyz pass; the shared-code policy agreement. This is the paper's most credible empirical content, because it is about failures the method found in its own models.
4. One or more blind or recovery results, once they exist. Without at least one, the paper is a proposal and should be labelled so.
