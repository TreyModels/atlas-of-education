# Massification and Its Mechanisms: Toward a Structural Account of Educational Convergence

**Trey Menefee**
Independent Researcher

*Working paper, v1, May 2026. Draft for discussion.*

-----

## Abstract

World culture theory has produced the most influential account of why mass schooling converges on similar institutional forms across otherwise dissimilar societies. Its core explanation—that globally legitimate scripts diffuse through mimetic and normative mechanisms—accounts for the speed and synchronization of convergence but not its depth, durability, or developmental consequences. This paper proposes a structural account organized around three claims. First, educational isomorphism is anchored in a species-specific biological substrate: the extended human developmental window produced by hypermorphosis creates a shared parameter that constrains institutional variation across all populations, and sustained structured engagement during that window appears to induce durable neurocognitive reorganization—with schooling’s effects on fluid reasoning, white matter architecture, and behavioral dispositions providing the developmental mechanism that institutional analysis alone cannot supply. Second, the primary motor of convergence is surplus rather than legitimacy: states seek taxable productive output and households seek better lives, and the internationally legible credential is the interface through which both goals are achieved. Third, this surplus logic, articulated through Tilly’s (1998) mechanisms of durable inequality and Gellner’s (1983) analysis of school-making as state-making, generates empirical predictions that world culture theory cannot. Two case studies develop the argument: the ASEAN Qualifications Reference Framework (AQRF), which demonstrates cooperative credential standardization driven by regional labor market integration; and the inversion of the Gender Parity Index above 1.0 in Muslim-majority states, which demonstrates the credential pipeline’s developmental logic overriding deep cultural resistance. The paper situates the well-documented education-fertility correlation within this account, treating it as one downstream expression of developmental window capture rather than the paper’s primary claim.

**Keywords:** niche construction, world culture theory, credential economy, isomorphism, gender parity index, ASEAN, educational convergence, fertility transition, extended developmental window

-----

Schools are a historically recent invention at population scale. For most of human history, developmental formatting happened through apprenticeship, kinship transmission, ritual, and local practice. Elite scribal schools, imperial examination systems, and religious seminaries existed for millennia, but the institutional enclosure of the developmental window at population scale – the systematic capture of the first two decades of every child’s life inside a standardized evaluative institution – is barely a century old in most of the world and still incomplete across much of the global South. That novelty is worth stating before asking why the form that emerged looks so similar everywhere.

Why does mass schooling converge on the same institutional form—the bell, the seating plan, the examination calendar, the credential hierarchy—across societies that differ radically in language, religion, political structure, and historical trajectory? This question sits at the center of comparative education theory, and the dominant answer has been supplied by world culture theory, associated with John W. Meyer and the Stanford School.

The world culture account explains convergence through the diffusion of globally legitimate institutional scripts. States adopt similar educational structures not primarily because they independently solve the same practical problems but because internationally authorized cultural models define what a proper state, school system, and educated citizen should look like (Meyer et al., 1997; Ramirez & Boli, 1987). The theory is influential precisely because it explains so much: the spread of mass schooling, the expansion of higher education, the adoption of standardized curricula, and the proliferation of international assessments all look like the diffusion of legitimate templates (Schofer & Meyer, 2005).

Yet world culture theory has a documented weakness: it explains the form of convergence without explaining its depth. The theory predicts ceremonial adoption—states performing compliance with legitimate templates while actual practice diverges (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). But the convergence in cognitive outcomes, demographic behavior, and labor market participation that follows educational expansion is not ceremonial. You cannot decouple from a Visual Word Form Area. The niche installs the phenotype whether or not the state sincerely intended to adopt it.

This weakness is not unique to world culture theory. The three dominant traditions in comparative education share a common blind spot: they treat cognitive and behavioral outcomes as readable from institutional or political analysis rather than as objects requiring independent developmental explanation. World culture theory explains why schooling looks the same; it does not explain what it does to the organisms that pass through it. The political economy tradition explains who benefits from credential systems; it does not explain why credential-gated cognitive profiles are durable across generations. The postcolonial tradition explains whose knowledge systems are suppressed; it does not explain why alternative pedagogies rarely produce the same formation outcomes. What is missing across all three is a developmental account – an explanation of what the institution is physically doing to organisms during the plastic window, and why that mechanism produces convergent effects wherever it operates.

This paper proposes a structural account organized around three claims. First, the shared biological substrate of the human species—specifically, the extended developmental window produced by hypermorphosis—provides a natural parameter that constrains institutional variation. Sustained structured engagement during that window appears to induce durable neurocognitive reorganization; institutions that exploit it systematically produce convergent developmental outcomes across dissimilar populations. Second, the primary motor of educational convergence is surplus capture rather than legitimacy: states seek taxable productive output and households seek improved life chances, and the internationally legible credential is the interface through which both goals are achieved. Third, this surplus logic, articulated through Tilly’s (1998) mechanisms of durable inequality and Gellner’s (1983) analysis of school-making as state-making, generates empirical predictions that institutional analysis alone cannot.

Two case studies develop the argument. The ASEAN Qualifications Reference Framework (AQRF) demonstrates cooperative credential standardization driven by regional labor market integration rather than legitimacy diffusion. The inversion of the Gender Parity Index (GPI) above 1.0 in Muslim-majority states demonstrates the credential pipeline asserting its developmental logic against deep cultural resistance. The paper also engages the well-documented education-fertility correlation, situating it as one downstream expression of developmental window capture rather than the paper’s primary claim.

## The World Culture Account and Its Limits

World culture theory, developed by Meyer and colleagues across five decades, offers the most comprehensive explanation of global educational isomorphism. Its core claim is that modern nation-states adopt strikingly similar institutional forms not primarily because they independently solve the same practical problems but because globally legitimate cultural models define what a proper state, citizen, and school system should look like (Meyer et al., 1997; Meyer & Ramirez, 2000). The theory identifies normative diffusion through professional expert communities, mimetic diffusion through the copying of prestigious models under uncertainty, and organizational carriers—international organizations, INGOs, and expert networks—that package and circulate institutional scripts across borders (Boli & Thomas, 1997; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983).

The theory also predicts decoupling: states and organizations often adopt globally legitimate forms ceremonially while everyday implementation remains partial, uneven, or locally transformed (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). This is analytically useful but has been criticized as a rescue device—if adoption counts as evidence for the theory and non-implementation also counts as evidence via decoupling, the theory risks becoming unfalsifiable (Silova & Brehm, 2013).

More fundamental criticisms concern the concept of culture the theory employs. Downey et al. (2020) argue that world culture theory underplays coercion and geopolitical hierarchy, giving too much weight to voluntary cultural diffusion and too little to the role of powerful states and international financial institutions. Eisenstadt’s (2000) multiple modernities framework argues that different civilizations reinterpret modernity differently, so enduring diversity is constitutive rather than residual. Recent analysis of World Values Survey data documents worldwide divergence of values rather than simple convergence, with regional clustering becoming more pronounced over time (Inglehart, 2021).

These critiques share a common limitation: they operate in the same register as the theory they challenge, treating culture as a normative and ideological phenomenon—as scripts, values, and legitimacy claims that travel through social networks. None addresses why the isomorphic institution produces the specific biological, cognitive, and demographic consequences it produces. Jackson’s (2025, 2026) work exemplifies this register at its most philosophically developed: her central question is how education handles social difference, which is legitimate and important. But handling difference is not the same question as why difference is collapsing at the population level. The GPI inversion in Muslim-majority states, the fertility transition in East Asia, and the cognitive convergence documented in Flynn Effect research are not primarily about how schools manage social difference. They are about what the school does to organisms that pass through it, regardless of what those organisms believe.

A further limitation follows from WST’s state-centric unit of analysis. The theory explains why states adopt educational scripts, but adoption alone does not describe the household financing and behaviour that make a school system operate. Bray’s *Counting the Full Cost* (1996) documents substantial parental and community contributions—cash, labour, materials and forgone earnings—across nine East Asian systems, including nominally public schooling. His Cambodia study, *The Private Costs of Public Schooling* (1999), makes the implementation gap concrete through household and school evidence on contributions, supplies, transport and tutoring. These findings do not show that household demand arose independently of the state, nor do they by themselves refute cultural diffusion. They show that a state-adoption account is incomplete unless it also explains why households pay and participate. The surplus account addresses that missing mechanism: households invest because credentials offer access to more secure and remunerative lives, while formal declarations of free schooling do not eliminate the actual costs of attendance.

The structural account proposed here does not deny that scripts exist or that they matter. But it draws on Tilly’s (2006) analysis of contentious repertoires rather than Meyer’s legitimacy diffusion to explain how they work. In Tilly’s account, actors facing a coordination problem do not invent new forms of action from scratch. They reach for historically available, culturally recognizable repertoires – limited sets of performances that everyone already knows how to execute, that have been proven to coordinate the relevant parties, and that get repeated not because they’re optimal but because they’re available. Repertoires accumulate through practice, not through rational design or ideological conversion.

This is how institutional scripts function in the credential pipeline. The K-12 structure is a repertoire. The four-year undergraduate degree is a repertoire. Recognition of prior learning frameworks are repertoires—costly institutional technologies that took European systems years to develop within the Bologna Process, and that ASEAN working groups then adapted rather than invented from scratch. The demand for RPL recognition existed before the European template: ASEAN states needed to make their informal labor legible across borders. The European framework reduced the transaction cost of solving a problem the surplus motor had already generated. Scripts travel not because they are legitimate in the abstract but because actors facing known coordination problems reach for available solutions. Legitimacy is a property some repertoires acquire after they spread. It is not the motor. Scripts are how the surplus motor coordinates; they are not why it runs.

The distinction between these two uses of “script” matters enough to state explicitly, because conflating them produces the objection that surplus pursuit is itself a global script—a Meyer-style cultural model that states learn from international organizations. That objection only holds if scripts are constitutive in Meyer’s sense: normatively loaded models that shape actors’ identities and define their legitimate ends. In that frame, states build schools because the global culture defines a proper modern state as one that educates its citizens. But if scripts are Tillyan repertoires—coordination devices that lower the cost of pursuing independently generated ends—then surplus pursuit is not a script at all. It is the structural condition that activates demand for scripts. States and households want surplus because they need to survive and compete in a credential-gated interstate economy; that imperative is not learned from INGOs. The repertoires they adopt—examination calendars, qualification levels, recognition frameworks—are the tools they reach for to solve coordination problems that surplus pursuit generates. Meyer’s account explains the existence and global spread of the idea that modern states educate their citizens. The surplus account explains the specific calibration: why K-12 and not K-10, why the window extends into the late twenties in high-saturation economies, why the pipeline keeps stretching. That specificity is not supplied by the legitimacy script. It is negotiated by the surplus motor against the developmental clock.

## The Biological Substrate: Hypermorphosis and the Extended Developmental Window

The convergence of educational institutions across dissimilar societies is anchored in a biological substrate that world culture theory does not address: the human developmental window is a species-specific capacity for extended neural plasticity, and institutions that systematically exploit and extend it will produce convergent outcomes wherever they operate. The claim here is stronger than a simple “permissive condition” argument. The credential pipeline does not merely fill a pre-given window of fixed length. It actively extends the window by delaying the life-history transitions—reproduction, full economic participation, exit from structured learning—that in non-pipelined populations would trigger earlier neurocognitive consolidation. The 32-year figure discussed below is not a universal fixed clock. It is the upper bound that the human developmental system reaches under conditions of maximal plasticity-supporting institutional input. The pipeline constructs the extended plasticity it then exploits. That is the niche construction claim the paper’s title invokes.

The relevant biological concept is hypermorphosis, not neoteny. Neoteny refers to the retention of juvenile features into adulthood through developmental retardation. Hypermorphosis refers to the extension of development beyond the ancestral endpoint—the developmental clock runs longer, not slower (Shea, 1989). Human neural architecture has the capacity to remain plastic for longer than that of any other primate, allowing progressively more complex cultural content to be installed during the extended window. This is what makes cumulative culture possible. The comparison with Neanderthals is instructive: their fast early-growth developmental strategy appears to have capped neural plasticity considerably earlier than in modern humans (Been et al., 2024), suggesting that the capacity for extended plasticity is at minimum a prerequisite for the kind of cross-generational knowledge accumulation that complex institutions require. A window that closes earlier cannot receive as much of what the previous generation built.

The sapiens developmental window has molecular anchors. SRGAP2C, a human-specific gene duplicate that emerged approximately 3.4 million years ago, inhibits its ancestral copy and slows synaptic maturation in human neurons (Charrier et al., 2024). Human postsynaptic densities mature two to three times more slowly than those of macaques or mice. The window is not passive retention of juvenile architecture but an actively encoded delay—a molecular brake that establishes the capacity for extended plasticity. What determines how far that capacity is realized is the niche the organism inhabits during development. The niche construction claim here operates on developmental and phenotypic timescales rather than genetic selection timescales: the pipeline does not alter allele frequencies across generations, it entrains cortical architecture within the developmental window of the current generation. That distinction matters for evolutionary biologists reading the argument but does not weaken it – the developmental effects are real, measurable, and durable within a lifetime.

Mousley et al. (2024), mapping cortical topology across more than 40,000 participants in Western credential-pipeline samples, place the completion of structural brain development at approximately 32 years. This figure should be read as an upper bound observed under conditions of maximal environmental enrichment and maximally delayed life-history transitions—not as a fixed species parameter. In populations where reproduction begins at 16 to 19 and formal structured learning ends at the same point, the life-history transition into adult roles likely coincides with earlier neurocognitive consolidation, not because of a different biological clock but because the niche provides different inputs during the plastic period. The pipeline, by delaying reproduction, prolonging economic dependency, and maintaining structured cognitive demand into the third decade of life, pushes the window toward its biological upper bound. The three-stage structure of the modern pipeline—compulsory K-12 schooling through the first two decades, tertiary education through the early twenties, extended early-career credentialing through the mid-to-late twenties—tracks the diminishing but still substantial plasticity of the maturing cortex. The match between pipeline structure and window extent is not a coincidence or a policy design choice. It is the credential economy negotiating against the developmental clock, and winning more of the window with each decade of expansion. This also generates a testable prediction: populations with equivalent biological substrates but different pipeline exposure should show different white matter maturation trajectories at matched ages, with earlier consolidation in non-pipelined populations. The Flynn Effect is interpretable as a population-level signature of this dynamic: each generation of expanded schooling pushes more of the population toward the window’s upper bound, producing rising fluid cognitive scores until pipeline saturation is reached.

The educational implication is direct. Mass schooling is the first institution in human history to systematically exploit the full biological extent of this window at population scale. The biological argument explains something world culture theory cannot: why the convergence runs deep enough to produce convergent cognitive outcomes (the Flynn Effect), convergent morphological outcomes (literacy-driven VWFA reorganization, near-work myopia), and convergent demographic outcomes (the education-fertility correlation). These are not ceremonial. They follow from the developmental mechanism. The institution exploits the same biological substrate wherever it operates, and the substrate responds similarly wherever it is exploited.

The causal evidence is strong. Ritchie and Tucker-Drob’s (2018) meta-analysis across 42 datasets and more than 600,000 participants estimates one to five IQ points per additional year of schooling, with effects persisting into the seventies and eighties. Brinch and Galloway (2012), exploiting Norway’s compulsory schooling reform, found substantial IQ effects at age 19 in more than 100,000 males – the reform targeted adolescence, not early childhood, directly challenging the assumption that the window closes at school entry. Most strikingly, Judd et al. (2022) found that two years of schooling produced a larger effect on working memory and both fluid and crystallized intelligence than the lifetime consequences, since birth, of socioeconomic position or genetic propensity for cognition. The institution is not sorting pre-existing capacity. It is building it.

Two clarifications about the explanatory division of labor are necessary here. The developmental window is a permissive condition: it explains why humans can be formatted in this way, why the effects are real rather than ceremonial, and why the convergence is deep rather than formal. It does not by itself explain why the specific institutional form—bells, seating plans, examination calendars, credential hierarchies—fills that window. That selection pressure is explained by the surplus motor developed in the following section. The biology constrains the range of possible outcomes; the political economy selects among them.

The bridge between these levels runs through what Heyes (2018) calls cognitive gadgets: culturally inherited mechanisms requiring extensive installation through structured environments. Literacy, numeracy, abstract reasoning, and symbolic manipulation are not unlocked capacities; they are constructed through the pipeline during the plastic window. The critical empirical demonstration comes from Scribner and Cole’s (1981) five-year study of the Vai in Liberia, who had developed their own writing system learned entirely outside formal schooling. Vai literates could read and write but showed minimal effects on abstract reasoning, categorization, and memory. Formal schooling in English produced significant effects on all three. The institution, not the skill, is the mechanism. This separates content from form: it is the sustained exposure to evaluation by strangers, to symbolic tasks disconnected from immediate practical use, to hierarchical assessment under time pressure, that produces cognitive reorganization – not the skill content itself. At the population level, the aggregate of installed gadgets is what Hidalgo and Stojkoski (2025) measure as economic complexity: the productive capacity a polity can assemble depends on which cognitive capability combinations its formatted population can supply. The window is the permissive condition; the gadgets are what gets installed; the surplus is the output that states and households are jointly pursuing. This chain—window, gadgets, surplus—is what the biological and political-economic sections of the argument need to be read as supplying together, not separately.

What the pipeline installs is a bundled package in which formatability is the invariant layer and content-specific cognitive gadgets are what varies by epoch and state need. Formatability means the demonstrated capacity to sustain compliant performance under arbitrary formal constraint, evaluated by strangers holding categorical power over the organism’s trajectory. The Chinese imperial examination system’s eight-legged essay—the baguwen required of candidates from the fifteenth century onward—makes the bundle concrete. The baguwen demanded that candidates argue a position through eight rigidly structured sections using parallel prose within a Confucian interpretive frame. The form looks arbitrary from the outside—nobody governed using parallel prose—but this framing requires qualification. The content was the Confucian classics, the commentaries, the historical precedents that constituted the shared operating system of imperial administration. A candidate who could deploy that content fluently in the eight-legged form had absorbed genuine administrative competencies: textual analysis, analogical reasoning, argumentative structure, and mastery of the cultural code that made coordination possible across a vast empire with no real-time communication. The form was the invariant formatability test. The content was the epoch-specific cognitive installation the state actually needed. Both were real, and both were being selected simultaneously.

The baguwen was therefore not a pure formatability test. It was a bundled credential: formatability plus content-specific installation, delivered together because the friction of mastering the form was the mechanism through which the content was absorbed. Prolonged symbolic performance under evaluative constraint appears to drive the neurocognitive reorganization the schooling literature documents, including white matter plasticity in tracts supporting attentional control, working memory, and abstract reasoning (Yeatman et al., 2018; Roy et al., 2024). The parallel prose nobody governed with was generating the structural connectivity that institutional life requires. The calculus nobody uses in most jobs is doing the same thing now.

The imperial examination system also generated something the formatability framing alone understates: a self-reproducing ecosystem. Households and villages pooled resources to sponsor promising candidates. Examination failures—those who reached the provincial level but not the metropolitan—cycled back into the system as tutors, producing a stratum of educational workers with a material interest in the pipeline’s continuation. The system pulled an elite out of a vast population rather than relying on hereditary transmission, which gave the state a meritocratic legitimation it would not have had otherwise, and gave the tutor class a livelihood. This self-reproduction is not a historical curiosity. It is the structural logic that the contemporary shadow education sector replicates: the credential creates its own supply chain, generating a constituency with material interests in the pipeline’s perpetuation long before any state mandates it.

The contemporary high-stakes examination—the Gaokao, the Suneung, the HKDSE—is the baguwen’s functional successor: bundled formatability and content-specific installation, self-reproducing through the shadow education ecosystem that competitive credential markets generate endogenously. The critical point for the world culture theory debate is that this is not a Western export or a neoliberal imposition. Structurally analogous credential-gating examination systems appeared convergently and independently across geographically isolated civilizations—the Han Chinese keju, the medieval European guild mastership, the Islamic ijazah, the Confucian-inflected Korean gwageo—before any transmission pathway connected them. They converged because they solve the same multi-function problem: how to select for sustained institutional compliance, install state-specific cognitive content, and generate a self-reproducing educational stratum, simultaneously. The surplus motor generates that demand; the formatability-plus-content bundle is the available solution; scripts and templates reduce the cost of implementing it.

A natural objection is that labor markets increasingly differentiate between credential types—that STEM credentials command premiums over humanities credentials because they certify specific competencies rather than generic compliance. This is true, and it does not contradict the formatability account. What varies across credential types is the mix of cognitive gadgets installed during the window: STEM pipelines install more of the abstract formal reasoning and mathematical competency that specific productive networks require. The formatability signal is common across credential types; the specific gadget content varies. Both matter. What the formatability account explains is why the pipeline form—standardized, sequential, evaluation-intensive—is structurally stable across domains where the content differs enormously. The form is what legibility requires; the content is what specific production functions reward.

The question of why this form rather than alternatives—apprenticeship networks, modular competency certification, or Montessori-style self-directed development—has two answers. The first is legibility: the standardized examination emits tokens readable by distant strangers without face-to-face assessment, which apprenticeship and modular systems do not achieve at scale. The second is perceived fairness. The Chinese Zhongkao, which sorts students into secondary school tiers at age fourteen, is perceived by students and families as broadly legitimate precisely because it is one test for everyone, applied uniformly. Dissatisfaction targets preparation access and resource inequality, not the principle of universal standardized sorting. Alternatives appear unthinkable not because the state imposed them but because, given Tilly’s mechanisms, any household that unilaterally opts out of the credential race transfers advantage to every household that stays in. The system is self-enforcing from below.

## The Surplus Motor: States, Households, and International Legibility

If world culture theory’s motor is legitimacy, this paper proposes an alternative: surplus. The term requires definition because it carries both Marxian and ideological connotations the argument does not require. The definition here draws on basic microeconomics: surplus is the gain above the minimum required to make a transaction happen – producer surplus, consumer surplus, the value each party captures above their reservation price. This is not a moral claim and not a totalizing theory of capitalist relations. It is a structural description of why actors converge on an instrument, in exactly the same register that Tilly uses exploitation as a structural description rather than an indictment.

Applied to education: states capture taxable productive output above what unformatted, non-credentialed labor would generate. Households capture life chances above what non-credentialed access to employment and social position would permit. Employers capture formatted, legible, institutionally compliant labor above what they would otherwise have to invest to train from scratch. The credential is the instrument that generates surplus on all three sides of this transaction simultaneously, without requiring coordination or shared ideology among the parties. That simultaneity is why the form spreads: it is not that any one actor imposes it, but that states, households, and employers each find independent reasons to demand and supply it. The motor stalls when the credential stops delivering – when credential premiums collapse, when the state captures no productivity gain from formatted labor, when employers find alternative sorting mechanisms. That is the falsification structure.

This definition connects to the anthropological use of surplus as a durable margin above immediate reproduction that permits accumulation, specialization, and more complex social forms (Halstead & O’Shea, 1989). The microeconomic and anthropological senses share the same structural logic: groups are pulled toward arrangements that yield more discretionary capacity than bare subsistence allows. Modern educational systems are one such arrangement – not the only one historically, but the one that, under conditions of credential-gated labor markets and transnational economic integration, delivers surplus to all parties in the transaction at once. Calling this dynamic surplus is therefore not an invocation of capitalism or neoliberal rationality. It is a claim about structural gravity.

States want more taxable productive output. Households want better lives. The internationally legible credential is the interface through which both goals are achieved. This motor operates independently of any ideological content—liberal, neoliberal, feminist, or otherwise—and generates the same structural outcome wherever states and households pursue the same objective through the same available instrument.

The surplus account is not simply human capital theory. Human capital theory treats the credential as tracking actual skill acquisition, with the return to education reflecting underlying productive capacity (Becker, 1964). The surplus account is more parsimonious: it treats the credential as an interface token whose value derives from its legibility within a network, not from any particular competence it certifies. This is consistent with the signaling literature (Spence, 1973) and with empirical evidence that credential requirements for occupational categories systematically exceed the skill content of those categories (Burning Glass Technologies, 2014; Douglas & Attewell, 2017).

The credential generates three distinct types of value simultaneously, and the surplus motor runs on whichever delivers returns in a given context. First, human capital value: the real cognitive capacity installed through the pipeline – fluid reasoning, formatability, the cognitive gadgets the developmental window enables. This is an absolute good; it raises productive capacity regardless of what anyone else holds. Second, positional value: the advantage derived from relative scarcity in competitive queues. This is Hirsch’s (1976) positional good – the credential is worth something because others lack it, and its value is zero-sum. Shadow education in Daechi-dong is positional competition through and through; the household is not trying to become more capable, it is trying to outrun the student at the next desk. Third, relational value: the legibility the credential provides within a network of mutual recognition. This is not about ranking above competitors. It is about being readable to strangers – employers, universities, migration authorities, banks – who have no other way to assess an unknown person. Being unranked is losing the race. Being illegible is not being in the race.

These three types of value are analytically distinct even when they co-occur. They behave differently under saturation, and distinguishing them clarifies the pipeline’s durability.

The state-level surplus logic follows Tilly’s (1990) analysis of war-making and state-making. War-making requires resources; extracting resources requires administrative capacity; administrative capacity requires standardized, literate, mobile populations. States that failed to produce such populations lost economic and military competition. Gellner (1983) extended this logic directly to mass schooling: industrial societies require a standardized, literate, mobile workforce, and states that fail to produce one fall behind in the competition Tilly describes. Gellner’s central claim—that the monopoly of legitimate education is now more important, more central to state power, than the monopoly of legitimate violence—states the contemporary condition in political science language. Education is typically the largest single line item in national budgets, consistently exceeding defense expenditure in most states with effective administrative capacity. This is not altruism. It is states investing in their primary production mechanism for formatted, network-compatible labor.

The household-level surplus logic is equally direct. Where education is a normal good—where demand increases with income—households invest in it because the credential is the primary token of access to better lives within the network economy. In many postcolonial contexts, households pay for public education through user fees, examination costs, and shadow education expenditure, not because they have been persuaded by legitimacy scripts but because the alternatives are worse. This buy-in requires no false consciousness; it requires rational calculation in a network economy where non-credentialed labor faces structural disadvantage.

The critical addition to both accounts is international legibility. For the credential to serve its network function, it must be readable outside the issuing jurisdiction. This requires standardization not merely within national systems but across them. The pressure toward isomorphism is therefore not primarily about appearing legitimate to an imagined world polity. It is about producing tokens that transnational labor markets can read. This distinction matters most in contexts where the legitimacy account predicts maximum resistance: states with strong pre-existing cultural institutions, recent colonial histories of imposed educational systems, and explicit ideological opposition to Western liberal scripts. In these contexts, the legitimacy account predicts resistance and decoupling. The surplus account predicts adoption wherever states and households conclude that the economic cost of non-adoption exceeds the cultural cost of adoption—which, as the following case studies demonstrate, is almost everywhere.

## Durable Inequality and the State Formation Parallel

States want taxable output. Households want better lives. Neither waits for the other. The credential pipeline self-reinforces from the household level upward through four mechanisms Tilly identifies in Durable Inequality: exploitation, opportunity hoarding, emulation, and adaptation. None of them requires ideology. None requires false consciousness. They operate through rational calculation in a categorical structure no individual actor designed and no individual actor can exit.

Exploitation: credential-holding groups extract value from non-credential-holding groups by controlling access to categorical positions. Opportunity hoarding: credential-holding households restrict access to high-quality pipeline entry through residential sorting, private schooling, and shadow education expenditure. Emulation: non-credential-holding households copy the credential strategies of more successful households regardless of whether the credential actually matches the skill it certifies. Adaptation: household strategies, employer expectations, and institutional structures mutually adjust until the credential is the dominant sorting mechanism. The four mechanisms interlock. Each one makes the others harder to escape.

The shadow education sector is the most visible expression of all four mechanisms operating simultaneously at the household level. Shadow education—private tutoring, cram schools, examination preparation—is not imposed by states or international organizations. It emerges endogenously wherever households observe that marginal credential quality determines life-course outcomes in positional competition. The Daechi-dong hagwon district in Seoul, where private tutoring density produces a secondary schooling system that operates outside and alongside the formal school, represents not a policy failure but the household-level expression of Tilly’s mechanisms under high-enclosure credential competition. Parents are rationally adapting to categorical structures they did not design and cannot individually exit. At the unit level this is adaptation; at the aggregate level it produces the same intensification that looks like opportunity hoarding. This is precisely why critiques attributing shadow education to neoliberal ideological capture miss the explanatory target: the system reproduces itself from the household level up, through rational adaptation, without requiring that anyone hold neoliberal beliefs.

A further mechanism deepens why the pipeline is so sticky. Tilly’s later work on trust networks (2005) identifies a structural logic beneath the durable inequality mechanisms. Trust networks are the closed, high-commitment groups – kinship systems, religious communities, trade diasporas, local guilds – through which people historically placed valued outcomes at risk to others: illness, old age, the raising of children. Tilly defines trust as precisely this: placing valued outcomes at risk to others’ malfeasance. Trust networks manage that vulnerability through dense, long-duration, reputation-embedded relationships. They work because everyone knows everyone.

Mass schooling spread through cities first, where trust networks had already been disrupted by mobility, commercial life, and the breakdown of kin-based transmission. The credential enters as an institutionalized trust shortcut: it allows organizations, employers, and markets to extend commitment across relationships they cannot directly observe, without requiring the face-to-face knowledge that trust networks once provided. What the kin group once communicated through reputation and relationship, the credential communicates categorically to strangers. That is why it must be standardized and legible – trust extended across distance requires a shared language that local knowledge can no longer supply at scale.

The urban-to-rural sequence of pipeline expansion is the structural signature of this substitution. Hong Kong is an unusually compressed and visible instance: missionary schools entered a population already severed from mainland kin networks by colonial commerce; the refugee influx after 1949 severed whatever networks remained; the government universalized education through the 1970s, completing a substitution that demographic disruption had already begun. The result is credential saturation among the highest in the world and the demographic outcomes the trust-substitution mechanism predicts.

Once credentials become the primary language of trust between strangers, categories stabilize around credential access. The sorting, closure, and matching that Tilly describes in Durable Inequality operate through the credential as the categorical device: it enables trust extension while simultaneously producing the categorical order through which exploitation and opportunity hoarding reproduce. The trust function explains why the form must be standardized and legible. The categorical function explains why it is self-enforcing from below.

States are in the business of selling protection – whether their subjects want it or not. In the credential economy, the protection takes a specific form. States create or tolerate the categorical exclusion of non-credentialed labor from economic participation. They then sell the remedy through the pipeline. This is not an indictment. It is a description of how state capacity gets built. The protection racket and the development project are the same operation viewed from different positions. Building the credential pipeline simultaneously builds administrative infrastructure, expands the tax base, and increases the international competitiveness of the state’s labor force. Whether the ruler who builds it understands that sequence in advance matters less than the fact that the sequence runs the same way regardless.

In postcolonial contexts the sequence is compressed and the dependency is visible. Colonial powers built credential architectures that displaced local cultural transmission – Gellner’s exo-socialization – and then departed. Postcolonial states inherited those architectures. They extended them not out of ideological continuity with colonialism but because the functional requirement was the same: surplus capture requires formatted nodes, and formatted nodes require a pipeline. The state that does not build the school gets street kids, illegible labor forces, and administrative incapacity. Not as worst-case scenarios. As structural outcomes of operating without the developmental formatting infrastructure that network economies require. Where states cannot finance the pipeline domestically, the World Bank and bilateral donors provide it, because they have concluded that the alternative is worse for everyone involved. States, households, and donors align on the same instrument for different but mutually reinforcing reasons. That is the surplus motor at multiple levels. It is not legitimacy diffusion.

The historical trigger for massification is urbanization, not industrialization. Gellner tied the credential pipeline to industrial production – the mobile, literate, context-free worker slotting into factory and office nodes. That connection was real for Europe. But deindustrialization has not reversed massification. Countries that never industrialized in Gellner’s sense have massified education as they urbanized. The variable that holds across cases is not industrial output. It is rural-to-urban migration. In 1920 approximately 20% of the world’s population lived in cities. By 1950 that figure had reached 30%. By 2007 it crossed 50% for the first time in human history. By 2026 it stands at approximately 57%, with the fastest growth in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The UN projects 68% urban by 2050. China moved approximately 500 million people from rural to urban settings between 1980 and 2020. That movement, and movements like it, drove massification. The school followed the city.

What accelerated the migration was not only industrial pull but agricultural displacement. Cullather’s (2010) account of the Green Revolution shows how the Cold War project of raising agricultural productivity through high-yield varieties and mechanization – designed to stabilize rural populations and prevent communist insurgency – instead displaced the tenant farmers and smallholders whose labor-intensive agriculture had kept rural populations in place. The Green Revolution intended to keep people on the land. Mechanization moved them off it, into cities that had not yet built the credential architecture to receive them.

The pipeline that absorbs these populations does more than format labor for urban employment. It sculpts the life history of the persons who pass through it. Before mass schooling, puberty marked the transition to adult economic and reproductive roles – the social categories of teenager and emerging adult did not exist as distinct institutional stages. The credential pipeline constructs them: adolescence, extended economic dependency, the prolonged institutional childhood that now stretches into the mid-twenties in high-saturation economies. These are pipeline artifacts, not natural developmental phases. The unformatted person in a city cannot successfully navigate the bureaucratic, credential-gated, stranger-mediated institutions of urban economic life – cannot interface with employment systems that require credential legibility, cannot extend trust to institutions that require categorical proof of formation. The street kid and the ungovernable urban district are not moral failures. They are structural predictions of what happens when large populations are moved off the land before the pipeline has been built to receive them.

Elite schools existed before massification and explain nothing about it. Imperial examination systems, colonial schools, missionary schools in port cities – these formatted small fractions of populations to reproduce administrative elites and interface with external powers. Massification is a categorically different process. It fires when urbanization has moved enough population into cities that the governance and economic cost of leaving large numbers of urban residents unformatted exceeds the cost of building the pipeline at scale. Unformatted populations concentrated in cities generate crime, ungovernable neighborhoods, informal economies the state cannot tax, and recruitment pools for the alternative trust networks – gangs, ethnic criminal associations, informal credit networks – that compete with the state’s own institutional order for the same population. The credential pipeline is partly how the state wins that competition. It offers an alternative path into the urban coordination system that the state controls, taxes, and administers, against the informal networks it cannot reach.

The urban-to-rural sequence of pipeline expansion then follows structurally. Cities format first because that is where the governance problem is acute and where the formal economy can absorb credential holders. Secondary towns follow as the urban economy extends its reach. The countryside is last and is often still being chased – and in many contexts will never be fully covered, not because states lack commitment but because the pipeline faces compounding obstacles in rural areas that do not apply in cities. Intact kin networks compete directly with the credential as a trust and risk-management architecture; the rural household has not yet experienced the trust-network disruption that makes the credential’s legibility valuable. The formal economic absorption capacity for credential holders often does not exist: graduates of rural schools face credential returns near zero in local labor markets, and migration to cities where returns are real requires severing the kin network the credential was supposed to supplement. A third obstacle is state coherence. Where the pipeline has not reached, the state’s presence is typically coercive rather than developmental – soldiers and police rather than teachers and administrators. The household’s primary interface with the state is its extractive face, and investing in a pipeline that the extractive state nominally provides requires trusting an institution whose visible function is taking rather than giving. That trust problem compounds a discount rate problem: rural households must defer certain present costs – foregone child labor, school fees, opportunity costs – for returns that are distant, uncertain, and contingent on a formal economy that may not exist locally when the child graduates. The pipeline is self-sustaining where credential returns are visible and the formal economy is present. Where neither condition holds, it is chasing something it may never fully catch.

## Case Study 1: ASEAN K-12 Alignment and the AQRF

The ASEAN Qualifications Reference Framework (AQRF) provides the clearest available demonstration of the surplus motor in operation at regional scale. Developed by a joint ASEAN, Australia, and New Zealand task force beginning in 2010, the AQRF benchmarks national qualifications frameworks into mutually comparable regional standards across eight levels of competency agreed among all ASEAN member states (ASEAN, 2018). The framework facilitates labor mobility through Mutual Recognition Arrangements covering engineering, nursing, architecture, medicine, dentistry, accountancy, surveying, and tourism.

The stated rationale for AQRF development is unambiguous: it is labor market integration for surplus capture. UNESCO-UNEVOC (2023) documents this directly: the impetus was to support intra-regional trade by facilitating labor market mobility and to fill the skills demands of employers across the region. Regional demand for aligning educational content and qualification standards became a key consideration to promote economic integration among ASEAN member states and the competitiveness of the region as a whole. The framework is explicitly labor-market driven.

The chronology matters here. The Philippine K-12 law, RA 10533, was enacted in 2013 and added Grades 11–12 to the basic education cycle. Formal AQRF endorsement came later: ASEAN economic and education ministers endorsed it in August and September 2014, and labour ministers completed ad-referendum endorsement in May 2015. The enacted AQRF therefore cannot have caused the 2013 reform. The defensible claim is narrower: Philippine reformers appealed to the established international twelve-year cycle, occupational recognition and anticipated ASEAN labour-market integration; the emerging regional qualifications work made future comparability salient, and the later AQRF supplied a common referencing framework. On this reading, K-12 reduced the liability of maintaining an unusual credential architecture, while the AQRF subsequently reduced the transaction cost of comparing qualifications. The timing weakens any simple claim that an already-operative AQRF forced K-12 adoption, but it does not erase the economic coordination problem to which both reforms responded.

The treatment of Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) within the AQRF process is particularly instructive for the scripts-as-coordination-devices argument. RPL—the institutional recognition of learning acquired outside formal education—is a technically demanding and politically contested mechanism that took European systems years to develop and implement within the Bologna Process and European Qualifications Framework. ASEAN working groups did not invent RPL from scratch; they adapted frameworks and language that already existed. This is mimetic diffusion in the conventional sense. But the demand for RPL recognition existed independently of the European template: ASEAN states needed to credential the substantial informal and non-formal learning their labor forces already possessed, in order to make that labor readable in regional markets. The European script reduced the transaction cost of solving a problem the surplus motor had already generated. This is the correct relationship between the two accounts: legitimacy scripts and prestigious templates operate as coordination devices that lower the cost of surplus-driven adoption; they do not create the demand.

The long-run extension of AQRF comparability toward European frameworks—the AQRF-EQF comparability initiative, which maps ASEAN qualification levels against the European Qualifications Framework—is consistent with this reading. The European Qualifications Framework and the Bologna Process are widely recognized reference points for regional qualification frameworks globally, and ASEAN working groups engaged them directly. Mimetic and normative mechanisms were present. What the surplus account contributes is an explanation of why those mechanisms were activated at this moment, in this domain, by states with otherwise very different relationships to European institutional models. The coordination problem of regional labor market legibility existed before the EQF existed. The EQF made solving it cheaper.

## Case Study 2: GPI Inversion in Muslim-Majority States

World culture theory would attribute the inversion of the Gender Parity Index above 1.0 in Muslim-majority states—meaning more women than men enrolled in tertiary education—to the diffusion of global feminist norms and human rights scripts about gender equality. The surplus account attributes it to the credential pipeline asserting its developmental logic against cultural resistance wherever the economic cost of non-adoption exceeds the cultural cost of adoption.

The empirical facts are striking. Female tertiary enrollment exceeds male enrollment in over 100 countries, and the gap has been growing for more than two decades (World Bank, 2024). In the Middle East and North Africa, ten countries have achieved tertiary gender parity, and Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan report over 99% gender parity in overall educational attainment (WEF, 2023). Morocco’s tertiary GPI crossed 1.05 in 2020 (UNESCO, 2020). Iran, despite significant constraints on female professional participation, has seen female tertiary enrollment approach and cross parity. Saudi Arabia has seen substantial increases in female credential acquisition alongside increased labor force participation.

These developments cannot be attributed to feminist norm diffusion. Saudi Arabia did not adopt Western feminist values. Iran did not decouple from Islamic gender ideology when it opened university access to women. Morocco did not undergo a cultural revolution in gender norms when female tertiary GPI crossed 1.0. What happened in each case is that states and households concluded that leaving female cognitive capacity unformatted is economically costly. The credential economy cannot function optimally on half a formatted population when regional and global labor markets are accessible to women. States that restrict female credential access lose taxable output and international economic competitiveness. Households in those states that restrict daughters’ credential access forgo the household income premium that female credentials command.

The case empirically distinguishes the two accounts. World culture theory predicts that feminist norm adoption should precede GPI inversion and that GPI inversion should correlate with broader liberalization of gender norms across domains. The surplus account predicts that GPI inversion can precede broader gender norm liberalization – because the pipeline captures the developmental window before it changes what people believe – and that credential expansion should proceed even in societies that explicitly resist feminist ideology. Iran illustrates: female tertiary enrollment crossed parity despite official resistance to Western feminist scripts, sustained pronatalist policy, and significant constraints on female professional participation. The pipeline’s economic logic overrode the cultural script. World culture theory, which predicts the script drives adoption, has no clean account of this sequence.

## Discussion

The proposed framework does not replace world culture theory. It identifies what world culture theory requires but cannot supply: a mechanism for why the isomorphic form produces the specific outcomes it does. Meyer’s account explains the spread and synchronization of educational institutions. The surplus account explains why states and households adopt those institutions even without legitimacy pressure, and why the adoption runs deep enough to produce biological and cognitive consequences that cannot be attributed to ceremonial compliance. The two accounts operate at different stages of the causal chain: legitimacy lowers the political cost of pipeline construction; surplus explains its persistence, calibration, and deepening.

The complementarity is most visible in the ASEAN case: world culture theory correctly predicts that ASEAN states will converge on comparable educational structures; the surplus account explains why they do so cooperatively, driven by the labor market integration imperative rather than world polity authorization, and why the specific calibration – the eight AQRF levels, the Philippines K-12 extension, the RPL frameworks – is negotiated against regional economic requirements rather than against a legitimacy script.

The competition is sharpest in the GPI inversion case: world culture theory attributes GPI inversion to feminist norm diffusion and predicts correlation with broader cultural liberalization. The surplus account attributes it to the credential pipeline’s economic logic and predicts that pipeline capture of the female developmental window can precede cultural liberalization – which is what the Iran case shows.

The credential pipeline persists even where the microeconomic surplus logic weakens, and Tilly’s mechanisms explain why. Individual defection from the credential system is catastrophic: non-credentialed labor faces categorical exclusion, not merely lower wages. Collective action to dismantle or replace the system faces the exact coordination problems Tilly’s four mechanisms systematically reproduce. Credential-holding groups have material interests in maintaining the barrier. Non-credential-holding households rationally emulate rather than organize. Employers and state institutions adapt their infrastructure around the credential, making alternatives structurally unavailable. The system becomes stable below its surplus floor – held in place by coercion and coordination failure even when returns compress.

The three-value taxonomy explains why this floor is more durable than positional analysis alone predicts. When credential saturation collapses the positional premium – when everyone has the BA and it no longer sorts anyone relative to competitors – the positional account predicts the pipeline should weaken. It does not weaken. The reason is relational value. Once the credential becomes the language of trust between strangers in the urban economy, not having one does not mean losing the race. It means being illegible – invisible to the formal institutions that organize employment, credit, housing, and migration. The floor is not a positional floor. It is a floor of relational inclusion. Dropping below it is not ranking lower in a queue; it is dropping out of the conversation that queue is embedded in. Hong Kong illustrates this precisely. After 2019, the state lost political legitimacy across large portions of the population. Many households rejected what the government represented. Positional competition for elite placements intensified as opportunities contracted. The pipeline ran anyway. It ran because the DSE was still the language employers, universities, and migration authorities used to read applicants. The state administers the token. The network of institutions honors it. As long as that network held – and it did – the state could be hated and the pipeline would still run.

Credential inflation extends this logic. At high saturation the credential stops functioning as a trust signal because everyone in the relevant hiring pool holds one. Job allocation reverts to network access – who you know, which internship, which institution. Households respond by investing more intensively in elite positioning: premium institutions, shadow education, extracurriculars. This drives credential inflation upward – the BA becomes the entry floor, the Masters the new BA – not because more skill is being certified but because the positional competition for network access requires a longer pipeline to differentiate above the floor. Tilly’s opportunity hoarding operates at both levels: the credential gates access to the labor market, and elite institutional credentials gate access to the networks that actually allocate positions within it.

A limitation the paper states plainly: the developmental effects of schooling are real and durable but not uniformly positive by any welfare standard. Entorf and Dohmen (2025) find that extended compulsory education decreases emotional stability, grit, and patience while increasing openness to experience and consequential thinking. A French compulsory schooling extension improved cognitive scores in men but increased depressive symptoms in women. The pipeline installs a trait profile suited to institutional life that is not identical to human flourishing. The argument for the form’s persistence is structural, not normative.

## Conclusion

Population-scale institutional enclosure of the developmental window is historically recent. For most of human history, developmental formatting happened through apprenticeship, kinship transmission, and local practice. Elite institutions – the imperial examination hall, the madrasa, the guild apprenticeship – formatted small fractions of each population. The systematic capture of the first two decades of every child’s life inside a standardized evaluative institution is a twentieth-century achievement, still incomplete, and its consequences are still unfolding.

This paper has argued that explaining why the form that emerged looks so similar everywhere requires three things that the dominant traditions in comparative education have not yet integrated. A biological anchor: the extended human developmental window is a shared species parameter, and institutions that exploit it systematically produce convergent developmental outcomes across otherwise dissimilar populations. A structural motor: states and households both want surplus – more taxable output, better lives – and the internationally legible credential is the available instrument for both, generating isomorphism through parallel problem-solving rather than script diffusion. A developmental mechanism: sustained structured engagement during the plastic window appears to induce durable neurocognitive reorganization, and the form that wins the selection competition is the one that scales that process while emitting tokens legible to distant strangers.

The ASEAN case demonstrates the surplus motor operating through cooperative standardization driven by labor market integration. The GPI inversion in Muslim-majority states demonstrates the pipeline asserting its developmental logic against cultural resistance. Together they show that the pressure toward isomorphism is structural and demand-side rather than ideological and supply-side.

The non-cynical core of the argument is this: schools do something real and largely irreplaceable at population scale. The alternative to institutional developmental formatting is not some more authentic or natural form of human development – it is the limited and unequal developmental opportunity that characterized most of human history, and the specific brutality of being an unformatted person in a city that runs on credential legibility. The form that emerged is often pedagogically crude, developmentally costly, and selected for legibility rather than learning. It installs behavioral profiles suited to institutional life that are not identical to human flourishing by any culturally neutral measure. These facts coexist. Understanding why the form persists requires understanding what it does, not just what it signals.
