Production, coordination and who gets the surplus

Based on Sarah Klassen, Scott G. Ortman, José Lobo and Damian Evans, Provisioning an Early City: Spatial Equilibrium in the Agricultural Economy at Angkor, Cambodia, published online in 2021, journal volume 2022, pages 763–794. Read the paper ↗

What was the economy?

The paper describes an agricultural urban system: households and temple communities occupied the same developed landscape as fields, ponds, reservoirs and routes. Local communities coordinated production, often under elite ownership, and contributed to state resources. The civic-ceremonial core included people doing work beyond farming, so it depended on surplus from that landscape. The paper also recognizes workshops, household gardens, fish and other resources. A temple community is a productive and institutional unit; the stone shrine alone is not the entire economy.

Its accounting interpretation places household subsistence first, state transfers next, then local temple activity and building from remaining proceeds. This is the authors’ analytical interpretation, not a surviving universal tax schedule. The paper explicitly leaves the distribution of gains unresolved: coercive elite capture and more communal shared benefits could both produce its aggregate patterns.

What this browser model reproduces

Community output follows Y = Y₀Nᵝ. Table 5 reports β = 1.227, SE 0.023; settlement scaling theory supplies 7/6 as a comparator. A constant-productivity comparison uses β = 1. The plotted per-person index is 100 × (N/100)^(β−1). For a fixed illustrative population divided into equal communities, total production has the same relative index. This comparison assumes the fitted relationship transfers to regrouping; it does not establish a causal consolidation policy.

The paper’s output measure is the product of archaeological proxies for labour, land and irrigation water. It is not measured tonnes of rice. Because population also enters the constructed outcome, the regression warrants particular care as evidence for a causal productivity effect. The Atlas shows the relationship without converting it to a calibrated grain budget.

Separate distance cards normalize Table 5’s log–log slopes to 100 at 10 km: output/hectare = 100 × (d/10)^−0.846; reservoir volume/hectare = 100 × (d/10)^−0.409; land/person = 100 × (d/10)^0.521. Distance is straight-line distance from Bayon. These are separate associations, not coefficients of one multivariable production function. The original distance regressions explain only 2.6–5.1% of variance.

Temple-platform volume has an estimated elasticity of 0.085 with respect to the production index. The authors interpret net proceeds in percentage terms, around 8%. A log–log regression slope by itself does not identify a constant share of output, especially across different proxy units. We retain its mathematical meaning and do not impose an 8.5% tax, profit or relief rule.

Did temples insure households against famine?

That is a distinct hypothesis. This paper provides no calibrated rule for storing grain, releasing it during a shortage, identifying recipients or enforcing an obligation to provide relief. Eileen Lustig’s 2009 thesis, section 4.6.2, questions the evidence for large-scale redistribution back to producers. Her discussion reports no epigraphic or archaeological evidence then identified for the proposed temple granaries and notes selective documentation of temple rations. This is a dated assessment, not a claim that future evidence cannot change it.

Terry and Eileen Lustig (2015) analyze food allowances and show how incomplete and difficult the accounts are. Records of endowments, offerings and personnel support establish some provisioning arrangements; those records do not establish universal bad-year protection for farmers.

A future insurance comparison would declare contributions, a finite reserve, storage losses, shortfall-triggered releases, eligibility and competing ritual/state claims. Collective welfare and elite retention would be rival rules under matched harvests. None of those rules is attributed to this paper or silently added to the current model.

What “charter state” means here

Victor Lieberman’s Charter State Collapse in Southeast Asia, ca. 1250–1400, as a Problem in Regional and World History (2011) uses the term for early extensive indigenous polities that provided political and territorial templates for successors, including Pagan, Angkor and Đại Việt. “Charter” describes that formative legacy. It does not mean a modern charter city, nor does it establish a particular famine-relief contract.

The comparative charter-state argument concerns the formation and fragmentation of major political orders. The moral-economy hypothesis concerns obligations and distribution within them. The provisioning paper concerns how productive organization and location could support urban populations. These can be compared together while keeping their claims and evidence separate.

Atlas contribution and checks

The mathematical relationships and table come from the cited paper. This is an implementation of published associations, with no re-estimation from the underlying archaeological records. Separate dynamic Petri lab: 3,072 runs

Open the dynamic extension ↗. We ran two climate scenarios × six mechanism settings × 256 matched weather seeds. The paper supplies the coordination exponent; every stock, dynamic rate, insurance rule and shock schedule is an Atlas extension. Model years are elapsed time, without historical dates. A fixed 500-person community gains a multiplier of 5^0.227 relative to 100-person communities. Applying that association causally is an explicit hypothesis.

The state variables are rural residents, core residents, developed land, water condition, monuments and finite grain stores. Harvest is land × 1.5 × coordination × hydraulic condition × climate × available farm labour. Gross extraction funds core consumption, ritual, maintenance, reserves, reclamation and new building in that order. Household shortfalls can draw reserves. Growth responds to food margins and hunger; migration transfers people between the two residential stocks. Rates are inspectable in the engine and remain uncalibrated.

With all mechanisms, the economy grows, suffers drought setbacks and recovers by the horizon. Removing coordination produces persistent decline in this scenario. Removing relief slightly increases the food gap. Removing temple obligations improves food security. Removing hydraulic dependence creates a stronger economy because it also removes the model’s water-related production penalty; it is not evidence that real irrigation was harmful. The knockout design exposes the assumptions rather than confirming an Angkor collapse theory.

Every annual grain ledger balanced within 10⁻¹⁰ normalized units. This verifies arithmetic, not historical validity. The 256 seeds vary small weather noise, not all uncertain parameters.
