POPULATION & SCHOOL SYSTEMS · FERTILITY · EXPLORATORY

Why is fertility collapsing?

Six places — the Philippines, Thailand, South Korea, Japan, Hong Kong and China — tested with development models, mechanism simulations and a tipping model. The headline claim fails the trend check. Life expectancy and fertility are strongly related in levels. Changes in one do not predict changes in the other. No place is cointegrated over 1961–2024 at the 5% level; only Japan passes, from 1964. The 1.3–1.5 floor is a fitted curve, not a finding. The cohort and tipping labs are illustrations with invented parameters, not estimates. A post-2015 period dummy fits the timing but cannot separate smartphones, COVID or economic conditions. The credential race and marriage squeeze appear only in simulations.

The story in four acts

REAL DATA · LEVELS ONLY

1 · Survival changes everything

Once children survive and people live long lives, big families stop being necessary. Life expectancy tracks each place's fall in levels. It fails the trend check: changes in life expectancy do not predict changes in fertility, and no place is cointegrated over 1961–2024 at 5%. Held out one place at a time, the average error is 0.40 children (1985–2024), ranging from 0.24 to 0.81.

SIMULATED

2 · Children become credentials

Mass higher education turns childrearing into an arms race. Each child costs more, so parents stop at one. In the cohort model (an illustration with invented parameters) this costs about 0.4 children in every regime, through fewer births per married couple.

SIMULATED · NOT YET PROVEN

3 · The marriage market jams

Where births need marriage, women overtaking men in status leaves many unmatched. It is the biggest single pressure in marriage-bound societies and shrinks by about 70% where births outside marriage are common. National enrolment data cannot confirm it.

REAL DATA

4 · Post-2015 period dummy

From 2015 to 2024, all six places fell further below their development path, by about 0.46 children on average. A post-2015 period dummy fits better than country-to-country spread. The dummy cannot separate smartphones, COVID or economic conditions; all three fit the timing.

Tipping lab: can a temporary shock do permanent damage?

People adopt a “small family, no rush to marry” life plan once social pressure passes their personal threshold. Part of the pressure comes from peers who already have. A shock runs in years 30–39, then stops. Policy can push back from year 45. Illustration with invented parameters.

Methods, limits and downloads

Data: World Bank fertility, GDP and life expectancy via FRED, 1961–2024; female-to-male tertiary enrolment ratios (sparse). Models are fitted leaving one place out. Period TFR mixes timing with quantity; Korea's gap includes about 0.2 children of postponement. The levels and first-difference trend check, with Engle-Granger tests, is in the project repository under experiments/fertility-trend-check.

Six places is a small sample. Leave-one-out on six places rests on six held-out errors, so the spread is not a confidence interval. Mechanism and tipping results come from synthetic models with invented parameters, and some conclusions are partly built into how they are coded. The eight development-family models behave like 1.33 independent models; a real model crowd needs new kinds of data, above all who stays unmarried by sex and education.
