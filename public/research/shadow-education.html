Why do pupils pay their own teachers?

In Cambodia, about four in five secondary pupils buy private tutoring, and most of it comes from their own teachers. An agent-based model of 72 teachers and 1,260 pupils tests three rival explanations against seven published patterns. All three fit about equally well, so the data cannot tell them apart. But all three agree on what policies would do, and that agreement is the useful result.

Three rival explanations

THIN TIMETABLE

The school day is too short

The curriculum does not fit the official hours, so pupils buy the rest. Teachers gain nothing by holding content back.

DIVERSION

Teachers hold content back

Teachers withhold parts of the regular lesson that they then cover only in paid sessions.

FAVOUR

Buying buys goodwill

Paying your own teacher brings a benefit of its own: better grades or a better relationship.

Methods, limits and downloads

Six Siem Reap–style schools, Grades 9 and 12, four subjects. Each teacher chooses whether to tutor, how much to withhold, or to take a side job. Each pupil chooses, subject by subject, between their own teacher, the parallel-class teacher, a commercial provider or no one. Calibrated to participation (81.9%; 74.7% in Grade 9, 89.8% in Grade 12), the urban–rural gap, own-teacher (57.7%) and other-teacher (40.9%) shares, about half of teachers tutoring, and a small commercial share.

Limits: the favour calibration ended with a low effective sample size; ABC model choice is fragile; policy effects are model-conditional, not evidence of real-world effects. “Coverage” is the model's share of curriculum received, not measured learning. Tutoring is not yet linked to household work or dropout.
