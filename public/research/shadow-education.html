Why do pupils pay their own teachers?

In Cambodia, about four in five secondary pupils buy private tutoring, and most of it comes from their own teachers. An agent-based model of 72 teachers and 1,260 pupils tests three rival explanations against seven published patterns. All three fit about equally well, so the data cannot tell them apart. All three agree on what policies would do, but only because they share most of their code, so that agreement is not evidence.

Three rival explanations

THIN TIMETABLE

The school day is too short

The curriculum does not fit the official hours, so pupils buy the rest. Teachers gain nothing by holding content back.

DIVERSION

Teachers hold content back

Teachers withhold parts of the regular lesson that they then cover only in paid sessions.

FAVOUR

Buying buys goodwill

Paying your own teacher brings a benefit of its own: better grades or a better relationship.

Who leaves school? The credential-floor test

A separate Atlas model offers three explanations for who leaves school, each predicting a different share of lower-secondary non-completers from the poorest 40% of households. UIS completion rates by wealth quintile (from the Cambodia DHS 2021–22) put that share at 55–64%.

StructurePredicted share from poorest 40% (median, 5–95%)Verdict against 55–64%

With a hard liquidity threshold no structure fits the full wealth profile, and the relational floor fails. With a graded threshold all three fit and predict the same share, so this test cannot separate them. Child weights per quintile are assumed; the DHS microdata would remove that assumption.

Methods, limits and downloads

Six Siem Reap–style schools, Grades 9 and 12, four subjects. Each teacher chooses whether to tutor, how much to withhold, or to take a side job. Each pupil chooses, subject by subject, between their own teacher, the parallel-class teacher, a commercial provider or no one. Calibrated to participation (81.9%; 74.7% in Grade 9, 89.8% in Grade 12), the urban–rural gap, own-teacher (57.7%) and other-teacher (40.9%) shares, about half of teachers tutoring, and a small commercial share.

Limits: the evidence ratios rest on a handful of fitting proposals. The three explanations share all but 6 of about 215 lines of model code, so their agreement on policy is not independent evidence. Policy effects are model-conditional, not evidence of real-world effects. “Coverage” is the model's share of curriculum received, not measured learning. A dropout and household-work channel exists behind a flag but is not calibrated.
