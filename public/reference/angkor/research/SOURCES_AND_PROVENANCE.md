# Sources, conceptual borrowings, and evidence grades

This is a reading map for the research hypotheses, **not a claim that every numerical parameter in the code was estimated from these works**. Titles and author-year references below identify source families for further verification. The program contains no source-linked empirical dataset.

## Direct Angkor evidence and syntheses

1. **Carter, Alison K., et al. (2021).** “The evolution of urbanism at Angkor.” *Science Advances* 7, eabf8441. Regional demographic reconstruction and time-varying urban development. Supports broad population-scale discussion, subject to uncertainty in sampling and interpolation.
2. **Klassen, Sarah, and Damian Evans (2020).** Research on the organization and changing structure of Angkor's agricultural landscape and water management. Relevant to elite estate consolidation and locally organized agricultural communities. Confirm exact article metadata before formal publication citation.
3. **Hawken, Scott (2013).** Research mapping Angkor's rice fields and agrarian landscape. Relevant to the magnitude and structure of field bunds and agricultural space. Confirm exact article/dissertation edition for citation.
4. **Fletcher, Roland, et al. (2008).** “The water management network of Angkor, Cambodia.” *Antiquity* 82: 658–670. Relevant to the engineered water system and its development.
5. **Buckley, Brendan M., et al. (2010).** “Climate as a contributing factor in the demise of Angkor, Cambodia.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 107: 6748–6752. Drought and climatic variability as one contributing pressure, not a deterministic collapse proof.
6. **Zhou Daguan, *Zhenla Fengtu Ji* (1296–1297 visit; later editions/translations).** Primary eyewitness account of Angkorian markets, society and unfreedom. A single outsider's description, not a census or estate ledger.
7. **Lustig and Lustig**, work on Angkorian inscriptions and rotating service groups organized by lunar fortnights. Useful for the labor-calendar hypothesis; confirm the specific publication before a formal citation.
8. **Ta Prohm foundation inscription (late twelfth century).** Institutional personnel and provisioning figures. Figures refer to categories of support and should not be converted directly into resident populations.

## Comparative urbanism, water and political organization

9. **Lucero, Lisa J., Roland Fletcher, and Robin Coningham (2015).** “From ‘collapse’ to urban diaspora: the transformation of low-density, dispersed agrarian urbanism.” *Antiquity* 89: 1139–1154. Comparative framework including Tikal, Angkor and Anuradhapura.
10. **Lansing, J. Stephen, and James N. Kremer (1993).** “Emergent properties of Balinese water temple networks: coadaptation on a rugged fitness landscape.” *American Anthropologist* 95: 97–114. Decentralized water coordination mechanism; not evidence that Angkor had an identical system.
11. **Wolters, O. W. (1982).** *History, Culture, and Region in Southeast Asian Perspectives*. Mandala-style political authority and regional relationships. The mandala is a heuristic, not a single uniform constitutional system.
12. **Tambiah, Stanley J. (1976).** *World Conqueror and World Renouncer*. Comparative Southeast Asian kingship and galactic polity framework.
13. **Tilly, Charles (1990).** *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990*. War-making and extraction as conceptual analogy, not a direct Khmer source.
14. **Scott, James C. (2017).** *Against the Grain*. Grain legibility, coercion and early states; debated general theory, not proof of Angkor's institutional structure.
15. **Aung-Thwin, Michael A. (1985).** *Pagan: The Origins of Modern Burma*. Religious institutions, land and political economy in Bagan; relevant comparative mechanisms.

## Labor, ritual and monumental comparisons

16. **Patterson, Orlando (1982).** *Slavery and Social Death*. Framework for comparing legally and socially distinct systems of bondage. Khmer dependency should not automatically be equated with Roman chattel slavery.
17. **Scheidel, Walter**, demographic scholarship on Roman slave reproduction; useful for testing captive supply versus natural reproduction, with ongoing historiographical disagreement.
18. **Historical studies of Spanish-period Philippine haciendas**, including religious estates in Laguna and sugar estates in Negros. This is a *comparative prompt*, not evidence of identical Khmer plantations; identify specific archive and author before formal citation.
19. **German Archaeological Institute archaeological research at Göbekli Tepe.** Recent work on settlement and domestic activity cautions against describing it solely as a seasonal ceremonial camp before agriculture.
20. **UNESCO World Heritage documentation** for Angkor, Bagan, My Son, Sukhothai and the Citadel of the Ho Dynasty. Useful for basic descriptions of monumental forms and regional comparisons, not detailed economic calibration.

## Evidence-grade key

- **Observed archaeological feature:** documented moat, wall, field bund, pond, temple, excavated habitation, inscription.
- **Published reconstruction:** regional population, rice-field extent, chronology, hydraulic function, demographic inference.
- **Cross-cultural analogy:** Bali, Rome, Egypt, Mesopotamia, Philippines, Göbekli Tepe, Bagan.
- **Working hypothesis:** capturable productive capital, endogenous military competition, religious prestige feedback, labor/calendar coordination.
- **Software assumption:** exact coefficient, random distribution, military cost, fertility response, population growth rate, appropriation fraction, conflict probability.

Only the first two categories provide direct constraints on historical Angkor, and even those have varying uncertainty. The code's numerical rules overwhelmingly belong to the final category.
