## Prespecified rules

- **demand**: mean of the last 12 seasonally adjusted months (2022)
- **graduates**: 2023 as announced (11.58m); then +mean annual change 2020-23 per year
- **tertiary_entrants**: held at 2022
- **property**: level held at end-2022
- **seasonal_adjustment**: calendar-month means over 2018-01..2022-12 only

## Why it fails: graduate wage speed (sensitivity, not a forecast)

With demand held flat after 2022, the strict model still has graduate job-finding nearly double in 2023: the 2022 graduate surge is absorbed as graduate wages catch up. The fitted wage speed lam_g (0.142 per month) is weakly identified by 2018-22. Refitting with lam_g held slower:

| lam_g | 2018-22 fit MAE | 2023 H1 MAE | New-method MAE | New-method bias |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.005 | 1.43 | 3.90 | 3.22 | +0.49 |
| 0.01 | 1.46 | 4.54 | 4.12 | -3.57 |
| 0.03 | 1.54 | 5.84 | 4.70 | -4.66 |

In-sample fit barely changes while the forecast error roughly halves, so the forecast is decided by a parameter the fitting window cannot pin down. Choosing lam_g from this table would use the test data. The honest route is an outside measure of graduate starting-wage adjustment (MyCOS starting salaries, TODO §1 P2) fixed before the forecast is scored. Even the best row loses to naive on 2023 H1.

## Reading it

The new-method naive benchmark compares a 2022 old-method value with a new-method observation; the two definitions differ (searching students counted vs excluded), and the model applies the right observation rule while the naive forecast cannot. That asymmetry favours the model, so a model that still loses to naive has failed cleanly. Old/new definition-compatible benchmarks need a published overlap series, which NBS has not released.
