# Evidence cards for the child-labour intervention simulator

**Seven papers, seven cards.** 6 November 2026. Prepared for `TODO.md` §10 ("Evidence cards with effect sizes extracted from the papers") and lane task A8. The simulator is `web/childlabour.js` (stylised household model, four states: school, school-and-work, work, neither; levers in `POLICY0`); its `EVIDENCE` array already lists the expected signs. These cards supply the magnitudes behind those signs, state what each paper can and cannot pin, and flag what could not be verified.

**How to read a card.** Each card gives: (1) citation with verified DOI/URL and the version read; (2) setting and data; (3) intervention and comparison; (4) outcome definition; (5) effect size with units and, where reported, standard error/CI; (6) identification strategy; (7) threats and external validity; (8) what it pins in `web/childlabour.js`, naming the lever or parameter, or why it cannot pin it.

**Verification standard.** Every citation was resolved on 6 November 2026 (Crossref DOI lookup, OpenAlex/Crossref metadata, publisher abstract, or the working-paper PDF itself). The effect sizes below were read from the paper text — the published abstract where the full text is paywalled, and the full text of the openly available working-paper version otherwise. Where published and working-paper versions disagree, both are reported and the difference is labelled. Nothing below is recalled from memory. One paper (Kruger 2007) could not be read beyond its abstract; its magnitudes are explicitly marked **UNCONFIRMED**.

**Version note.** Four of the seven have an openly available working-paper version whose numbers can differ from the published article: Edmonds & Schady (NBER WP 15345, 2009 → AEJ: Policy 2012), Beegle, Dehejia & Gatti (NBER WP 10088, 2003 → JDE 2006), Atkin (NBER WP 18266, 2012 rev. 2015 → AER 2016), Edmonds & Theoharides (NBER WP 26190, 2019 → JDE 2020). The card names which version produced each number.

## Card 1 — de Hoop & Rosati (2014), cash transfers and child labour: the review

**Citation.** de Hoop, J., & Rosati, F. C. (2014). Cash transfers and child labor. *The World Bank Research Observer, 29*(2), 202–234. <https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lku003>. Working-paper version read in full: de Hoop, J., & Rosati, F. C. (2014). *Cash Transfers and Child Labor* (Policy Research Working Paper No. 6826). World Bank. <https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-6826> (open access PDF).

**Setting and data.** A systematic review of quantitative evaluations, not a new experiment. Primary analysis: 30 studies — 7 of unconditional cash transfer (UCT) schemes and 23 of conditional cash transfer (CCT) schemes; 23 of the 30 are from Latin America and the Caribbean, 5 study Mexico's Oportunidades alone; more than half are peer-reviewed and 14 are RCT-based. Outcomes cover participation in economic activity and hours worked; the age ranges differ across studies and the authors flag this.

**Intervention and comparison.** CCT and UCT programmes versus eligible non-recipients, across the included studies. The review also discusses old-age pensions as a credit-constraint test (unanticipated vs anticipated income streams), and effects on household chores and school participation.

**Outcome definition.** Child labour = participation in economic activity (and, where available, hours worked); some studies separate work for pay, farm/business work and domestic chores. The review notes that hours measures set hours to zero for non-working children, so they combine the extensive and intensive margins.

**Effect size (with units).** No pooled meta-analytic effect size or pooled standard error is reported; the review reports study-level estimates and a synthesis. Verified statements:
- "We find no evidence that cash transfer interventions increase child labor in practice"; broadly, CCTs and UCTs lower both participation and hours.
- CCT participation effects: "Impact estimates range from no statistically significant change in eight of the 16 studies to a reduction in child labor of 10 percentage points for Cambodia's CESSP scholarship program (Ferreira, Filmer, and Schady 2010)." No study finds a statistically significant increase.
- Hours: "reductions in hours worked are modest (the average reduction is approximately one hour and a half a week)."
- Cross-programme association: "Each percentage point increase in school participation is associated with a reduction in child labor of 0.31 percentage points" (significantly different from −1, i.e. school gains are not fully mirrored in work reductions).
- UCT examples: Ecuador's Bono de Desarrollo Humano "lowered children's participation in economic activities by 17 percentage points for 6- to 17-year-old children (Schady and Araujo 2006) and by 25 percentage points for 11- to 16-year-old children (Edmonds and Schady 2012)"; Brazil's rural pension: +100 Reais (~US$50) benefit raised girls' school enrolment by nearly 10 pp and reduced girls' paid work by 3.6 pp.
- Gender: "Boys experience particularly strong decreases in economic activities, girls in household chores."

**Identification strategy.** Review of identification in the primary studies: the 14 RCTs plus quasi-experimental designs (propensity score matching, difference-in-differences, IV using randomised eligibility). Table 1 of the WPS describes each study's design.

**Threats and external validity.** Heterogeneous outcome definitions and age bands; likely publication bias against null results (the authors do not claim to test it formally); mostly Latin American CCTs, so applicability to low-income, low-enrolment Asian settings (Cambodia) is limited; short follow-ups; transfers are usually modest relative to child earnings; effects are conditional on the household being credit-constrained or near subsistence. The review cannot separate income from substitution/conditionality effects structurally.

**How to use in the simulator.** This card sets the **sign prior and the plausible envelope**, not a structural parameter. In `web/childlabour.js` it supports the `EVIDENCE` entries for `cct` (sign −1) and `uct` (sign −1), and it bounds what a calibrated run should produce: participation effects up to about −10 pp (with most studies between 0 and −10 pp), hours effects of the order of −1.5 hours/week, and a school–work linkage weaker than one-for-one (the simulator's four-state utility already allows school gains without work reductions, which is the right structure). The 0.31-pp-per-pp association is a useful **cross-check on the simulated school/work correlation**, not a target for a single run: a simulation in which every pp of school gain removes 1 pp of work is too tightly coupled. The gender result (boys' economic activity, girls' chores) **cannot** be represented: the simulator has no chores state (`neither` lumps domestic work with idleness), so the girls' channel is unmodelled.

## Card 2 — Edmonds & Schady (2012), Ecuador's Bono de Desarrollo Humano

**Citation.** Edmonds, E. V., & Schady, N. (2012). Poverty alleviation and child labor. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 4*(4), 100–124. <https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.4.4.100>. Working-paper version read in full: NBER Working Paper No. 15345 (September 2009). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w15345>. World Bank version: Policy Research Working Paper No. 4702. <https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-4702>.

**Setting and data.** Ecuador; the Bono de Desarrollo Humano (BDH), an unconditional cash transfer paid to mothers. Poor women with children were selected at random for the experiment; follow-up covers 2,153 children observed at baseline and follow-up. The transfer is US$15 per household per month — 7 percent of monthly expenditures for recipient households — and does not vary with family size. It is paid to mothers and carries no conditions. Transfer size is less than 20 percent of median child-labour earnings (median child wage US$80 per month). Read at full text (NBER 2009); published abstract read for AEJ version.

**Intervention and comparison.** Lottery winners (households selected at random to receive the BDH) versus lottery losers; because of leakage and non-take-up (the WP notes 39 percent of the control population received the BDH and 32 percent of the treatment group did not), assignment is used as an instrument for receipt. The WP also restricts to "pre-transition" children — in school and not in paid work at baseline.

**Outcome definition.** "Child labour" here is **work in paid employment outside the child's home** (not farm or family work). The AEJ published version also reports unpaid economic activity inside the home and time in unpaid household services. This matters: the headline percentage reduction is relative to a low baseline of paid work, not to total child work.

**Effect size (with units).** Two versions, with different magnitudes:
- *NBER WP 2009 (full text, Table 2):* reduced form −2.2 percentage points (SE 1.5) on the probability a child 10+ at baseline works for pay; IV estimate for BDH receipt −7.0 pp (SE 4.7). Baseline paid-work rate among lottery losers was 17 percent, so the IV estimate is a 41 percent relative reduction. Neither the RF nor the IV estimate is significant at the 10 percent level in this version. For pre-transition children, the IV estimate is −11.8 pp (SE 5.3), i.e. "a 12 percentage point decline in child labor" (significant at 5 percent). The WP reports declines significant only for baseline per capita expenditures below US$348 per person per year (32.5 percent of the sample).
- *AEJ: Policy 2012 (published abstract):* "Students in families induced to take up the transfer by the experiment reduce paid employment by 78 percent and unpaid economic activity inside their home by 32 percent. Time in unpaid household services increases, but overall time spent working declines."
- *Published version as tabulated by a later review:* de Hoop & Rosati (2014, WPS 6826, Figure 2 and Table 1) record Edmonds & Schady (2012) as a household-level RCT for ages **11–16**, with the outcome "economic activities for pay or for household [enterprise]", and an average program effect of **−25 percentage points**. This is the pp figure to use if a single number is needed; it is consistent with the abstract's −78 percent if the relevant control baseline is about 32 percent (25 ÷ 0.78 ≈ 32), but that correspondence is an inference, not a quotation.
- **Version reconciliation (flagged, not resolved):** the NBER 2009 WP measures work for pay away from home for children 10+ (IV −7.0 pp on a 17 percent baseline ≈ −41 percent); the published AEJ 2012 version measures economic activities for pay or household enterprise for ages 11–16, where the effect is −25 pp (≈ −78 percent of paid employment). The two are not the same estimand. **Do not average them or treat either as the model's single target**; use the WP figure for the broad 10+ paid-work margin and the published figure for the older-child/ broader-activity margin. The AEJ full text was not accessible in this session, so the exact sample and specification behind the −25 pp figure are unconfirmed.

**Identification strategy.** Random assignment to the BDH lottery (the evaluation used a randomised lottery among eligible poor households); parish fixed effects; controls for baseline characteristics; IV using lottery assignment interacted with baseline characteristics; robustness to attrition (94.1 percent re-interviewed).

**Threats and external validity.** (i) The outcome is paid employment outside the home only, so the paper speaks to the extensive margin of market work, not to family-farm work or chores; (ii) the effect is concentrated in families with a single school-age child and is not statistically significant in the full sample of the WP; (iii) the transfer is small relative to child earnings, so the result is evidence about *why families send children to paid work* (subsistence/precautionary rather than pure income maximisation), not about large transfers; (iv) Ecuador's poor, two-fifths-of-population sample and its labour market limit external validity; (v) leakages and mistaken beliefs about conditionality complicate interpretation (the paper argues the results are not driven by the latter).

**How to use in the simulator.** This is the best available anchor for the **`uct` lever** and for the luxury-axiom kink (`C.subsist`, the Stone–Geary `0.6 × need` threshold in `util`). In model units the transfer is `uct ≈ 0.07` (7 percent of household income) — the same relative size as the BDH. Two design implications:
1. Because `earnFull ≈ 0.32` for a 15+ child and direct school costs are 5–12 percent of income, a 7 percent unconditional transfer is small relative to a teenager's earnings but can be decisive for a household just below the subsistence kink. The simulator will reproduce the paper's central qualitative finding — effects concentrated among the poorest and among households with little "slack" — only if `subsist` is set close to the income distribution's lower tail. Calibration target: choose the target margin explicitly. On the WP's broad 10+ paid-work margin, expect a low-single-digit pp average effect; on the published 11–16 "economic activities for pay or household enterprise" margin, the evidence points to roughly −25 pp for the affected group. Do not force both into one number, and do not feed the 78 percent in as a probability-point target.
2. The published 78 percent is a relative effect on a paid-work baseline for a selected (older/affected) group; the model's `work` state includes family enterprise and market work, so the comparable object is a pp change among children of the matching age, not the paper's headline.

## Card 3 — Filmer & Schady (2008), Cambodia's JFPR scholarships for girls

**Citation.** Filmer, D., & Schady, N. (2008). Getting girls into school: Evidence from a scholarship program in Cambodia. *Economic Development and Cultural Change, 56*(3), 581–617. <https://doi.org/10.1086/533548>. Working-paper version read in full: World Bank Policy Research Working Paper No. 3910 (May 2006). <https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-3910>.

**Setting and data.** Cambodia; the Japan Fund for Poverty Reduction (JFPR) scholarship program. 93 lower-secondary schools selected; approximately 45 girls beginning 7th grade per school awarded scholarships of US$45 each; the program covered about 15 percent of Cambodia's 698 lower-secondary schools in 2003/04. Data: scholarship application forms (socioeconomic status, parental education, distance, assets), plus an unannounced school visit to establish enrollment and attendance for 8th-grade girls; enrollment status established for 95.2 percent of applicants (4.8 percent attrition). Read at full text (WPS 2006).

**Intervention and comparison.** Scholarship recipients versus non-recipients among applicants, at the same schools. The "scholarship" is in fact a **conditional cash transfer**: families receive cash provided the daughter is enrolled, maintains a passing grade and is absent without good reason fewer than 10 days a year. It is automatically renewable for the three years of lower secondary. Selection was by a composite socioeconomic score, with roughly the 45 lowest-scoring girls per school receiving scholarships.

**Outcome definition.** Three outcomes: enrolment at the JFPR school the girl applied to; attendance at that school on the day of an unannounced visit; enrolment at any school (JFPR or otherwise). Baseline application-form variables used as controls.

**Effect size (with units).** Preferred estimates: program effects on enrolment and attendance **at program schools of 30 to 43 percentage points**; recipients also more likely to be enrolled at any school by **22 to 33 percentage points**. RD estimates in the preferred specification: **30 pp on enrolment** and **43.6 pp on attendance**, with "much larger" standard errors. Robustness examples with standard errors reported: enrolment effect 25.5 pp (SE 12.1) in a quadratic-plus-log specification, 29.1 pp (SE 24.9) with a quartic; 34.0 pp (SE 15.5) with the threshold at rank 42 and 36.1 pp (SE 25.5) at rank 48. Unadjusted threshold comparisons (paper's Table 1 discussion): girls ranked 45 or lower were 4.3 pp more likely to be enrolled at a JFPR school (p = 0.04), 3.9 pp more likely to be present (p = 0.08), 2.0 pp more likely enrolled at any school (p = 0.29) — a reminder that the *unadjusted* threshold comparison is much smaller than the regression-adjusted effect. Effects largest among girls of lowest socioeconomic status, with less-educated parents, and living farther from a secondary school; the JFPR program "appears to have dramatically reduced socioeconomic gradients" in enrolment.

**Identification strategy.** OLS with application-form controls and school fixed effects; propensity score matching; regression discontinuity around the rank-45 cut-off (cubic in the SES score, school fixed effects), with the rank indicator as an instrument for scholarship receipt; bounding for selective attrition (Smith–Welch-style bounds). The RD relies on the fixed number of scholarships per school generating a discontinuity in the probability of treatment at the 45th rank.

**Threats and external validity.** (i) The sample is applicants, not a random sample of Cambodian girls — application was encouraged by primary schools, so external validity to non-applicants is limited; (ii) the scholarship was not portable, so "any school" effects are diluted; (iii) attendance at an unannounced visit is a point-in-time measure; (iv) the program is girls-only, lower-secondary-only and relatively well funded (US$45 ≈ 15 percent of 2002 GDP per capita of ~US$300), so magnitudes should not be transferred to smaller or differently targeted transfers; (v) the RD is local to the threshold and the standard errors are large; (vi) it measures enrolment/attendance, not learning or child labour.

**How to use in the simulator.** In `web/childlabour.js` the close analogue is the **`cct` lever** (with `cctTarget` for the poorest quintiles and `cctMonitor` for conditionality), not `feeCut`: the JFPR instrument paid cash conditional on enrolment, attendance and passing, which is exactly `cct` paid in the `school` state and half-paid in the `both` state (the `both` discount is `1 − 0.5 × cctMonitor` in the code). What the paper pins:
- **Sign and rough magnitude of the schooling response to a well-targeted conditional transfer for poor girls:** 20–40 pp enrolment gains are plausible when the transfer is large relative to the household's cash budget and the baseline is low.
- **Targeting:** effects are largest for the poorest — supports `cctTarget` below 2 quintiles (the default `cctTarget = 2` is already the poorest 40 percent).
- **Conditionality content:** the transfer's conditions were enforced (enrolment, passing grade, ≤10 absences), so `cctMonitor` close to 1 for scholarship-like interventions is evidence-consistent.
- **The model's price of schooling:** the paper's $45 ≈ 15 percent of GDP per capita means the relevant transfer is large in model units. If `cct` is set to a token amount (e.g. < 0.02 in model units), the simulation will not reproduce the Cambodian effect; the evidence supports `cct` in the range 0.05–0.15 of household income for the poorest quintile in a low-income setting.
- What it cannot pin: learning (`quality`), the girl-specific norm parameter `C.norm` (the paper does not estimate a gender-preference structural parameter, only a girls-only programme), or the wage/return component of `valueSchool`.

## Card 4 — Beegle, Dehejia & Gatti (2006), crop shocks and credit constraints in Tanzania

**Citation.** Beegle, K., Dehejia, R., & Gatti, R. (2006). Child labor and agricultural shocks. *Journal of Development Economics, 81*(1), 80–96. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2005.05.003>. Working-paper version read in full: NBER Working Paper No. 10088 (November 2003), "Child Labor, Crop Shocks, and Credit Constraints"; earlier World Bank Working Paper No. 3075 (May 2003), "Child Labor, Income Shocks, and Access to Credit". <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10088>.

**Setting and data.** Kagera region, Tanzania; four rounds of household panel data (Kagera Health and Development Survey, 1991–1994). Regression sample: 3,839 child observations (ages 10–15) in 636 households; some specifications extend to ages 7–15 (5,591 observations, 716 households). Agriculture is the main economic activity; crop shocks are measured from self-reported crop loss (log value of crop loss; an alternative dummy for crop loss exceeding 10 percent of total crop value). Read at full text (NBER 2003).

**Intervention and comparison.** Transitory negative crop-income shocks (crop loss) versus normal years within the same household; the key interaction is the shock × collateralisable assets (per capita durable goods, as a credit-access proxy). The comparison is therefore not a programme but contrast in exposure to an income shock, with households that have collateral (credit access) versus those without.

**Outcome definition.** Hours worked in the last week by children, including time on economic activities and household chores (the paper notes this broad definition). The shock is transitory by construction (the paper tests persistence and finds lagged shocks do not predict current shocks).

**Effect size (with units).** Verified from the text and tables:
- Continuous shock (log value of crop loss), household fixed effects, ages 10–15: **+0.22 hours/week per log unit (SE 0.08)**, significant at 1 percent (Table 5, column 3); the OLS and community-dummy versions are +0.33 (SE 0.09) and +0.30 (SE 0.09).
- Crop loss of **10 percent or more of total crop value**: **+1.48 hours/week (SE 0.75), about a 7 percent increase** in child labour hours (Table 7, column 1); with a land-size restriction, +2.47 (SE 0.96), about 19 percent; with a collateral dummy, +3.38 (SE 1.06).
- Credit access: children in households with **no durables** have **12 percent higher** labour hours when crop losses occur, compared with **4 percent higher** for children in households with median per-capita durable assets. In the land-restricted sample: +19 percent (no durables) versus +8 percent (mean durables). "A household with a median level of durables is able to offset 50 percent of the shock" (Table 6 interaction).
- Identification caveats in the paper's own words: reverse causation tested (child labour does not predict shocks); community-time trends and fixed effects leave coefficients essentially unchanged; other wealth measures do not show the same interaction.

**Identification strategy.** Household fixed effects (so the shock is relative to the household's own mean), with controls for child age, age squared, parental education, season, district and round; robustness to community-time trends and community-time fixed effects; placebo/alternative shock definitions; tests that shocks are transitory and not predicted by child labour.

**Threats and external validity.** (i) Self-reported crop loss may be correlated with unobserved household conditions, though household FE absorb fixed differences; (ii) collateralisable durables proxy credit access but also wealth and risk preference; (iii) Kagera is a specific agrarian setting with high shock frequency; (iv) hours include chores, so the child-labour definition is broader than the paid-work definitions in cards 2–3; (v) the published JDE version's estimates were not re-read — the numbers above are from the 2003 NBER working paper and may differ slightly from the 2006 article.

**How to use in the simulator.** This is the calibration evidence for the **shock-and-credit block** in `web/childlabour.js`: `C.shockP`, `C.shockSize`, `C.borrow` (the borrowing buffer) and the `credit` lever (which raises the buffer by `0.25 × P.credit`). What it pins:
- **Sign:** a negative income shock raises child hours; credit access dampens the response — supports `credit` sign −1 in `EVIDENCE`.
- **Elasticity order of magnitude:** about a 7 percent increase in child hours for a crop loss of 10 percent or more; the response is concentrated in households without durables. In model terms, the buffer `borrow` should be large enough to offset most of a *small* shock for median households, but not for the no-durables tail. The code's default `borrow = 0.10` (10 percent of income) buffers a shock of `shockSize = 0.45` by at most 10 percentage points of income — a much bigger typical shock than Beegle's 10 percent crop loss. **Calibration warning:** either `shockSize` should be interpreted as a catastrophic tail (harvest failure) rather than the Beegle margin, or `shockP`/`shockSize` should be retuned so the modal shock is of the Beegle scale (a few percent of household income) with a rare large tail. The paper's crop-loss margin maps to a *partial* income shock, not a 45 percent fall.
- **Heterogeneity:** the simulator's quintile structure (`q <= 1` for the poorest) is the right place for the "no durables" response; a run should show child labour rising most in the poorest quintiles after a shock.
- What it cannot pin: the functional form of the borrowing buffer (the model's linear `min(shockSize × y0, (borrow + 0.25 × credit) × y0)` is an assumption), the interest rate, or the return to child work.

## Card 5 — Atkin (2016), export manufacturing and school dropout in Mexico

**Citation.** Atkin, D. (2016). Endogenous skill acquisition and export manufacturing in Mexico. *American Economic Review, 106*(8), 2046–2085. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20120901>. Working-paper version read in full: NBER Working Paper No. 18266 (August 2012, revised July 2015). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w18266>.

**Setting and data.** Mexico, 1986–2000, during major trade reforms. Data combine the 1990 and 2000 Mexican censuses (cohort schooling, attendance, grade attainment by commuting zone) with IMSS municipality-level formal employment data used to construct net new export-manufacturing jobs per worker. The analysis uses 25,289 cohort × commuting-zone observations and 1,808 commuting zones; IV specifications use large firm openings/closings (≥50 employees in a single year) as the instrument for job arrivals (Kleibergen–Paap F = 11,121). Read at full text (NBER 2012 rev. 2015); published AER abstract verified.

**Intervention and comparison.** Local expansions in export-manufacturing employment (net new jobs per worker) arriving when a cohort is around age 16 (the legal age at which factory employment begins in Mexico), compared with cohorts in the same commuting zone less exposed at that age and with other sectors' job growth. The comparison is between cohorts and local labour markets, not between households.

**Outcome definition.** Cohort average completed years of schooling (2000 census); change in the proportion of 16-year-olds attending school (1990–2000); grade-9 dropout rate (share of students obtaining exactly 9 years of schooling among those obtaining 9 or more); pre-grade-9 dropout (share not completing secondary by 2000); Mincerian returns used in validation.

**Effect size (with units).** Verified:
- Main coefficient (Table 2): net new export-manufacturing jobs per worker at age 16 on cohort average years of schooling = **−3.155 (SE 0.638, OLS)**, **−3.192 (SE 0.614, IV)**, **−3.306 (SE 0.690, reduced form)**. One unit is one net new job per worker (i.e. a 100-percentage-point employment increase), so the policy-relevant scale is the shock distribution.
- Concrete magnitude: the 90th percentile of large firm expansions is **0.017 net new export jobs per worker**, which implies **0.06 fewer years of schooling** on average for the exposed cohort.
- Headline interpretation: **"for every twenty-five new export jobs that arrived, one student in the cohort dropped out at grade 9 rather than continuing on to grade 12."**
- Grade-9 dropout: coefficient on exposure at age 16 = **0.374** (significant at 5 percent); a 90th-percentile shock increases grade-9 dropout by **0.64 percentage points**. The largest pre-grade-9 dropout responses occur at exposure ages 11–13, consistent with the earlier school-transition decision.
- Attendance (Table 3): coefficient on net new export jobs per worker = **−0.222 (SE 0.042)**, i.e. local job growth reduced the share of 16-year-olds in school.

**Identification strategy.** Shift-share/cross-cohort design: variation in the timing of factory openings across commuting zones, identified from cohort variation in exposure at age 16 relative to the same zone's other cohorts; controls include state × time dummies, commuting-zone dummies and commuting-zone linear trends; IV uses large firm openings/closings (arguably less endogenous to local schooling trends) to instrument for net job arrivals; standard errors clustered at the commuting-zone level.

**Threats and external validity.** (i) Plant openings are not random: local demand shocks could move both jobs and schooling; the paper's IV and trend controls address this only partly; (ii) migration and informal-sector responses (the paper excludes migrants and Mexico City; it assumes no informal sector/unemployment in its simple model); (iii) the 1986–2000 Mexican export boom is a specific historical episode (maquiladora growth, post-NAFTA), not a generic "industrial policy" effect; (iv) the outcome is years of schooling/grade-9 dropout, not child labour per se (though the mechanism is the opportunity cost of staying in school); (v) compositional effects: the paper notes the interpretation that education may have risen everywhere but less for exposed cohorts; (vi) cohorts born in different years face different school systems.

**How to use in the simulator.** This is the evidence for the **demand-pull block**: `C.teenPull` (the teen-wage multiplier for children 15+) and the `teenBoom` lever (which raises it by `1 + P.teenBoom`). What it pins:
- **Sign:** a rise in local labour demand for less-skilled workers reduces schooling at the school-leaving margin — supports `teenBoom` sign +1 in `EVIDENCE` (the code records it as sign +1 for child work, which is the mirror image).
- **Age margin:** the response is concentrated at age 16, i.e. at the grade 9→10 transition, not in early adolescence. The simulator already restricts `teenPull` to children aged 15+, consistent with this.
- **Rough scale, only through the job→wage mapping:** the paper's natural unit is jobs, not wages, and the simulator's lever is a wage index. Without an auxiliary elasticity of teen wages to local job growth (not estimated in the paper), the 25-jobs-per-dropout result **cannot be converted into a `teenBoom` magnitude**. What can be done: (a) treat `teenPull` as the object that must be large enough that a 10–20 percent teen-wage rise produces a visible rise in `work` at ages 15–17; (b) if a jobs-to-wages conversion is wanted, estimate the wage elasticity from the same census/IMSS data (not done here) and document the step; (c) use the result as a **qualitative structural test**: a run in which a teen-wage boom does not reduce 15–17 schooling is inconsistent with Atkin.
- What it cannot pin: the child-wage level, the young-child (6–14) response (Atkin finds dropout responses at 11–13 for *pre-grade-9* outcomes but the headline mechanism is age 16), or the GE wage loop (`wChild`, `wAdult`).

## Card 6 — Kruger (2007), coffee production and child labour in Brazil

**Citation.** Kruger, D. I. (2007). Coffee production effects on child labor and schooling in rural Brazil. *Journal of Development Economics, 82*(2), 448–463. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2006.04.003>. Earlier working paper: "Coffee Production Effects on Child Labor and Schooling in Rural Brazil" (August 2004), SSRN Working Paper No. 625345. <https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.625345>.

**Setting and data.** Brazilian coffee-producing regions; **seven rounds of household surveys**. The published abstract read via the article's indexed metadata (LMU library record of the ProQuest/Elsevier abstract, PII S030438780600040X) and the 2004 SSRN abstract (read via the Internet Archive's 2025 snapshot of the SSRN page). **The full text was not accessible in this session** (publisher paywall; SSRN blocked by its bot challenge; no open-access copy found through Unpaywall). The survey name, years and sample size therefore could not be verified and are not stated here.

**Intervention and comparison.** Short-term variation in local economic conditions, proxied by **county-level value of coffee production** (which proxies for the value of children's time), across coffee regions and survey rounds. Comparison is across counties/periods with higher versus lower coffee production value, within the coffee regions.

**Outcome definition.** Child labour (work) and schooling (school enrollment/attendance) — as described in the abstract; the precise variable definitions and age ranges are in the full text and were not verified.

**Effect size (with units).** **UNCONFIRMED at the point-estimate level.** What is verified from the abstracts:
- "Increases in the county-level value of coffee production led to more work among middle-income boys and girls, poorer children were withdrawn from school, while richer children were not affected." (published abstract)
- The 2004 SSRN abstract: "Increases in county-level value of coffee production led to more work and less schooling among boys, and lower schooling among girls. Girls without childcare responsibilities were more likely to work. Poorer boys and girls were withdrawn from school, while richer children were more likely to attend. Thus, during periods of economic growth, education of the poor is adversely affected."
No percentage-point estimate, standard error or confidence interval was available in the accessible sources. Do not use a magnitude from this card; if the full text is obtained, extract the main county-production coefficient (and its SE) and record it here.

**Identification strategy.** Variation in coffee production across counties and time is used as a source of short-term changes in the local value of children's time. The paper's exact specification (fixed effects, controls, instrument, treatment of price vs production) is in the full text and was **not verified** in this session. The abstract indicates a short-term/transitory-shock design ("short-term variation").

**Threats and external validity.** (i) Coffee production is endogenous to local conditions (weather, land, migration) that may independently affect schooling — the unverified identification is the central threat; (ii) the effect is heterogeneous by income group (middle-income children work more; poorer children leave school; rich unaffected), which makes a single average effect misleading; (iii) coffee regions of Brazil are a specific commodity-boom setting; (iv) the boom is transitory, so schooling withdrawal may be temporary (the paper is cited elsewhere for the interpretation that transitory booms encourage delaying schooling); (v) no magnitudes were verified, so external validity of size is unknown.

**How to use in the simulator.** Only the **sign and heterogeneity pattern** can be used until the full text is read. In `web/childlabour.js`:
- It supports the `teenBoom`/`wChild` block: a rise in the local value of child time pulls children into work and out of school — consistent with `EVIDENCE` sign +1 for "Teen wage boom" (which cites both Atkin and Kruger).
- It adds a **heterogeneity prior**: the work response is concentrated among middle-income children, while the *schooling* response is concentrated among the poor (and girls without childcare responsibilities are more likely to work). The simulator's `both` state and quintile structure can represent this, but the current `EVIDENCE` entry does not encode it; a calibration note should record that the schooling response should be strongest in the poorest quintiles even if the work response is middle-income.
- It **cannot** pin a magnitude, and it does not identify the general-child-wage elasticity (the coffee boom is a labour-demand shock to a specific crop, not a uniform child-wage change). Treat it as a sign/heterogeneity cross-check, not a calibration target. Revisit if the JDE PDF is obtained.

## Card 7 — Edmonds & Theoharides (2020), the KASAMA productive-asset RCT in the Philippines

**Citation.** Edmonds, E. V., & Theoharides, C. B. (2020). The short term impact of a productive asset transfer in families with child labor: Experimental evidence from the Philippines. *Journal of Development Economics, 146*, 102486. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2020.102486>. Working-paper version read in full: NBER Working Paper No. 26190 (August 2019). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w26190>.

**Setting and data.** The Philippines; the government's KASAMA programme (Kabuhayan Para sa Magulang ng Batang Manggagawa), a key component of its child-labour elimination programme. Clustered randomised trial across **164 communities and 2,296 households** where child labour is prevalent; rollout August 2015–September 2018. The intervention is a **one-time US$518 in-kind productive asset transfer** (beneficiary-selected) plus training/mentoring. The sample includes households in barangays also covered by the 4Ps conditional cash transfer (a separate stratum). Endline observations are children aged 12–17; analysis is intent-to-treat, with standard errors clustered at the barangay (randomisation unit) level and FDR-corrected q-values. Read at full text (NBER 2019).

**Intervention and comparison.** Treatment communities/households receiving the KASAMA asset grant versus control communities; within the sample, heterogeneity by whether the household had a nonfarm enterprise at baseline and by whether the child was in child labour at baseline.

**Outcome definition.** Child time allocation: "economically active in the last 7 days" (any productive activity within the UN System of National Accounts definition, inside or outside the household); "works for pay"; "in child labour in the last 12 months" (any work illegal under Philippine law); "in hazardous child labour in the last 12 months" (hazardous under Philippine law and ILO Convention C182). Household outcomes: reports family firm, family-based economic activity index, per-capita expenditure, food-security index, child well-being and schooling indices.

**Effect size (with units).** Verified from the text and tables:
- *Full sample (Table 5):* no statistically significant effect on any child-labour outcome. ITT estimates on co-resident children: economically active **+2.6 pp (SE 2.0; q = 0.306)**; works for pay **+0.8 pp (SE 1.6)**; in child labour **+1.9 pp (SE 1.8; q = 0.364)**; hazardous child labour **+2.5 pp (SE 2.2; q = 0.334)**. Control means: 81.7 percent economically active, 19.2 percent works for pay, 76.8 percent in child labour, 62.3 percent hazardous.
- *Children not in child labour at baseline (Table 6):* economically active **+8.3 pp (SE 3.9; q = 0.093)**, a 12.5 percent increase over the 66.2 percent control mean; child labour +3.4 pp (SE 3.7; q = 0.614, not significant). Children already in child labour at baseline: no change.
- *Heterogeneity by baseline nonfarm enterprise, children not in child labour at baseline (Table 9):* in households **with** a baseline nonfarm enterprise, child labour **+19.4 pp (SE 7.1; q = 0.026)** and hazardous child labour **+22.6 pp (SE 6.9; q = 0.007)**; with baseline controls, +17.3 pp (SE 7.3; q = 0.064) and +22.2 pp (SE 7.0; q = 0.010). In households **without** a baseline enterprise, effects are essentially zero (child labour −0.5 pp, SE 4.0; hazardous +0.1 pp, SE 4.3). Control means in the enterprise group: 62.7 percent in child labour, 50.7 percent hazardous.
- *Mechanism (Table 3):* treatment raises "reports family firm" by **+9.7 pp (12.7 percent)** and the family-based economic activity index by **+0.19 SD**; food-security index +0.05 SD. The 4Ps stratum shows larger increases in child labour-related outcomes (at least 3 times the full-sample magnitude), consistent with more "available labour" there.

**Identification strategy.** Clustered randomised controlled trial (community-level assignment, barangay-clustered SEs), pre-registered outcomes (AEA registry; Stage 1 pre-analysis plan), ITT with stratification fixed effects and baseline controls; FDR correction for multiple testing; balance tests and attrition checks (3.7 percent of the sample without direct child survey responses, uncorrelated with treatment).

**Threats and external validity.** (i) Short-term (≈1–2 year) follow-up; the paper cannot speak to whether the asset eventually raises incomes enough to reduce child labour; (ii) the intervention is bundled (asset + training/mentoring), so the asset alone is not isolated; (iii) the setting is poor Philippine communities with high baseline child labour and a large existing CCT (4Ps), so the counterfactual includes another transfer; (iv) the subgroup findings by baseline enterprise status involve multiple testing (the paper reports FDR q-values and warns the exploratory heterogeneity analysis was not pre-specified); (v) the child-labour definition is legal/ILO-based, not hours-based, and some "hazardous" exposure is working conditions (noise, chemicals) rather than occupations; (vi) the large positive estimates have wide confidence intervals and come from a subsample.

**How to use in the simulator.** This is the evidence for the **`asset` lever and the `enterprise`/`famRet` block** in `web/childlabour.js`. The code implements exactly the mechanism the paper identifies: an asset raises household income (`assetInc = P.asset × (enterprise || q ≤ 1) × 0.25`) and raises the return to child time in a family enterprise (`famRet = h.enterprise ? (1 + P.asset × 0.6) : 0`), so the model has both a negative income effect (wealthier households need less child work) and a positive substitution effect (the asset needs labour). What it pins:
- **Sign of the enterprise interaction:** the substitution channel dominates for households with a pre-existing enterprise — the simulator's `P.asset × 0.6` multiplier on `famRet` for `enterprise` households is the right object, and the evidence says its effect on child labour (not just economic activity) can be **large and positive** for children not already working.
- **Sign of the pure wealth effect:** for households without an enterprise, the effect is near zero at this horizon — so the simulator should not let `assetInc` alone produce a big fall in child labour. This is a testable calibration check: with `enterprise = 0`, the `asset` lever should move child labour only slightly.
- **Rough magnitude:** in model terms, a US$518 grant to a poor Philippine household (roughly 15–25 percent of annual household expenditure) moved child labour among non-working children in enterprise households by 17–19 pp (and hazardous work by 22 pp). If `asset = 1` is meant to represent a grant of that relative size, then the `famRet` multiplier should be calibrated so that the enterprise-household child labour response is of that order (10–25 pp), not 50 pp.
- **Note on `enterprise` share:** the paper's sample is selected for child labour and has ~61 percent of households with a nonfarm enterprise at baseline; the simulator's `rural_sea` default `enterprise = 0.35` and `hiv_south = 0.25` are for different contexts. For a Philippines-like run, `enterprise` would need to be much higher, and the `asset` effects would be correspondingly larger in aggregate.
- What it cannot pin: the asset-to-income conversion, the time profile (the model has no multi-year asset depreciation), the training/mentoring component, or the general-equilibrium wage response.

## Synthesis: what the seven cards pin, and what remains assumption

### 1. Sign table (all seven papers agree with the simulator's `EVIDENCE` array, with one refinement)

| `web/childlabour.js` lever | Sign in code | Evidence | Refinement the cards add |
|---|---|---|---|
| `cct` (conditional transfer) | −1 | de Hoop & Rosati; Filmer & Schady | Magnitude window: 0 to −10 pp participation for typical CCTs; 20–40 pp schooling gains for a large, well-targeted scholarship |
| `uct` (unconditional transfer) | −1 | de Hoop & Rosati; Edmonds & Schady | WP: −2 to −12 pp on paid work, concentrated among the poorest and pre-transition children; published: −25 pp on economic activities for ages 11–16 |
| `credit` / `borrow` | −1 | Beegle et al. | ~7% hours rise for a ≥10% crop loss; median durables offset 50% of the shock |
| `teenBoom` / `teenPull` | +1 (work) | Atkin; Kruger | 25 jobs → 1 dropout; age-16 margin; Kruger adds heterogeneity, no magnitude |
| `asset` (family enterprise) | 0 in code | Edmonds & Theoharides | Sign should be **+ for enterprise households, ≈0 for non-enterprise**; aggregate sign 0 is defensible but hides the interaction |
| `ban`, `safety`, `ovcSupport`, `feeding`, `flexible`, `bridging` | various | not in these seven papers | Not pinned by this card set (see below) |

### 2. Concrete calibration actions for `web/childlabour.js`

1. **`uct` ≈ 0.07** in model units for a BDH-like unconditional transfer (7 percent of household income), targeted at the poorest quintile (`uctTarget = 1`). Test both margins explicitly: on the broad 10+ paid-work margin expect a low-single-digit pp average effect (WP), while the older-child/economic-activity margin should show a larger response (published −25 pp for 11–16). Do not collapse the two into one target, and do not force a 40–80 percent fall. (Card 2.)
2. **`cct` in the 0.05–0.15 range** of household income for a Cameroon/Cambodia-style large scholarship for the poorest quintile, with `cctMonitor` close to 1 (conditions enforced: enrolment, passing, attendance). Test: schooling gains of 20–40 pp for the poorest girls when baseline enrolment is low. (Card 3.)
3. **`borrow`/`shockSize` consistency check:** the default `shockSize = 0.45` is a catastrophic tail, while Beegle's identified margin is a ~10 percent crop loss raising hours by ~7 percent. Either relabel `shockSize` as a tail event with a separate small-shock process, or retune `shockP`/`shockSize` so the modal shock is small; keep the finding that the response concentrates among households without collateral. (Card 4.)
4. **`asset` block:** keep the family-enterprise interaction (`famRet`), and calibrate the multiplier so enterprise households' child labour rises by ~10–25 pp for a US$518-scale grant; verify that with `enterprise = 0` the asset lever barely moves child labour. Do not use the full-sample "no effect" as evidence of no effect — it averages a positive enterprise response against a null non-enterprise response. (Card 7.)
5. **`teenPull`/`teenBoom`:** do not calibrate the magnitude from Atkin without a separate jobs→wages elasticity; use it as a structural test that a teen-wage boom must reduce 15–17 schooling. Use Kruger only for sign and the middle-income-work/poor-schooling heterogeneity pattern. (Cards 5–6.)
6. **`enterprise` share by context:** the KASAMA setting has ~61 percent of households with a nonfarm enterprise; the `rural_sea` default of 0.35 is a different context. Record the context dependency rather than tuning `enterprise` to the Philippines without checking other moments. (Card 7.)
7. **`cct` vs `feeCut`:** the Cambodian scholarship and the BDH are transfers paid conditional on school, not fee reductions; map them to `cct`/`uct`, not `feeCut`. The model's `cctMonitor`-scaled half-payment for the `both` state is a defensible representation of partial conditionality, but no paper in this set identifies that parameter directly (de Hoop & Rosati note conditionality matters but do not estimate a structural parameter). (Cards 1, 3.)

### 3. What remains assumption, and must not be presented as evidence-based

- **Structural utility parameters:** the Stone–Geary subsistence level (`C.subsist`), the `0.6 × need` threshold, the log curvature `C.tau`, and `C.v0` (the base value of school) are not identified by any of these seven papers. The cards provide signs and reduced-form magnitudes; they do not identify a utility function.
- **The child wage level and the GE wage loop** (`wChild`, `wAdult`, `C.subst`): none of the seven estimates a general-equilibrium child-wage elasticity. Kruger and Atkin are local labour-demand shocks; Beegle is an income shock; none closes the labour market.
- **The ban levers** (`ban`, `banVisible`, `banYears`): the relevant evidence is Bharadwaj, Lakdawala & Li (2020) and Basu & Van (1998), not in this card set; the simulator's known failure to reproduce the partial-ban increase is documented in `TODO.md` §10 and is not fixed by these cards.
- **Schools-as-protection levers** (`safety`, `ovcSupport`, `feeding`, `flexible`, `bridging`): the relevant framing is Nordtveit (2010, 2016), not in this set; the magnitudes in the code are placeholders awaiting Bjørn Nordtveit (as `TODO.md` states).
- **Chores and domestic work:** neither the simulator's four states nor the evidence cards here capture domestic chores as a separate margin, even though de Hoop & Rosati find girls' main response is in chores and Edmonds & Schady find substitution into unpaid household services. This is a known limitation, not a calibration gap.
- **Learning:** none of the seven papers measures learning outcomes; the simulator's `learn` accumulation is an assumption.

### 4. Evidence quality ranking for the simulator's purposes

**Strongest for magnitude:** Filmer & Schady (RD with clear first stage and reported SEs; large, policy-relevant effect); Beegle et al. (panel FE, transparent hours estimates, subgroup by collateral); Edmonds & Theoharides (pre-registered clustered RCT with FDR-corrected results and direct child interviews). **Strong for sign and design, weaker for magnitude:** Edmonds & Schady (randomised, but a small transfer and outcome definitions that differ across versions — work for pay in the WP, broader economic activities in the published paper); Atkin (credible shift-share, but jobs not wages and a specific historical episode). **Sign and heterogeneity only:** de Hoop & Rosati (review, no pooled estimate); Kruger (magnitudes unconfirmed in this session).
