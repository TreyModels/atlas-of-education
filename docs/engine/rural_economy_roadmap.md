You’re asking how education sits inside an economy where households face different costs, firms face different opportunities, and the state changes what both can do. That requires rural production, transport, finance and public services to enter the model alongside schooling and employment.

Start with the household as a producer. A farming household allocates money and family time between crops, paid work, care and school. Agricultural household models study those decisions together when labour, credit or insurance markets are incomplete. Hiring someone for harvest may be impossible, so a child’s time carries a seasonal value that a national wage misses. LaFave and Thomas’s Farms, Families, and Markets belongs in this part of the model. 

For schooling, we need an explicit household cost:

C^{school}_{h,t}
=
fees+tutoring+transport+materials
+opportunity\ cost.

Sanitation enters through who pays for facilities, illness, attendance and time spent collecting water. Some household spending substitutes for missing public provision; tutoring can also purchase an advantage at an admissions gate. Those mechanisms require different equations.

Spikes can emerge from timing and constraints. Fees come due before harvest income arrives. Flooding raises transport costs. An examination year increases tutoring demand. A household may afford schooling over the year while lacking the cash needed this month. We therefore need seasonal cash flows and borrowing limits, with costs recorded when payment occurs.

The wider economy needs these relationships:

Relationship	What we model	What would distinguish countries or regions
Agricultural production	Land, labour, inputs, weather, irrigation and technology	Land distribution, crop mix, seasonal risk
Market access	Farmgate prices, travel costs, commuting and migration	Road quality, reliability, distance to buyers and jobs
Finance	Working capital, investment, debt and insurance	Borrowing limits, collateral rules, interest and repayment timing
Firm investment	Expected sales and returns from adopting technology	Electricity, management, finance, available skills
State provision	Schools, health, infrastructure, transfers and administration	Funding, delivery capacity, access and enforcement

Roads illustrate why we need evidence for each connection. Asher and Novosad found that India’s rural-road programme primarily helped workers move out of agriculture over four years; agricultural outcomes, income and assets changed little, and village-firm employment expanded only slightly. A road can change access to work without generating a large local investment response. 

I take your point about the state to include enforceable rights: land tenure, contracts, identity documents and eligibility for services. These affect investment risk, mobility and access to finance. Their effects depend on implementation. We should represent the relevant rule and its enforcement, then test the resulting behaviour.

For a planner trying to create jobs, the key question becomes: which constraint prevents an additional investment from becoming productive employment? Different answers produce different policies.

Binding constraint	Policy experiment	Outcomes to check
Seasonal household liquidity	Transfers or credit timed around school payments	Attendance, borrowing, farm inputs, child work
Poor access to markets	Roads or transport services	Prices, commuting, migration, firm entry
Unreliable electricity or finance	Infrastructure or investment finance	Technology adoption, production, hiring
Shortage of relevant skills	Training linked to tasks and employers	Productivity, earnings, vacancies filled
Weak demand for firms’ output	Procurement, export access or demand support	Sales, sustained employment, fiscal cost

Every experiment needs a financing rule. Tax-financed construction, foreign aid and domestic borrowing change household resources, imports, credit conditions and future budgets differently. The World Bank’s MAMS framework directly addresses this planning problem by connecting education and infrastructure spending, financing choices, sector production and labour markets. It gives us an existing architecture to examine. 

For the Atlas, I’d make the next economic unit a rural district connected to a town, with households differentiated by land, wealth, location and access to credit. Run the same education intervention under measured differences in roads, public provision and productive opportunities. That would let us investigate how a scholarship, a road or a training programme changes schooling and work, including who captures the gains and who pays.
